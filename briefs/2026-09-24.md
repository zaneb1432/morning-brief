## 1. Tech & AI News

**Anthropic ships Claude Opus 5.5, cuts prices 20–40%, kills the five-hour cap.** On September 22, Anthropic released Claude Opus 5.5, its first model since CEO Dario Amodei publicly called for the industry to slow down. The new flagship costs $4 per million input tokens and $20 per million output tokens — a 20% cut from Opus 5's $5/$25, with cached-token reads falling 60% to $0.20/million. On typical agentic workloads at default settings, Anthropic says the effective cost is down about 40% versus Opus 5, while the model tops internal and third-party benchmarks in agentic coding and computer use. Just as notably, Anthropic is removing the five-hour usage caps for Pro, Max, Team and seat-based Enterprise subscribers in favor of a rolling rate-limit reset — a direct response to power-user complaints and a signal Anthropic is fighting on both price and access as competition with OpenAI intensifies. [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/22/anthropic-releases-opus-5-5-with-lower-prices-and-fable-level-performance/) · [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-opus-5-5)

**OpenAI answers with GPT-6 Sol and Luna, permanently halving API prices.** The same week, OpenAI released two new tiers beneath its flagship GPT-6 Astra: GPT-6 Sol at $2/$10 per million tokens and GPT-6 Luna at just $0.10/$0.50, both with 1.05-million-token context windows. OpenAI confirmed to press that these are permanent list prices, not introductory discounts — an unusually direct shot in the ongoing price war with Anthropic, Google and Chinese labs like DeepSeek and Moonshot, all of which have been undercutting on cost per token this year. The pairing of a cheap high-context model (Luna) with a capable mid-tier model (Sol) suggests OpenAI is optimizing for the high-volume agentic and enterprise-integration workloads that are increasingly where the real revenue is. [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/openai-releases-gpt-6-sol-and-luna-slashing-api-costs-50-or-more) · [The New Stack](https://thenewstack.io/openai-gpt-6-sol-luna-release/)

**Cisco Talos discloses CLOSEDQUORUM, the first reported fully autonomous AI-directed malware.** Talos researchers disclosed a Windows implant that removes the human operator from command-and-control decisions entirely: it gathers host information, submits structured attack options to a panel of up to four commercial LLMs (DeepSeek, Qwen, Mistral and Google Gemini were all configurable targets), and executes whichever action wins a voting process among the models — including LSASS credential dumping, browser credential theft, crypto-wallet extraction, and process-hollowing shellcode injection. There's no confirmed in-the-wild deployment yet, but Talos tied artifacts in the binary to a developer active on carding forums since 2025. Alongside the disclosure, Talos released CAIRN, an open toolkit for hunting AI-embedded artifacts (prompt templates, API keys, provider endpoints) inside malware samples — the clearest sign yet that "AI decides the attack" is moving from hypothetical to observed threat category. [Cisco Talos](https://blog.talosintelligence.com/the-closed-quorum-inside-the-first-reported-autonomous-ai-c2-implant/) · [BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/new-closedquorum-windows-malware-uses-ai-for-attack-decisions/)

**Frontier labs brief the UN Security Council on losing control of AI.** On September 23, France — holding the Council presidency this month — convened a session on "the future of AI and international security" in New York, drawing OpenAI's Sam Altman, Anthropic's Dario Amodei, and Hugging Face's Clement Delangue, alongside invited briefings from China's DeepSeek and Moonshot AI. Yoshua Bengio, co-chair of the UN's Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, also addressed the Council. The session focused on malicious use of frontier models and the risk of losing control over increasingly autonomous systems — notable both for getting rival US and Chinese labs into the same room and for the optics of AI CEOs directly petitioning a body historically focused on nuclear and conventional security threats, even as the Trump administration has dismissed international AI oversight efforts as a "globalist scheme." [UN News](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/09/1168414) · [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/09/23/trump-un-ai-globalist-scheme-altman-amodei-security-council/)

**Anthropic says Claude independently discovered a novel, CRISPR-like enzyme system.** Anthropic's newly formed life-sciences research group had Claude search a database of bacteriophage DNA for uncharacterized protein families, largely on its own initiative after only high-level direction from scientists. Running roughly 950 agents over 21 hours and processing 210 million tokens, Claude gathered over 200,000 reverse-transcriptase sequences, flagged 3,500 candidate systems, and narrowed those to 20 for detailed analysis — work Anthropic says typically takes expert biologists weeks to months. The result, dubbed "array-associated reverse transcriptases" (ART), has properties — programmable cutting, copying and pasting of DNA — previously seen together only in a handful of known systems, CRISPR among them. Gene-editing stocks dipped on the news as investors weighed whether AI-driven discovery could compress biotech's usual multi-year timelines. It's an early but concrete data point for AI-accelerated science actually producing a novel, wet-lab-relevant finding rather than just faster literature review. [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-discovers-novel-enzyme-system) · [Unite.AI](https://www.unite.ai/anthropic-says-claude-discovered-a-new-enzyme-system-resembling-crispr/)

**Google folds its Antigravity coding agent into the Gemini API.** Google shipped `antigravity-preview-09-2026`, a new managed-agent harness that brings the tools and behavior of its Antigravity IDE agent directly into Google AI Studio and the Interactions API, running on Gemini 3.8 Flash. Rather than a single chat completion, a call to the new harness triggers a full autonomous loop — reasoning, code execution, file management and web browsing inside a hosted Linux sandbox — available on both free and paid tiers. It's Google's clearest move yet to commoditize "agent as API call," competing directly with Anthropic's and OpenAI's own agentic tooling and lowering the barrier for developers to embed a capable coding/ops agent without building the scaffolding themselves. [Google AI for Developers](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/antigravity-agent)

## 4. Founder Biography: An Wang

Before Silicon Valley existed as a concept, there was An Wang — a Shanghai-born engineer who invented one of the two or three most important components in the history of computing, sold the patent for what in hindsight was a pittance, and built a company from $600 in savings into one of America's most successful tech firms of the 1970s and '80s, only to watch it collapse within a few years of his death.

Wang arrived in the United States in 1945 to study applied physics at Harvard and joined the university's Computation Laboratory under Howard Aiken, a pioneer of early digital computing. Within his first month on the job, Wang had the core insight that would define his career: that a small ring of magnetizable material could store a bit of data and be read *and immediately rewritten* without losing the information — the foundation of what became magnetic-core memory. It sounds abstract, but it solved one of the central bottlenecks of early computing: fast, reliable, affordable memory. For roughly two decades, into the early 1970s, magnetic core memory powered nearly every computer built anywhere in the world.

When Harvard shut down its computer research program in 1951, Wang struck out on his own, founding Wang Laboratories as effectively a one-man operation making and selling magnetic cores. He negotiated a consulting arrangement with IBM in 1953 that gave the fledgling company a steady thousand-dollar-a-month income, and in 1956 sold his core-memory patent outright to IBM for $400,000 — a sum that, in retrospect, was a fraction of the value IBM extracted from owning the foundational patent for computer memory over the following two decades. It's a pattern familiar to anyone who's watched a founder underprice a breakthrough before its true market existed.

Undeterred, Wang used the capital and credibility to build Wang Laboratories into a major force in desktop calculators and then office computing and word processing, at its peak in the late 1970s and early '80s employing over 30,000 people and generating close to $3 billion in annual revenue — one of the era's genuine tech powerhouses, and one of the first large American companies founded and run by a Chinese immigrant.

The unraveling is as instructive as the rise. In 1986, as Wang stepped back from day-to-day leadership, he insisted on installing his eldest son, Frederick, as president over the objections of the company's existing president, John Cunningham, who resigned in protest. Fred Wang struggled to navigate the shift from proprietary minicomputers to the IBM-PC-compatible world, and by 1987 the company posted a loss of more than $70 million. An Wang eventually removed his own son from the role in 1989, but the damage was structural, not just personnel: Wang Laboratories lost 90% of its market capitalization in four years and filed for bankruptcy in August 1992 — just over two years after An Wang's death in 1990. It remains one of the most cited case studies in business schools on the dangers of founder-led succession decisions made on family loyalty rather than operational fit — a cautionary tale still worth remembering every time a founder starts talking about who inherits the company next.
