## I. Tech & AI News

### Anthropic ships Claude Opus 5 at half the price of its predecessor
Anthropic released Claude Opus 5 across Claude Max, Claude Pro, and the API on July 24, becoming the default model on Max and the strongest available on Pro. The headline is economics as much as capability: Opus 5 approaches the frontier intelligence of Anthropic's top-tier model at roughly half the cost of Opus 4.8, priced at $5 per million input tokens and $25 per million output tokens — unchanged sticker price for a materially stronger model. On Frontier-Bench, Opus 5 scored 43.3% against Opus 4.8's 18.7%, and Anthropic reports a score triple the next-best model on the ARC-AGI 3 reasoning test. The release matters because it resets the price-performance floor for coding and agentic-workflow use cases just as competitors are racing on the same axis.
Source: [VentureBeat — Anthropic launches Claude Opus 5](https://venturebeat.com/orchestration/anthropic-launches-claude-opus-5-a-cheaper-ai-model-for-coding-agents-and-enterprise-workflows)

### OpenAI previews "Astra," a model built to run agents for days at a time, on Capitol Hill
Sam Altman spent July 29–30 in Washington, meeting senators including Raphael Warnock and Bernie Moreno and giving closed-door demos of Astra, OpenAI's next model family. Rather than a one-shot chatbot, Astra is built to coordinate multiple agents over long horizons — planning, running tests, revising work, and pushing through multi-step research or coding tasks with minimal human input. OpenAI is also expected to publish a report on Astra solving ten previously open mathematical problems. The Washington timing is deliberate: the demos landed days after OpenAI disclosed a rogue-agent incident and just ahead of a federal AI-policy deadline, making this as much a lobbying moment as a product preview.
Source: [AI Weekly — OpenAI previews Astra to US senators](https://aiweekly.co/node/8675)

### The EU AI Act's next phase takes effect today, but split-speed amendments soften the blow
August 2 is the AI Act's general application date, and as of today providers of any AI system placed on the EU market must disclose to users that they're interacting with AI, and newly launched systems generating synthetic audio, image, video, or text must carry machine-readable watermarks (systems already on the market get until December 2 to comply). But a late "Digital Omnibus on AI" amendment package has split the calendar: while these transparency rules land on schedule, the heavier high-risk system requirements have been pushed out to December 2027 and August 2028. It's a real compliance deadline for anyone shipping AI in Europe today, but a notably lighter one than originally planned.
Source: [Technology.org — What actually applies on 2 August 2026](https://www.technology.org/2026/07/17/eu-ai-act-what-actually-applies-on-2-august-2026/)

### Munich court rules Suno illegally trained on copyrighted music, handing GEMA a landmark win
The Munich Regional Court ruled on July 31 that AI music generator Suno infringed copyright by processing songs represented by German rights society GEMA — including work by Alphaville ("Forever Young") — without a license, both in training and in the outputs Suno generates. The court ordered Suno to stop the unauthorized reproduction and training, disclose revenue tied to the infringing use, and pay damages still to be determined. Suno says it disagrees and is evaluating an appeal. This is one of the first significant European court rulings to directly address whether AI companies need licenses to train on copyrighted creative work, and it arrives as similar suits play out in the US and UK.
Source: [Music Ally — GEMA wins copyright suit against Suno](https://musically.com/2026/07/31/german-collecting-society-gema-wins-its-copyright-infringement-lawsuit-against-suno/)

### FTC closes comment period on a policy statement targeting state "AI accuracy" mandates
Friday, July 31 was the deadline for public comment on an FTC policy statement addressing how Section 5's ban on deceptive practices applies to AI companies — specifically taking aim at state laws that would require altering the "truthful outputs" of AI models. The statement stems from a December executive order directing the FTC to weigh in on the tension between state-level content rules and model accuracy. With no comprehensive federal AI statute in place, this is one of the clearest signals yet of how the current administration wants to use existing consumer-protection law, rather than new legislation, to shape what AI companies can be compelled to say.
Source: [Federal Trade Commission — FTC seeks comment on AI accuracy policy statement](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/07/ftc-seeks-public-comment-policy-statement-addressing-ai-accuracy)

### Commonwealth Fusion Systems raises another $1B — this time from pension funds, not VCs
CFS announced $1 billion in additional equity financing on July 30, bringing its total raised since 2018 to $4 billion — roughly 30% of all capital ever raised by the fusion industry. What stands out isn't the amount but the source: the checks came from pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, and infrastructure investors rather than the venture and tech-giant money that has funded fusion bets until now. That's a meaningful signal about how conservative, long-horizon capital is starting to price fusion risk, as CFS works toward its Sparc demonstration reactor hitting scientific breakeven in 2027 and finalizes the design of Arc, its first commercial power plant.
Source: [TechCrunch — Commonwealth Fusion Systems raises another $1B](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/30/fusion-power-darling-commonwealth-fusion-systems-raises-another-1b/)

Function has grown to over 500,000 members and 100 million lab tests since its 2023 launch, and has now raised more than $800 million combining equity and this newer style of growth capital that's paid back only as it drives revenue.

## IV. Founder Biography

### An Wang — Founder, Wang Laboratories (1920–1990)

Wang was born in Shanghai in 1920, the oldest of five children, and taught himself English at home before entering school at the third-grade level at age six. He graduated from Shanghai Jiao Tong University with a degree in electrical engineering in 1940, then immigrated to the United States in 1945 to study at Harvard, earning a PhD in physics in 1948 while working on Howard Aiken's Mark IV computer project — one of the first fully electronic computers ever built.

At Harvard, Wang and a classmate invented a pulse-transfer device enabling read-after-write memory operations, a breakthrough that helped make magnetic-core memory practical. In 1955 he sold the patent to IBM and used the proceeds to build his own company rather than cash out and stop. He'd already started it in June 1951, alone, in a room above an electrical fixtures store in Boston — a one-man operation with no product yet and no guarantee IBM's rivals would ever need what he was building.

Wang Laboratories went on to become one of the most successful American technology companies of the 1970s and early '80s, first through desktop calculators and then office word-processing computers that made Wang a household name in corporate America. By the mid-1980s the company employed over 15,000 people across Lowell, Massachusetts, and offices worldwide. Wang handed the presidency to his son Frederick in 1986, then returned himself in 1989 as IBM's dominance of the personal computer market eroded the company's revenue — a reversal he didn't live to see resolved. He died in Boston in March 1990; Wang Laboratories filed for bankruptcy in 1992 and was ultimately acquired by Getronics in 1999. His name is less remembered today than contemporaries like Jobs or Gates, but the memory breakthrough he sold to IBM, and the company he built from that single check, made him one of the first immigrant engineers to go head-to-head with IBM and win market share doing it.
