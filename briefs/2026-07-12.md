## TECH & AI NEWS

*Note: July 11–12 was a relatively quiet stretch for major, independently-verifiable breaking news. **Apple sues OpenAI over alleged theft of hardware trade secrets**
Apple filed a sweeping lawsuit against OpenAI in federal court in Northern California on July 10, alleging that OpenAI stole confidential Apple technology "at every level" — from engineers on OpenAI's technical staff up to hardware chief Tang Tan, a former Apple vice president — to accelerate development of its own consumer AI devices. The complaint centers on OpenAI's roughly 400 hires from Apple, including claims that a former Apple senior systems electrical engineer, Chang Liu, kept an Apple-issued laptop after leaving for OpenAI and used it to download confidential technical documents on unannounced products. The suit also names Jony Ive's hardware startup io Products, which OpenAI acquired for $6.4 billion, as part of the alleged scheme. This is a striking reversal for two companies that were partners just two years ago, when ChatGPT was built into iOS — relations have curdled since OpenAI moved into hardware, putting it in direct competition with Apple's core business. The case could shape how aggressively AI labs are allowed to poach talent and expertise from incumbents building competing devices.
Source: https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/10/apple-sues-openai-over-alleged-trade-secret-theft/

**SK Hynix surges 13% in blockbuster Nasdaq debut, underscoring the AI memory boom**
SK Hynix, the world's second-largest memory chipmaker, closed its first day of trading on the Nasdaq up about 13% on July 10, jumping from its $149 offer price to roughly $168 and raising $26.5 billion — the largest-ever US listing by a foreign company and the second-largest US IPO in history, behind only SpaceX's recent debut. The offering of American depositary receipts was oversubscribed roughly sevenfold, reflecting voracious investor appetite for exposure to the memory chips (HBM in particular) that power AI data centers. SK Hynix's chairman told CNBC that "demand is enormous," pointing to prolonged shortages of high-bandwidth memory as AI labs and cloud providers race to build out compute capacity. The listing gives US investors direct access to a company that, alongside Samsung and Micron, effectively controls the supply of memory chips underpinning the current AI buildout — making it a bellwether for how long the AI infrastructure spending cycle can run before memory supply constraints bite.
Source: https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/10/sk-hynix-skhy-stock-nasdaq.html

**OpenAI ships GPT-5.6 family and launches ChatGPT Work, an autonomous "finished work" agent**
OpenAI completed the broad public rollout of its GPT-5.6 model family — flagship Sol, mid-tier Terra, and budget Luna — on July 9, after the US Commerce Department's AI Standards and Innovation office cleared wider access following safety concerns about misuse in cyberattacks, biology, and coding. Alongside the models, OpenAI launched ChatGPT Work, an agent that takes a stated goal, pulls context from connected apps like Slack and Microsoft Teams, and independently produces finished spreadsheets, slide decks, documents, and web apps over hours-long sessions rather than just chatting. Work is available to all paid desktop plans immediately and is expanding to web and mobile for Plus, Business, Enterprise, and Edu users over the following days; OpenAI is simultaneously folding its Codex coding agent into a unified desktop app and sunsetting its Atlas browser by August 9. Pricing for the new models ranges from $1 to $5 per million input tokens depending on tier, undercutting GPT-5.5 while improving capability. The launch intensifies the fight for enterprise "agentic" workflows against Microsoft Copilot, Google's Gemini, and Anthropic's Claude.
Source: https://openai.com/index/chatgpt-for-your-most-ambitious-work/

**xAI releases Grok 4.5, an "Opus-class" coding and agentic model**
xAI launched Grok 4.5 on July 9, its first model purpose-built for coding and long-horizon agentic work, trained jointly with Cursor on top of a 1.5-trillion-parameter foundation model. Elon Musk described it as "an Opus-class model, but faster, more token-efficient and lower cost," and the model took the top spot on SWE-bench-style long-horizon coding benchmarks, running hundreds of tool calls with minimal human intervention. It's priced aggressively at $2 per million input tokens and $6 per million output tokens, and is live today in Grok Build, in Cursor across all plans, and via the xAI API console (EU availability is pending regulatory review). One notable caveat that emerged in independent testing: Grok 4.5's hallucination rate reportedly jumped sharply versus its predecessor even as raw accuracy improved, a reminder that capability gains in this generation of models are coming with real reliability trade-offs. The release keeps xAI in the frontier-model conversation alongside OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google heading into the second half of 2026.
Source: https://x.ai/news/grok-4-5

**Federal Reserve taps Marc Andreessen to co-lead new task force on AI's economic impact**
Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh announced on July 9 the leadership of five new task forces meant to overhaul how the central bank conducts monetary policy, and tapped venture capitalist Marc Andreessen, Stanford economist Charles I. Jones (currently on leave at Anthropic), and Microsoft Xbox CEO Asha Sharma to co-lead the "Productivity and Jobs" group. That task force is explicitly charged with assessing how AI and other general-purpose technologies are affecting economic output and employment, feeding findings directly into the Federal Open Market Committee's interest-rate decisions. It's a notable move: a sitting Fed chair is formally inviting one of Silicon Valley's most prominent AI boosters and investors into the process of shaping how the central bank thinks about AI-driven productivity and labor-market disruption. The panel is expected to deliver concrete recommendations by the end of 2026, making this one of the clearest signals yet that AI's macroeconomic effects are now a first-order concern for US monetary policymakers, not just tech-industry commentary.
Source: https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20260709a.htm

**SambaNova raises $1B at an $11B valuation, lands JPMorgan Chase as an inference partner**
Palo Alto AI chip maker SambaNova Systems announced the first close of a $1 billion Series F round led by General Atlantic, with participation from Seligman Ventures, T. Alongside the raise, the company landed a major commercial win: JPMorgan Chase selected SambaNova as an inference-infrastructure partner, with its SN40L and SN50 systems set to power secure, on-premises AI inference inside the bank. The deal is notable both as a funding signal — investors are still willing to write billion-dollar checks for Nvidia challengers — and as validation that a non-Nvidia chip startup can win a marquee enterprise customer for sensitive, on-prem workloads.

**Lyzr raises $100M Series B — using its own AI agent to run the fundraise**
Jersey City-based Lyzr, a three-year-old startup that helps enterprises build AI agents, closed a $100 million Series B at roughly a $500 million valuation. What made the round unusual: Lyzr had its own AI agent, nicknamed SivaClaw, actually run the fundraising process — fielding questions from more than 130 investors, drafting investment memos, and tracking which slides backers lingered on. The approach drew $400 million in investor interest from Silicon Valley, the Middle East, and financial-sector backers without founders doing the traditional Sand Hill Road courtship circuit. It matters because it doubled as a live product demo: rather than just pitching what its agents can do, Lyzr let one do real, high-stakes deal work in front of the same investors deciding whether to fund it.
Source: https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-09/a-startup-that-builds-ai-agents-used-one-to-raise-100-million

**Ollama raises $65M Series B as open-model usage nearly doubles**
Ollama, the 14-person team behind the widely used tool for running open-weight AI models locally, raised a $65 million Series B led by Theory Ventures, with Benchmark, 8VC, Y Combinator, Pace Capital, 49 Palms, and GTMFund participating. The company, previously backed by a $15 million Series A led by Benchmark's Peter Fenton, has now raised $88 million total; a specific new valuation wasn't disclosed. Ollama says it has grown to 8.9 million monthly active developers and is embedded in 85% of Fortune 500 companies, with usage roughly doubling since January and about a million new installs added weekly. Theory Ventures' Tomasz Tunguz framed the bet as a platform-layer play: as open models close the capability gap with closed ones for most real-world tasks, whoever owns the layer where those models actually run captures outsized value — the same dynamic that made prior computing-platform bets so lucrative.
Source: https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/09/popular-open-source-ai-developer-tool-ollama-raises-65m-grows-to-nearly-9m-users/

**Databento raises $97M Series B to take on Bloomberg's terminal in market data**
Salt Lake City-based Databento, a market-data platform offering real-time and historical data across futures, options, and equities via a unified API, closed a $97 million Series B led by New Enterprise Associates (NEA), with DRW Venture Capital, Redpoint Ventures, and Tribe Capital also participating. The round was heavily oversubscribed, drawing over $300 million in investor demand. Three years after launch, the company says it reached profitability with just 24 employees, more than doubled its active API users in recent months, grew revenue 6.65x year-over-year, and has maintained over 97% enterprise logo retention since inception — unusually strong efficiency metrics for a fintech infrastructure company at this stage. The capital will fund expansion into new asset classes and geographies, including growing to more than 20 data centers worldwide, as Databento positions itself as a modern, API-first alternative to incumbents like Bloomberg's terminal business. NEA partners Rick Yang and Danielle Lay are joining the board as director and observer, respectively.
Source: https://siliconangle.com/2026/07/09/market-data-platform-startup-databento-closes-97m-round-drawing-300m-investor-demand/

**U.S. VCs deployed a record $412.7 billion in H1 2026 — and almost none of it reached the broader startup market**
Fortune's July 10 analysis of the venture capital market found that U.S. VCs put $412.7 billion to work in the first half of 2026, roughly 30% more than the whole of 2025, breaking the previous full-year record with two quarters to spare. But the piece's real thesis is bifurcation, not abundance: AI deals accounted for 86% of that capital, and 91% of dollars went into rounds of $100 million or larger, meaning the "record" is really a story about a handful of frontier labs and infrastructure players absorbing the market. Companies like OpenAI and Anthropic alone are estimated to have soaked up over 40% of all global startup funding raised in the same period. For everyone outside that top tier — seed and early-stage founders, non-AI sectors, funds without mega-round access — deal counts and check sizes are flat or shrinking even as the headline numbers hit all-time highs. This matters for how the industry reads 2026: venture "growth" this year is a concentration story, not a broad-based boom, with implications for how LPs evaluate manager performance and how founders outside AI infrastructure should set fundraising expectations.

## FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY

**Sara Blakely**

In 1998, Sara Blakely was a 27-year-old door-to-door fax machine saleswoman in Florida, unable to get into law school after twice failing the LSAT. She had no background in fashion, retail, or manufacturing. What she had was a specific, nagging problem: she wanted to wear white pants to a party but couldn't find hosiery that wouldn't show visible lines under the fabric. Her solution was almost absurdly simple — she cut the feet off a pair of control-top pantyhose and wore them under her pants. It worked. That kitchen-table hack became Spanx.

Blakely spent two years developing the idea nights and weekends while keeping her day job selling fax machines, funding everything with $5,000 of her own savings. She wrote her own patent application using a textbook from the library to save on legal fees, and she personally called hosiery mills across North Carolina to find someone willing to manufacture her prototype. Most turned her down outright. One mill owner initially passed, then called back after his daughters tried the product on and convinced him it was worth making. Deliberately, she told almost no one — not even her family — what she was working on for a full year, later saying that if she'd absorbed the skepticism of others too early, she'd probably still be selling fax machines.

The real turning point came when she cold-called the buying office at Neiman Marcus and talked her way into ten minutes with a buyer. The pitch wasn't landing verbally, so Blakely took the buyer into a bathroom, changed into her prototype, and walked out in white pants to show the difference in person. The buyer's reaction — "Whoa" — got Spanx into seven Neiman Marcus stores, the company's first retail placement. The bigger break came in November 2000, when Blakely sent a gift basket with a handwritten explanation of her product to Oprah Winfrey's show; Oprah named Spanx one of her "Favorite Things" that year, and the company went from a scrappy startup to a national name almost overnight.

What makes Blakely's story particularly instructive is what she refused to do as much as what she did. She never took a single outside investor or loan in the company's more than two-decade run, retaining full ownership and control while building it into a business valued north of a billion dollars. When she finally sold a majority stake to the private equity firm Blackstone in 2021, the deal valued Spanx at roughly $1.2 billion — a return of more than 200,000 times her original $5,000 stake, achieved without ever giving up equity to raise capital. She also credits deliberately shielding the idea from early criticism, and reframing her father's dinner-table question growing up — "What did you fail at today?" — as the mindset that let her keep iterating through hundreds of rejections from manufacturers, retailers, and patent attorneys before anyone said yes.

Her story is a useful counterweight to the venture-backed, scale-fast mythology that dominates most founder narratives: a bootstrapped consumer product, built on a personal frustration, sold through sheer persistence and one bathroom-stall demo, with no funding round in sight.
