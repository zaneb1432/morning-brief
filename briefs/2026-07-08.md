## TECH & AI NEWS

**Illinois Enacts the Nation's Toughest AI Safety Law**
Governor JB Pritzker signed the Artificial Intelligence Safety Measures Act (SB 315) on July 6, making Illinois the third state — after California and New York — to impose binding safety obligations on frontier AI developers rather than relying on voluntary industry commitments. The law targets only the largest AI systems (those built by developers generating over $500 million in annual revenue using massive compute) and requires them to publish a framework for identifying "catastrophic risk," defined as a credible chance of causing death or serious injury to 50+ people or over $1 million in property damage. Most notably, it mandates annual independent third-party safety audits with no financial conflicts of interest — the first state law to require this — plus 72-hour (or 24-hour for imminent danger) incident reporting to the state, and whistleblower protections for employees who flag safety concerns. Because Illinois, California, and New York together represent roughly 40% of the U.S. AI market, this trio is functionally creating a de facto national compliance standard in the continued absence of federal AI legislation. That matters most for labs like OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind, who will now need to build audit and disclosure infrastructure regardless of what Washington does.
Source: https://www.nbcchicago.com/news/local/illinois-new-ai-safety-law-is-the-nations-strongest-advocate-says/3958692/

**Chinese Open-Source Models Are Eating Into OpenAI and Anthropic's Enterprise Business**
A CNBC analysis published July 7 found that 30–46% of enterprise AI token usage routed through OpenRouter is now going to Chinese models like DeepSeek and Zhipu's GLM 5.2, up from just 11% on average over the prior year and only 4.5% in early 2025. The draw is overwhelmingly price: OpenRouter data cited in the piece shows Chinese open-source models running 60–90% cheaper than comparable frontier releases from Anthropic and OpenAI, with GLM 5.2 landing within a percentage point of Anthropic's Opus 4.8 on a widely watched agentic benchmark at roughly one-fifth the cost. The story includes a concrete case study: AI startup Lindy moved 100% of its production traffic off Claude and onto DeepSeek in June, with CEO Flo Crivello describing watching the cost curve "crash to the ground." This is a meaningful data point in the broader efficiency-driven shift reshaping enterprise AI budgets, and it lands right as OpenAI and Anthropic are reportedly raising per-token prices — creating real margin and competitive pressure on both companies heading into anticipated IPOs.
Source: https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/07/chinese-ai-models-costs-us-openai-anthropic.html

**DeepSeek Is Quietly Building Its Own AI Inference Chip**
Reuters reported July 7, citing three sources familiar with the effort, that DeepSeek has spent roughly a year working on a custom inference-focused chip intended to reduce its dependence on both Nvidia and domestic alternative Huawei. The project is still early — discussions with manufacturing and memory partners and quiet hiring of chip-design engineers, but no named foundry, prototype, or benchmarks yet. The strategic logic is notable: DeepSeek is deliberately targeting inference rather than training silicon, and with roughly 70% of global AI compute demand now going to inference workloads rather than training, that's the segment where Chinese chip design is already closest to being competitive and where a purpose-built chip pays off fastest given serving-cost sensitivity. Combined with the CNBC story above about Chinese models winning U.S. enterprise share on price, this signals China's AI ecosystem is moving up the stack from just competitive models to vertically-integrated, cost-optimized infrastructure — a longer-term threat to Nvidia's inference-market dominance if it succeeds.
Source: https://www.engadget.com/2209378/deepseek-reportedly-developing-ai-chips/

**xAI Formally Dissolves Into SpaceX, Rebrands as SpaceXAI**
Elon Musk's xAI officially completed its rebrand to SpaceXAI on July 6, unveiling a new logo and X handle and folding Grok and its other AI products fully under the SpaceX umbrella. This formalizes a plan Musk announced in May, following SpaceX's acquisition of xAI (along with Grok and X) back in February. It also raises governance and antitrust-adjacent questions worth watching, given how much of Musk's empire (X, Tesla, SpaceX, Starlink) is now intertwined through a single AI product line, and it changes the competitive framing for Grok, which now competes as an arm of a space and infrastructure giant rather than a standalone AI lab.

**First Fully Autonomous AI-Driven Ransomware Attack Confirmed**
Sysdig's Threat Research Team published a report (covered widely on July 6, including by TechCrunch) documenting "JADEPUFFER," which researchers describe as the first ransomware campaign where an AI agent — not a human operator — handled the entire technical kill chain: reconnaissance, credential theft, lateral movement, privilege escalation, and ransomware deployment. The agent exploited a known Langflow remote-code-execution flaw (CVE-2025-3248) to harvest cloud and LLM-provider credentials, pivoted into a production MySQL/Nacos server via a 2021 authentication bypass, and encrypted 1,342 configuration items — notably losing its own ransom key, making the attack unrecoverable even if a victim paid. Researchers point to over 600 payloads containing plain-language comments explaining the agent's own reasoning, and one instance where the agent diagnosed and fixed a failed login in 31 seconds, as evidence of genuine autonomous operation rather than scripted automation. The important caveat is that a human still initiated and oversaw the campaign; the "autonomy" refers to hands-off technical execution, not a rogue AI acting alone. Regardless, this is the clearest public evidence yet that agentic AI has crossed into offensive cyber operations, and it's likely to accelerate enterprise urgency around agent-permission controls and AI-specific incident response planning.
Source: https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/06/the-first-ai-run-ransomware-attack-still-needed-a-human/

**UN Holds First-Ever Global Dialogue on AI Governance Amid "Catastrophic Harm" Warnings**
The UN's first Global Dialogue on AI Governance, paired with the AI for Good Summit, convened in Geneva on July 6–7, bringing together nearly all UN member states to discuss coordinated AI oversight for the first time. The UN's Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, co-chaired by Yoshua Bengio and Maria Ressa, warned that AI "could cause catastrophic harm, either on its own or due to malicious users," and that the technology is "outpacing both scientific understanding and governments' ability to adapt" — a notably starker framing than prior UN AI statements. Secretary-General António Guterres pushed for the dialogue to "give the world direction," invoking both AI's potential to compress decades of development into years and risks extending to autonomous weapons. The practical limitation is real: the Dialogue produces only a non-binding co-chair summary, not enforceable rules, so its significance is more diplomatic than regulatory — but it matters as a signal that global AI governance is shifting toward an explicit attempt at multilateral coordination, right as national laws like Illinois's (above) are moving in the opposite, more fragmented direction.
Source: https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/07/1167862

## STARTUP WORLD

**Norm AI raises $120 million Series C, hits $1.2 billion unicorn valuation for AI-native law firm**
Norm AI, the roughly three-year-old startup building an "AI-native law firm" called Norm Law, announced a $120 million Series C led by Khosla Ventures on July 7, pushing its valuation to $1.2 billion and total funding past $260 million. The round drew a notably blue-chip investor list — Blackstone, Bain Capital Ventures, Coatue, Craft Ventures, Vanguard, TIAA, and New York Life, plus individuals including former Blackstone COO Tony James and former Kirkland & Ellis chairman Jeff Hammes. Norm's model is unusual: its AI agents do legal work supervised by human attorneys, and it charges clients based on outcomes rather than billable hours, directly challenging the industry's traditional pricing structure. Its client base reportedly represents more than $30 trillion in combined assets under management, mostly regulated enterprises that need governance around how AI operates in high-stakes decisions. This is one of the clearest signals yet that agentic AI is moving from hype into a real, fundable service business inside a famously conservative industry.

Munich-based Proxima Fusion announced a €411 million Series A on July 7, led by XTX Ventures and East X Ventures, valuing the company at €2.4 billion (~$2.7B). The capital will fund Proxima's push toward a demonstration plant and cements Europe's push to build a homegrown fusion champion to compete with well-funded U.S. players like Commonwealth Fusion Systems.

**Quaise Energy raises $134 million to drill for superhot geothermal power using MIT-born microwave tech**
Quaise Energy, an MIT spinout, announced the first close of a $134 million Series B on July 7, led by climate-focused Prelude Ventures with strategic backing from two major Japanese energy players, JERA and Idemitsu. The deal matters because it's one of the largest bets yet on next-gen geothermal as a scalable, always-on complement to solar and wind, and the involvement of two Japanese utilities signals real appetite for the tech outside the U.S.

**Together AI raises $800 million Series C at $8.3 billion valuation to scale open-source AI infrastructure**
Together AI, a "neocloud" that rents Nvidia GPU capacity and lets enterprises train and run open-source models like DeepSeek, Nemotron, and Kimi, announced an $800 million Series C on July 1, roughly quadrupling its valuation to $8.3 billion in just sixteen months. The round was led by Aramco Ventures, with Vista Equity Partners, General Catalyst, Emergence Capital, Nvidia, March Capital, Pegatron, and SentinelOne's S Ventures also participating. Together AI says annual bookings crossed $1.15 billion last quarter as open-source model usage roughly tripled industry-wide over the past year, and the company has separately lined up commitments for more than 500 megawatts of compute capacity to be built out by investors. CEO Vipul Ved Prakash tied the raise to a bigger structural bet: "History shows that the biggest technology shifts are won by open ecosystems that make innovation cheaper, faster and available to everyone." For VCs, the round is notable as a rare mega-deal for an AI-infrastructure "pick-and-shovel" play rather than a frontier model lab, and as validation of the thesis that open-weight models are becoming a durable, monetizable alternative to closed systems like OpenAI's and Anthropic's.

## FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: Chuck Feeney

In 1988, Forbes put Chuck Feeney on its list of the four hundred richest Americans, pegging his fortune at $1.3 billion. It was a mistake. Feeney had already given nearly all of it away, secretly, to a foundation almost no one knew existed — not Forbes's researchers, not his own business partners, not even most of his family. For a man later dubbed "the James Bond of philanthropy," it was fitting misdirection: the world's ledger said billionaire; his own said otherwise.

Charles Francis Feeney was born in 1931 in Elizabeth, New Jersey, to an insurance underwriter and a nurse in a working-class Irish-American family. He was the first in his family to attend college, and got there only via the GI Bill, earned as a U.S. Air Force radio operator in the Korean War. At Cornell's School of Hotel Administration, which he entered in 1952, his entrepreneurial streak showed early: he ran a sandwich business out of his dorm profitable enough to earn him the nickname "the sandwich man." Decades later, Cornell's president would call him the university's "third founder," ranking him alongside Ezra Cornell himself — a title earned through nearly a billion dollars in quiet donations rather than sandwiches.

The fortune traces to an idea obvious only in hindsight. After Cornell, Feeney and classmate Robert Warren Miller began selling duty-free liquor to American servicemen across Asia and the Mediterranean in the 1950s. Sailors and airmen had money, port access, and no easy way to buy tax-free goods; the two became middlemen, then retailers, then an empire. They formalized the business as Duty Free Shoppers Group in 1960, and a breakthrough came when DFS secured the duty-free concession in Hawaii just as Japanese outbound tourism took off. Feeney and Miller built DFS into the world's largest travel retailer, selling Chanel, Gucci, and Cartier to Japanese travelers the moment they stepped off a plane.

The real turning point wasn't a business deal — it was a decision to disappear. In 1984, at the height of his wealth, Feeney secretly transferred his entire 38.75 percent stake in DFS, worth roughly $500 million, into a new entity called The Atlantic Philanthropies, quietly reducing his own net worth to under $5 million while running the company as though nothing had changed. He kept the transfer hidden even from Miller. For thirteen years Atlantic gave anonymously, until a legal dispute over DFS's eventual sale to LVMH threatened to expose everything in court. Rather than let the truth leak out sideways, Feeney called The New York Times himself; its 1997 story, "He Gave Away $600 Million and No One Knew," blew his cover for good.

What made Feeney more than merely generous was the philosophy behind the giving. He rejected the model of dying rich and leaving a foundation to dole out money in perpetuity, long after the donor's judgment could guide it. Instead he championed "giving while living" — spending the fortune down, deliberately, while still around to see results and correct course. Atlantic Philanthropies was structured as a limited-life foundation, committed to giving away virtually everything and then closing. It made its final grant commitments in 2016, fittingly to Cornell, and formally shut its doors in a 2020 ceremony, having distributed more than $8 billion to public health, human rights, aging research, and peace efforts in Northern Ireland and South Africa. Bill Gates, whose Giving Pledge was reportedly influenced by Feeney's example, sent a video tribute.

Feeney lived the philosophy as much as he preached it: he flew economy into his eighties, wore a $10 Casio watch, carried belongings in a plastic bag instead of a briefcase, and by the end owned no house, no car, no bank account of his own — everything ran through the foundation. He died in October 2023 at 92, having achieved, by design, the rare distinction of dying essentially broke by choice.

His lasting significance lies less in duty-free shopping than in the model he left for what to do with money afterward. "Giving while living" is now a recognized term in philanthropy precisely because Feeney proved it could work on a deadline, with accountability, rather than banking a fortune for a foundation to manage long after its donor is gone. In an era of billionaires who pledge to give away wealth "eventually," Feeney's distinction is having actually finished the job — in full view of no one, until he had no choice.
