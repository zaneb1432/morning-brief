## 1. TECH & AI NEWS

**OpenAI previews GPT-5.6 Sol, Terra, and Luna — but almost nobody can use them yet.** OpenAI began a limited preview of its next model family: Sol (the flagship, aimed at the hardest coding and security-research problems, and the new state of the art on Terminal-Bench 2.1), Terra (a mid-tier model matching GPT-5.5 at roughly half the cost, built for high-volume business tasks), and Luna (the fastest, cheapest tier, for summarization and routine automation). What makes this launch unusual is the access model: per VentureBeat, OpenAI shared the models and its release plans with the U.S. government first, and the preview is restricted to around 20 partner organizations while a broader rollout is promised "in the coming weeks." OpenAI also confirmed Sol will run on Cerebras hardware at up to 750 tokens per second, a speed benchmark none of the incumbent inference stacks currently match. The episode is a preview of how frontier releases may increasingly work: government coordination before general availability, not after. [OpenAI: Previewing GPT-5.6 Sol](https://openai.com/index/previewing-gpt-5-6-sol/) · [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/openai-unveils-gpt-5-6-sol-terra-and-luna-models-but-only-accessible-to-limited-preview-partners-for-now-per-us-gov)

**Anthropic launches Claude Science and starts running its own drug-discovery programs.** On June 30, Anthropic introduced Claude Science, a research workbench wired into 60+ scientific tools and databases (PubMed, Jupyter, single-cell RNA-seq pipelines, CRISPR design) aimed at biopharma researchers. More notably, Anthropic isn't just selling picks and shovels — it's opened internal drug-discovery programs targeting neglected and rare diseases, explicitly to generate the hands-on lab experience that should make its own AI tools better (and more credible to paying biopharma customers like Novo Nordisk and the Allen Institute, both early users). The move follows Anthropic's ~$400M acquisition of Coefficient Bio in April, which supplied the life-sciences expertise the launch needed. It's the clearest sign yet that the frontier labs see life sciences, not just chat and coding assistants, as a primary battleground — MIT Tech Review notes Anthropic now joins Google and OpenAI in a three-way AI drug-discovery race. [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/06/30/1139987/claude-science-is-anthropics-newest-flagship-product/) · [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/06/30/anthropic-launches-ai-drug-discovery-program-claude-science.html)

**The UN's first-ever Global Dialogue on AI Governance opens today in Geneva.** All 193 UN member states, plus industry, civil society, and academia, convene at Palexpo for a two-day session (July 6–7) — the first meeting of a new standing forum modeled on the Internet Governance Forum. It's deliberately non-binding: the output is a co-chairs' summary, not treaty language, and a second session is already scheduled for New York in May 2027. The timing is notable — it runs alongside the WSIS Forum and ITU's AI for Good Summit, meaning Geneva this week is effectively the closest thing the world has to an "AI governance summit season." Given how fractured national AI policy has become (export controls, model-safety mandates, compute rules), this is worth watching less for concrete outcomes and more as a signal of which blocs are aligning on what. [UN News](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/07/1167862) · [Global Dialogue on AI Governance](https://www.un.org/global-dialogue-ai-governance/en)

**Anthropic restores Claude Fable 5 globally after an 18-day export-control suspension.** Fable 5 (and the related Mythos 5 model) went dark on June 12 after Amazon researchers found a jailbreak that could coax the model into producing exploit code for a real vulnerability — serious enough that the U.S. government stepped in with an export directive. Anthropic says a new AI-powered safety classifier now catches the reported jailbreak pattern in over 99% of test cases, rerouting flagged prompts to Claude Opus 4.8 instead. Access resumed July 1 across Claude.ai, the API, Claude Code, and Claude Cowork, with Pro/Max/Team/Enterprise users getting Fable 5 for up to half their weekly usage through July 7. The episode is a useful data point on how fast a frontier-model safety incident can escalate into a government-mandated shutdown — and how fast a lab can ship a fix to get it lifted. [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/redeploying-fable-5) · [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/06/30/anthropic-says-trump-admin-has-lifted-export-controls-on-claude-fable-5-and-mythos-5.html)

The genuinely interesting detail: despite months of leadership pushing adoption of Tesla's own Grok, four sources say most staff prefer Anthropic's Claude. Tesla joins Uber (which capped spending at $1,500/month after blowing its 2026 AI budget by April), Meta, Amazon, and Walmart in reining in runaway internal AI costs — a quiet reversal of the "let everyone use whatever they want" phase of enterprise AI adoption. [The Information](https://www.theinformation.com/articles/tesla-caps-employee-ai-spend-200-per-week-adoption-push) · [Electrek](https://electrek.co/2026/07/02/tesla-caps-employee-ai-spending-200-week/)

This isn't a "one bad month" story so much as a trendline worth tracking: it's the clearest macro data point yet that AI-driven headcount reduction is showing up in the topline numbers, not just anecdotes. ## 4. FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: Gary Kildall

Gary Kildall (1942–1994) is the founder most people have never heard of who arguably should have been Bill Gates. A computer scientist teaching at the Naval Postgraduate School in Monterey, Kildall began consulting for Intel in 1972, at a time when the microprocessor was still a novelty in search of a purpose. By 1974 he'd written CP/M — Control Program/Monitor — the first real disk operating system for microcomputers, along with the BIOS layer that let a machine's software talk to its disk drives regardless of the specific hardware underneath. It was a genuine invention, not an incremental improvement: before CP/M, every machine needed its software written for its exact hardware configuration. Kildall and his wife Dorothy McEwen turned it into a company, Digital Research (originally, memorably, "Intergalactic Digital Research"), and by 1981 CP/M ran on some 3,000 different computer models, with Digital Research pulling in $5.4M a year — a serious business for the era.

Then came IBM. In 1980, IBM needed an operating system for its forthcoming Personal Computer and approached Digital Research first, wanting a version of CP/M for Intel's new 8086 chip. What happened next has been mythologized for decades — the popular version has Kildall literally flying his private plane instead of meeting IBM's negotiators, a story Kildall himself always denied and that historians now treat as largely apocryphal. The more mundane and better-documented truth is that Digital Research balked at IBM's terms, particularly a non-disclosure agreement IBM wanted before it would even describe the deal — deeply unusual conditions for a small software company used to a more open, faster-moving culture selling to hobbyists and hardware makers, not to the most powerful company in computing. IBM, needing an OS on a deadline, turned instead to a young company called Microsoft, which didn't have an operating system either — but which bought one (86-DOS, sometimes called "QDOS") from a small Seattle firm, adapted it, and licensed it to IBM as PC-DOS. Crucially, Microsoft structured the deal so it could also license the same OS to every IBM clone-maker that followed, while Digital Research's later CP/M-86 shipped months afterward at six times the price and never found comparable distribution or software support.

That one negotiation is arguably the hinge point on which the entire PC software industry's center of gravity swung from Digital Research to Microsoft. Kildall kept innovating for years afterward — Digital Research shipped GEM, an early graphical interface, and DR-DOS, a legitimate MS-DOS competitor that Microsoft was later found to have taken deliberate technical and marketing steps to undermine — but he never recaptured the position CP/M once held. Digital Research was sold to Novell in 1991 for $80M, a fraction of what a category-defining OS company could have been worth had the IBM deal gone differently. Kildall died in 1994 at 52, following a fall, largely out of the spotlight he'd once been positioned to dominate. He's remembered today less as a cautionary tale about any personal failing than as a case study in how much of technology history turns on a single contract negotiation — invention alone was never going to be enough against a rival willing to move faster and cut a more aggressive licensing deal.
