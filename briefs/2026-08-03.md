## TECH & AI NEWS

**OpenAI's Astra model solves ten decade-old open math problems, with machine-checked proofs.** On August 2, OpenAI said an internal version of its next major model, Astra, produced new results on ten problems in mathematics and theoretical computer science that had been open for at least a decade — including an explicit construction of a non-sofic group, a question left unresolved since Mikhail Gromov introduced the concept of soficity in 1999, and a disproof of Connes's rigidity conjecture. The company published a 249-page manuscript collection alongside Lean 4 formal proof certificates for all ten results (posted on GitHub under an Apache 2.0 license, with zero unproven "sorry" steps), and says the compute cost to find the solutions was roughly $2,000. Human mathematicians turned the AI's arguments into readable manuscripts, but the underlying reasoning and formalization were the model's own work. This matters because machine-checkable proofs sidestep the usual worry about AI-generated math — hallucinated or unverifiable claims — and because the results touch real, previously unsolved research territory rather than benchmark problems. If it holds up to community scrutiny, it's a genuine data point that frontier models are starting to do original research, not just recall or recombine known results. [SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/08/02/openais-astra-solves-10-long-open-math-problems-publishes-proofs/) · [Tech Times](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/322710/20260802/openais-astra-solves-ten-decade-old-math-problems-machine-checkable-lean-proofs.htm)

**The EU begins enforcing AI Act transparency rules.** As of August 2, the European Commission's AI Office and national authorities started enforcing a new tranche of AI Act obligations: chatbots must disclose that users are talking to an AI rather than a human, deepfakes must be labeled, and AI-generated images, video, and audio must carry machine-readable provenance marks. This is the first real enforcement teeth behind rules that were previously mostly aspirational guidance, and it sets a template — like GDPR before it — that global AI providers will likely apply well beyond the EU's borders rather than maintain separate compliance regimes per region. [European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-starts-enforcing-ai-act-rules-and-new-transparency-requirements-2-august)

**California's AI content-provenance law, SB 942, also took effect August 2.** The law requires generative-AI providers with more than 1 million monthly California users to embed provenance marks in AI-generated images, video, and audio, and to offer a free public tool for checking whether content was AI-made. Combined with the EU AI Act rules landing the same day, it's a notable coincidence of two major jurisdictions tightening AI-content disclosure rules simultaneously — increasing the odds that "AI-generated" labeling becomes a de facto global norm for large platforms rather than a regional patchwork. [Digital Strategy EU coverage / California SB 942 tracking]

The reversal signals Google wants a single consumer AI surface (Gemini) rather than a proliferation of AI-branded apps, echoing similar consolidation moves by other large AI labs. The web version at aistudio.google.com remains available. **A Chinese hacker weaponized DeepSeek for autonomous, largely unsupervised cyberattacks — and it partly worked.** Palo Alto Networks' Unit 42 detailed a Zhuhai-based actor (aliases "knaithe"/"KnYuan") who wired DeepSeek into an open-source agent framework via Telegram and attempted to exploit more than 460 internet-facing targets using both autonomous and manual methods, chaining eight CVEs across seven exploit paths. This is a concrete, documented case of an LLM being used as a semi-autonomous offensive tool at meaningful scale — a trend security researchers have warned about in the abstract for a while and are now tracking in the wild. ## FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: An Wang

An Wang isn't a household name the way Jobs or Gates are, but for about two decades he ran one of the most important computing companies in America — and his rise and fall is one of the sharper cautionary tales in tech history.

Wang was born in Shanghai in 1920 and studied science at Jiao Tong University before serving in the Chinese army's radio engineering corps during World War II. In 1945 he came to the United States, earned a PhD in applied physics at Harvard, and — while working in Harvard's Computation Laboratory — invented a method for magnetic-core memory: tiny magnetized rings that could store binary data reliably and cheaply. It became the standard memory technology for computers throughout the 1950s and into the 1960s, used in everything from early IBM mainframes to NASA's Apollo Guidance Computer.

When Harvard shut down the lab's computer research program in 1951, Wang struck out on his own with just $600, founding Wang Laboratories as effectively a one-person shop making and selling magnetic cores, with no contracts and no customers lined up. In 1955 he sold his core-memory patent to IBM — a company that would later become his fiercest competitor — and used the proceeds to fund the company's growth. Wang Labs found its footing making desktop calculators in the 1960s, then made a much bigger bet in the 1970s and '80s: dedicated word-processing computers. The Wang word processor became ubiquitous in law firms and corporate offices throughout the 1980s, at one point making Wang Laboratories one of the largest and most profitable computer companies in the world, with tens of thousands of employees and a Boston-area campus that anchored the region's tech economy.

The fall came almost as fast as the rise. Wang badly misjudged the shift to IBM-compatible personal computers and open standards, betting instead on proprietary systems even as PCs with word-processing software like WordPerfect and Microsoft Word ate his market from below. An intensely private, controlling founder, Wang also handed the CEO role to his son Fred Wang in 1986 over the objections of other executives — a succession decision widely blamed for accelerating the company's decline, since Fred lacked the industry standing to navigate the collapsing word-processor market. An Wang died in 1990; two years later, despite $1.9 billion in sales, Wang Laboratories posted a $139 million net loss and filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy. The company was eventually acquired by Getronics in 1999, and the name faded from the industry's vocabulary — though the Boston venue where the Celtics play was named the Wang Center (now Boch Center) in his honor, one of the more visible remaining traces of a company that briefly rivaled IBM.
