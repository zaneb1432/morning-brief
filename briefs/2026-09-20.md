## TECH & AI NEWS

**The frontier labs call for a slowdown — and Washington answers with an accelerator.** The defining story of the week is Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei's essay ["We Must Pace the Frontier,"](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier) published September 12, which argues that AI capabilities are now improving faster than the safety monitoring, alignment research, and institutions meant to keep pace with them — citing scenarios where a "swarm" of AI agents could within 6–12 months become capable of taking over meaningful parts of the internet. Amodei's three-part plan calls for embedded third-party evaluators inside frontier labs, coordinated U.S. regulation, and eventually global coordination (including with China). In an unusual show of unity, OpenAI's Sam Altman, xAI's Elon Musk, and Google DeepMind's Demis Hassabis all publicly endorsed the broad thrust of the proposal, even as rivals compete head-to-head. The reaction reached the White House: President Trump responded September 19 by dismissing safety concerns as overblown, [announcing plans to form an "AI Force"](https://www.axios.com/2026/09/19/trump-ai-czar-space-force-safety) modeled on the Space Force and to name an "AI czar," while vowing his administration would resist any effort to restrict the technology's growth, which he predicted could eventually account for a quarter of U.S. GDP. Separately, a lawsuit has been filed [accusing Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and SpaceX/xAI of "collusion"](https://thehill.com/policy/technology/6099571-lawsuit-accuses-anthropic-openai-spacexai-google-of-ai-pacing-collusion/) over the pacing agreement itself — a sign of how contested even a voluntary safety pact has become.

**Anthropic and Accenture bet $2B combined on embedding outside watchdogs.** On September 18, [Anthropic named Accenture's Faculty unit as its first "embedded evaluator,"](https://www.anthropic.com/news/accenture-embedded-evaluation) with both companies committing at least $1 billion each over five years. This is the first concrete implementation of the "Pace the Frontier" plan: Faculty's evaluators get employee-level access inside Anthropic to watch models being trained, review deployment decisions, and red-team safeguards in real time, rather than auditing from the outside after the fact. The arrangement is explicitly non-exclusive — Anthropic says it's in talks with nonprofit evaluator METR as well, and Accenture says it will offer similar embedded-evaluation services to other AI developers. It matters because "AI safety" has mostly meant external audits and model cards up to now; giving an outside party a badge and a desk is a meaningfully different (and more expensive) level of scrutiny, and other labs will face pressure to match it. [TechCrunch coverage.](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/18/anthropics-first-embedded-evaluator-is-accenture/)

**"Plugin4Shell" — a zero-click RCE flaw hits Claude Code, Codex, Copilot, and Gemini CLI.** Security researchers at AIR Security disclosed on September 18 [a supply-chain vulnerability](https://www.air.security/blog-posts/plugin4shell) that defeats the SHA-pinning mechanism developers rely on to lock an installed coding-agent plugin to a specific, reviewed commit. Attackers can create a git branch whose name matches the pinned 40-character commit hash; because git can prioritize a matching ref name over the actual commit object during checkout, the agent installs malicious code while still reporting a "successful" install against the expected hash. Because Claude Code and Codex auto-update plugins in the background by default, the exploit requires zero clicks from the victim. Anthropic patched Claude Code in version 2.1.179 and OpenAI fixed Codex in 0.146.0; Microsoft had not shipped a GitHub Copilot fix as of disclosure, and Google says it will not patch the already-deprecated Gemini CLI, instead telling users to migrate off it. This is being called the first real AI-agent supply-chain vulnerability, and it's a preview of a category of bugs the industry hasn't had to deal with at scale before. [Additional coverage via Help Net Security.](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/09/18/plugin4shell-ai-coding-agents-vulnerability/)

**Apple unveils the iPhone Duo, its first foldable.** At a September 9 event, Apple [introduced the iPhone Duo](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/09/apple-unveils-iphone-duo/) alongside the iPhone 18 Pro line — the company's first foldable phone and, per Apple, the biggest change to iPhone hardware in its history. The device opens to a 7.6-inch inner display (making it the thinnest iPhone ever when unfolded) and closes to a 5.4-inch outer screen retaining 90% of the iPhone 18 Pro's screen area; it's built with a machined titanium frame, Ceramic Shield front and back, and IP68 water resistance. It ships October 23 in roughly 70 countries with preorders opening October 16, starting at $1,999 and running up to $3,199 — making it the most expensive iPhone Apple has ever sold. It matters as Apple's answer to Samsung's multi-year head start in foldables, and as a test of whether a premium-priced foldable can move volume beyond early adopters. [CNN's hands-on.](https://www.cnn.com/2026/09/09/tech/apple-announces-iphone-duo-first-foldable-iphone)

**Qualcomm lands a $60B custom AI chip deal with Amazon.** Announced September 8, [Qualcomm and Amazon will co-develop custom silicon for AWS's AI data centers](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/qualcomm-amazon-to-develop-custom-chips-for-ai-data-centers-4891798) across multiple product generations, with Amazon able to purchase up to $60 billion in chips and related products over the life of the deal. Qualcomm is also granting Amazon warrants worth roughly $4 billion, vesting as purchases happen, exercisable at $161.26/share. The partnership extends to high-speed optical interconnects (up to 1.6 Tbps) needed to feed AI workloads at scale. This is a big deal for Qualcomm's diversification story — it's been courting cloud hyperscalers (Amazon now joins Microsoft and Meta) as an alternative to Nvidia, targeting $15 billion in data-center chip revenue by 2029, at a moment when its legacy smartphone-modem relationship with Apple is winding down.

## STARTUP WORLD

**Crusoe Energy raises a $3.9 billion Series F at a $30.9 billion valuation.** Crusoe, which builds AI-dedicated data center and compute infrastructure powered partly by stranded/flared natural gas, closed the first tranche of a Series F on September 17 led by Atreides Management, Mubadala Capital, Valor Equity Partners, Founders Fund, GIC, Nvidia, the Qatar Investment Authority, Radical Ventures, and TPG. It's one of the largest private AI infrastructure rounds of the year and underscores how much capital is chasing the physical, power-constrained layer of AI (data centers, energy, chips) rather than model companies themselves. [Coverage roundup via Tech Startups.](https://techstartups.com/2026/09/18/startup-funding-news-today-september-18-2026-arcos-crusoe-dtcpay-evas-intelligence-ryft-more/)

**Gimlet Labs raises $300M Series B at a $3B valuation for a "multi-silicon" AI inference cloud.** The round, announced September 4 and [led by Andreessen Horowitz](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-04/andreessen-backed-ai-startup-gimlet-is-valued-at-3-billion-in-new-round), brought in new investors Arm and M12 (Microsoft's venture arm) alongside existing backers Menlo Ventures, Sapphire Ventures, and Factory. Gimlet's pitch is that AI inference shouldn't be locked to one chip vendor: its software disaggregates model workloads and routes different phases of inference to whichever hardware — Nvidia, AMD, Intel, Arm, Cerebras, or d-Matrix silicon — is best suited, aiming to improve latency, throughput, and power efficiency. The company says it already has billions in contracted revenue and will use the capital to scale managed infrastructure into the hundreds of megawatts. It's a bet that as inference (not training) becomes the dominant AI compute cost, hardware-agnostic orchestration becomes its own category.

**Tandem Health raises €86.5M ($100M) Series B for AI-native clinical documentation.** The round, led by the Scaleup Europe Fund managed by EQT, is one of the larger recent European healthtech raises and reflects continued investor appetite for AI tools that plug directly into clinician workflows (ambient documentation, EHR automation) rather than general-purpose assistants. [Details via TechStartups' daily roundup.](https://techstartups.com/2026/09/14/startup-funding-news-today-september-14-2026-chift-qupital-tandem-health-fortaegis-more/)

**Relay, an AI workflow-automation startup, shuts down after five years.** Relay closed its doors September 15 after raising $8.1 million across two rounds since launching in 2021. Its wind-down is a useful counterpoint to the mega-rounds above: as hyperscalers and foundation-model labs bundle workflow-automation features directly into their own platforms, independent middleware startups without a defensible moat are getting squeezed out even in a record funding environment. [Post-mortem via The Startup Record.](https://thestartuprecord.com/relay-ai-startup-shutdown-acquihire-checklist)

## VENTURE CAPITAL

**Bain Capital Ventures closes $1.6B Fund XI, betting on a "post-AGI" world.** Announced September 17, [BCV's eleventh flagship fund](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/17/how-bain-capital-ventures-plans-to-deploy-its-fresh-1-6b-fund/) is 14% larger than its predecessor and marks a deliberate strategy shift: partner Matt Harris says software made up 80% of Fund IX's portfolio but only about 20% of Fund X, replaced by "AI-native" companies that sell finished work/outcomes rather than tools. The firm argues a working definition of AGI — agents performing many tasks as well as humans can — has effectively already arrived, and it's directing the new fund toward the infrastructure, physical AI, healthcare, and security startups needed to make agent-driven work reliable at scale. It's a notable data point on how the largest generalist VC firms are re-underwriting their entire theses around agents rather than treating them as a product category.

**AI captures the overwhelming majority of new VC dollars, and the biggest funds keep getting bigger.** Crunchbase reported that [Q1 2026 global venture funding hit roughly $300 billion](https://news.crunchbase.com/venture/record-breaking-funding-ai-global-q1-2026/), a record, driven almost entirely by AI — a continuation of a trend where AI-related startups captured a majority of all global VC investment in 2025, up sharply from just a few years prior. At the fund level, Andreessen Horowitz and Insight Partners each reported roughly $90 billion in assets under management in Q1 2026, ahead of Tiger Global ($58.5B) and Sequoia Capital ($56B) — reinforcing how concentrated venture capital has become around a handful of mega-firms with the balance sheets to write ever-larger AI checks.

## FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: An Wang

Long before "founder mode" was a phrase, there was An Wang — a Shanghai-born engineer who arrived in the United States in June 1945 with almost nothing, and built one of the largest computer companies in the world before an all-too-familiar founder mistake helped tear it back down.

Wang was born in Shanghai in 1920, the eldest of five children of an English teacher who pushed him hard academically. He studied communications engineering at Chiao Tung University, graduating in 1940 into a China at war, and came to the U.S. after the war's end to study at Harvard, earning a master's in applied physics in 1946 and a PhD in 1948. He stayed on at Harvard's Computation Laboratory, where he did some of the foundational work on magnetic core memory — the technology that let computers store data reliably and cheaply, and that underpinned mainframe computing for the next two decades.

When Harvard shut down its computer research program in 1951, Wang didn't look for another academic post — he founded Wang Laboratories that same year, essentially a one-person operation making and selling magnetic memory cores out of Boston. Four years later, in a move that would define his relationship to the giants of the industry, he sold a key memory-core patent to IBM for $500,000, cash that helped fund the company's next phase. Over the following two decades Wang Labs diversified from components into calculators and then into what became its signature product category: word-processing systems and desktop computers built specifically for offices, not scientists. By the 1980s, Wang Laboratories had grown into a genuine giant — gross income exceeding $2.5 billion, tens of thousands of employees, and a reputation as the company that put a computer, not just a typewriter, on every office worker's desk. An Wang personally held around 40 patents and had gone from penniless immigrant to one of the wealthiest men in America.

Then came the decision that unwound it. Preparing to step back in the mid-1980s, Wang passed over experienced non-family executives — company president John Cunningham resigned in 1985 largely over the succession plan — and installed his own son, Fred Wang, as president in 1986. Fred's tenure coincided with the company's failure to adapt to the shift from proprietary word-processing hardware to the IBM-compatible PC and open software standards sweeping the industry; losses mounted to more than $70 million in fiscal 1987 and over $300 million by fiscal 1989. An Wang eventually removed his own son from the role in 1989, but the damage to both the balance sheet and the company's strategic position was largely done. An Wang died in March 1990; two years later, in August 1992, Wang Laboratories filed for bankruptcy — one of the largest corporate bankruptcies in Massachusetts history at the time.

An Wang's story is now a fixture of business-school succession-planning case studies for exactly that reason, but it's worth remembering what came before the cautionary-tale ending: a penniless refugee scientist who invented a foundational piece of computing hardware, built a multibillion-dollar company from a one-man shop, and for three decades outcompeted much larger rivals on product design alone. The lesson isn't that founders shouldn't build empires — it's that the instincts that build one aren't always the instincts that know when, and to whom, to hand it over.
