## TECH & AI NEWS

**China's Moonshot AI releases Kimi K3, the largest open-weight model ever shipped.** Moonshot AI launched Kimi K3 on July 16, a sparse mixture-of-experts model with roughly 2.8 trillion total parameters, a 1-million-token context window, native vision, and always-on reasoning. It's priced at $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output tokens — about half the cost of Anthropic's Opus 4.8 — with full open weights promised by July 27. In blind testing by evaluator Arena, developers reportedly preferred Kimi over leading U.S. models, including Claude and GPT, for front-end coding tasks, though Moonshot itself says it still trails the top closed models on its own evaluation suite. It matters because it's the clearest evidence yet that China has closed most of the gap with U.S. frontier labs — and is doing it in the open, undercutting the pricing and licensing terms that have anchored the U.S. commercial AI stack. [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-17/china-s-powerful-new-moonshot-ai-model-closes-gap-with-us-rivals) · [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/07/16/moonshot-kimi-ai-china-model-openai-anthropic)

**Google's Gemini 3.5 Pro slips again after a scrapped pretraining run.** Gemini 3.5 Pro was expected to ship July 17; it didn't. Reporting says Google scrapped its original base model and restarted pretraining after engineers found structural failures in recursive tool-calling — a six-week-plus delay on top of earlier slippage. The episode matters because it's the second consecutive cycle where Google's flagship model has fallen short of internal expectations on coding and long-horizon reasoning, right as Anthropic prepares an IPO and Moonshot ships a competitive open-weight model at a fraction of the price — squeezing Google from both the premium and the low-cost ends of the market at once. [Tech Startups roundup](https://techstartups.com/2026/07/17/top-tech-news-today-july-17-2026-anthropic-apple-google-meta-moonshot-ai-nvidia-more/)

**Xi Jinping launches a China-led rival to Western AI governance.** On July 16-17 in Shanghai, 29 countries — including Brazil, Indonesia, South Africa, Russia, and Pakistan — signed on to found the World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organisation (WAICO), a new body headquartered in Shanghai. Xi framed it as a corrective to U.S. dominance ("AI development should not be a solo performance by a single country, but a symphony of international cooperation") and pledged 5,000 AI training slots for developing countries over five years, plus joint application centers with ASEAN, the African Union, and BRICS. It matters because the world now has two competing institutional frameworks for AI governance — one centered on Washington, Brussels, and the G7, the other assembling around Beijing and the Global South — with real consequences for whose technical standards and safety norms become the default outside the wealthy world. [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/17/chinas-xi-jinping-launches-new-ai-alliance-what-is-it) · [Xinhua](https://english.news.cn/20260716/b0449aa2133542868e310fdc45ef2969/c.html)

**Apple sues OpenAI, alleging systematic theft of hardware trade secrets.** Apple filed suit in federal court in Northern California on July 10, alleging that OpenAI — under hardware chief Tang Tan, a former Apple VP — solicited confidential designs from Apple employees during job interviews, coached departing staff on evading exit security, and pushed manufacturing partners to use an Apple-invented metal-finishing technique without permission. Apple says over 400 of its former employees now work at OpenAI. It matters because it's a dramatic reversal of the companies' 2024 partnership and signals Apple sees OpenAI's consumer hardware ambitions as a direct, and improperly sourced, threat. [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/13/the-wildest-allegations-in-apples-trade-secrets-lawsuit-against-openai/) · [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/10/apple-openai-lawsuit-trade-secrets.html)

**Mira Murati's Thinking Machines ships its first model, and admits it isn't the best one out there.** On July 15, Thinking Machines Lab released Inkling, a 975-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model (about 41 billion active parameters per task) trained on 45 trillion tokens across text, image, audio, and video, with text-only outputs for now. Unlike OpenAI, Anthropic, or Google's flagship models, it's fully open-weight — and the company says plainly that it's "not the strongest overall model available today, open or closed." The $12 billion startup doesn't plan to monetize Inkling directly; its actual revenue comes from Tinker, a fine-tuning tool already sold to customers like Bridgewater Associates. It matters as a bet that customizability, not leaderboard position, is the wedge that wins enterprise AI. [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/15/thinking-machines-amps-up-its-bet-against-one-size-fits-all-ai-with-its-first-open-model-inkling/) · [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/07/15/what-is-mira-murati-thinking-machines-first-ai-model-inkling/)

## FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: An Wang

Long before Silicon Valley existed as a phrase, there was An Wang — a Shanghai-born engineer who built one of America's most important computing companies from a $600 loan and a bright idea about memory.

Wang was born in 1920 near Shanghai, the son of an English teacher. He studied electrical engineering at Shanghai Jiao Tong University, graduating in 1940 into a country at war — Japan had invaded years earlier, and Wang spent part of the war helping the Chinese government build radio transmitters from salvaged parts, work that shaped his lifelong instinct for practical engineering under constraint. In 1945, with the war ending, the Chinese government sent him to the United States to study advanced technology. He enrolled at Harvard, earning a PhD in applied physics in 1948.

At Harvard's Computation Laboratory, Wang made the discovery that would define his career. Within his first month on the job, he conceived of magnetic-core memory: tiny magnetized rings that could store binary data and, crucially, be read and then immediately rewritten without losing the information — a "write-after-read" trick that made memory both fast and reliable. Working with colleague Way-Dong Woo, he turned the idea into a patented device. It became the dominant computer memory technology for nearly two decades, used in everything from IBM mainframes to NASA's Apollo guidance computers.

When Harvard scaled back its computing research in 1951, Wang didn't look for another institutional post — he started his own company. Wang Laboratories began as a one-man operation in a small Boston office, living off consulting work while An Wang built out a real engineering business. In 1955, he sold his core-memory patent to IBM for $500,000, seed capital that let him incorporate the company properly with a schoolmate, Ge-Yao Chu.

Wang Labs spent the 1960s building calculators and scientific instruments, but its defining act came in the 1970s, when Wang bet the company on dedicated word-processing machines — purpose-built systems, not general computers, designed specifically for office typing and document editing. It was a shrewd read of what businesses actually needed, and it worked spectacularly: by the mid-1980s, Wang Laboratories was a dominant force in office automation, employing over 30,000 people at its peak in 1989 and making An Wang one of the wealthiest immigrants in American history.

But the same instinct that made Wang Labs great — building specialized machines rather than general-purpose ones — became its undoing. As the IBM PC and open, general-purpose computing platforms swept through offices in the late 1980s, Wang Labs was slow to pivot away from its proprietary systems. An Wang died of cancer in 1990, and two years later, the company he'd built from nothing filed for bankruptcy.

Wang's legacy outlasted the company's collapse. He held dozens of patents, received the Medal of Liberty in 1986 as one of twelve honored naturalized citizens, and was inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame in 1988. He gave generously to Boston-area institutions, including funding a performing arts center that still bears his name. His story remains one of the sharpest lessons in American tech history: the same clarity of vision that builds an empire can just as easily blind you to the next one arriving.
