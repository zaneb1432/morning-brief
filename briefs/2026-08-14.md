## TECH & AI NEWS

**Google ships Gemini 3.7 Flash — and jumps ahead of its own flagship.** On August 13, Google released Gemini 3.7 Flash, a lower-cost "workhorse" model aimed squarely at coding and agentic workflows, arriving just three weeks after Gemini 3.6 Flash and notably before the still-delayed Gemini 3.5 Pro. Google says the new model beats comparable offerings from Anthropic and OpenAI across nine benchmarks and is markedly better at producing deployable, production-ready code on the first try. Pricing is aggressive too: $0.75 per million input tokens and $3.75 per million output through the end of 2026 — half the cost of its predecessor — available now in Google Antigravity, AI Studio, Android Studio, the Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform, and rolling out to Gemini app subscribers. The rapid Flash-tier cadence, even as the higher-end Pro model slips, suggests Google is prioritizing developer and agent workloads while it works out whatever is holding up its top-tier release. Source: [blog.google](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/introducing-gemini-3-7-flash/), [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-13/google-debuts-new-gemini-flash-while-top-ai-model-still-delayed)

**Apple builds its own AI model for China, with help from Alibaba.** Reuters reports (via multiple outlets, August 14) that Apple has trained a proprietary large language model specifically for the Chinese market in partnership with Alibaba, a marked break from its usual approach of licensing outside models for local markets. Apple Intelligence is expected to launch in China in the coming months following an iOS update, and the arrangement reportedly makes Apple the first foreign company approved by Beijing to offer a proprietary AI model domestically. Apple had previously agreed to fold Alibaba's Qwen model into Apple Intelligence features in China to satisfy local regulators; this in-house model appears to give Apple more direct control over its China AI stack as it faces growing competitive pressure from Huawei and other domestic phone makers. It's a rare case of Apple building rather than buying AI capability, and a sign of how central China-specific compliance and competitiveness have become to its AI strategy. Source: [MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/08/14/apple-trained-own-ai-model-for-china/), [Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/business/2026/08/14/apple-ai-model-china-alibaba/)

**DeepSeek ships V4-Pro out of preview — then has to raise prices to cope with demand.** DeepSeek moved its flagship DeepSeek-V4-Pro-0813 model to general availability on August 12–13, a mixture-of-experts system with 1.6 trillion total parameters (49 billion active per token), a context window up to 1 million tokens, and heavy emphasis on agentic tool-use tasks — scoring 87.9 on Terminal-Bench 2.1 among other agent benchmarks. The launch came with a catch: after DeepSeek matched OpenAI's price cuts earlier this month, demand reportedly surged to roughly 7 trillion tokens a week, more than its ~20,000-GPU fleet could comfortably absorb, forcing the company to announce a peak-hour price increase (output tokens rising from a flat $0.87 per million to as much as $3.96 per million) effective August 16. It's a vivid illustration of the current AI price war's dynamics: cutting prices to win share is easy, but scaling the compute to serve that share at the new price is the harder problem. Source: [Unite.AI](https://www.unite.ai/deepseek-ships-v4-pro-as-its-flagship-model-leaves-preview/), [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/08/13/deepseek-increases-prices-for-ai-services-by-multiple-times/)

**The AI price war intensifies as OpenAI and Anthropic cut, and DeepSeek is forced to hike.** Beyond the DeepSeek-specific story, the broader pattern this week is a full-blown pricing skirmish among the top model providers. OpenAI cut GPT-5.6 Luna's input price by 80% (from $1 to $0.20 per million tokens) and output price similarly, effective late July, while Anthropic's introductory Claude Sonnet 5 pricing of $2/$10 per million tokens is set to revert to a standard $3/$15 on September 1 — a reminder that "price cuts" in this market are often promotional rather than structural. The net effect is a market where frontier labs are using price as a primary competitive lever, and where Chinese providers who tried to out-price everyone are discovering that demand elasticity cuts both ways. For any company building on these APIs, the pricing floor is moving fast enough that architecture decisions from even a month ago may already be economically stale. Source: [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/08/13/deepseek-increases-prices-for-ai-services-by-multiple-times/), [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/sectors/technology/articles/openai-wants-price-war-anthropic-223103426.html)

**Uber and Pony.ai expand robotaxi partnership to 2,000+ vehicles across Europe and the Middle East.** Announced August 14, Chinese autonomous-driving firm Pony.ai and Uber are expanding their partnership — which began in Zagreb, Croatia — to deploy over 2,000 robotaxis across four additional (as yet unnamed) European cities plus markets in the Middle East, though no firm rollout timeline was given. Under the deal, Pony.ai supplies the autonomous driving technology while Uber contributes its ride-hailing platform and demand; fleet operations like maintenance, cleaning, and charging will be handled by local partners, with ownership of vehicles varying by market. This is one of the most concrete signs yet that autonomous ride-hailing is moving from single-city pilots toward genuine multi-market scale, and it puts a Chinese AV company at the center of Uber's international robotaxi strategy at a moment when European regulators are still working out AV rules. Source: [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/14/uber-and-pony-ai-plan-to-bring-2000-robotaxis-to-europe/), [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/14/uber-partners-with-chinas-ponyai-for-2000-robotaxis-in-europe.html)

## STARTUP WORLD

**Valar Atomics raises $1 billion to mass-produce nuclear microreactors for AI data centers.** Sequoia Capital led a $1 billion Series B for Valar Atomics on August 3, valuing the nuclear startup at $6 billion and helping fund its shift from demonstrating small modular reactors to manufacturing them at volume; the company also lined up a $200 million credit facility, with Atreides Management, Point72, and Snowpoint Ventures also participating and Sequoia's Shaun Maguire joining the board. Valar's pitch is that factory-built small reactors can be delivered faster and cheaper than traditional nuclear projects, directly targeting the power-hungry AI data-center buildout — in July the company already used a live reactor to power an Nvidia chip and host a website, and it's now planning a 30-megawatt AI facility in Utah. It's one of the clearest signals yet that "AI needs power" has become its own investable category, distinct from chips or models. ## FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: An Wang

An Wang's story is one of the more dramatic rise-and-fall arcs in American technology history — a founder who built one of the era's great high-tech companies from nothing, then watched it collapse in part because of a decision he made about family succession.

Wang was born February 7, 1920, in Shanghai, the oldest of five children in a family where his father taught him English at home before formal schooling began. He excelled at science and mathematics as a child, and as a teenager grew fascinated with radio, eventually building his own sets and studying communications engineering at Chiao-Tung University. When Japan occupied Shanghai during World War II, Wang fled to Guilin, where he led a small team scavenging parts to improvise radios for Chinese military use — an early, high-pressure lesson in engineering under constraint that would echo throughout his career. After the war he immigrated to the United States in 1945 and earned a PhD in applied physics from Harvard, where he made a genuinely foundational contribution to computing: a practical method for magnetic core memory, one of the key technologies that made reliable digital computer memory possible before semiconductor RAM existed.

When Harvard shut down its computer research program in 1951, Wang struck out on his own, founding Wang Laboratories as effectively a one-man operation making and selling magnetic memory cores. The pivotal early break came in 1956, when he sold his core-memory patent to IBM for $400,000 — capital that let him build out the company in earnest. Over the following two decades Wang Laboratories grew into one of the most successful American technology companies of the 1970s and early 1980s, first with desktop calculators and then with dedicated word-processing systems that dominated the office market before general-purpose PCs caught up; Wang personally held around 40 patents by the end of his career.

The company's undoing has become a classic case study in succession risk. In 1985, amid signs of slowing growth, Wang installed his son Fred Wang as company president — a decision widely seen at the time as prioritizing family control over proven management. It did not work out: by the late 1980s the company was in clear decline, undercut by its overreliance on dedicated word-processing hardware just as general PCs with word-processing software ate that market from underneath it. An Wang was forced to fire his own son as president on August 4, 1989, and imposed layoffs and a wage freeze in a last effort to stabilize the business. He died on March 24, 1990, in Boston — reportedly before Wang Laboratories' fate was fully sealed, but with the writing already on the wall. The company filed for bankruptcy protection on August 18, 1992, a little over two years after his death, in what remains one of the starkest examples of a technology pioneer's core innovation — magnetic core memory — being eclipsed by the very industry-wide shift to general-purpose computing that his own company failed to adapt to in time.
