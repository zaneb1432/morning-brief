## TECH & AI NEWS

**Apple briefly dethroned Nvidia as the world's most valuable company on July 17**, touching a market cap near $4.9 trillion intraday before Nvidia clawed back some ground by the close. The flip happened as much because of Nvidia's weakness as Apple's strength: Nvidia shares fell as much as 4.6% amid renewed jitters over AI-capex sustainability, while Apple has climbed roughly 23% this year on the theory that it's better positioned than the chipmakers to *monetize* AI — via services, ecosystem lock-in, and on-device hardware upgrades — without the capital-intensity risk. It's a notable sentiment shift: for two years the market treated Apple as the AI laggard for not building its own frontier models; now investors are rewarding distribution over raw compute. Whether Apple holds the title matters less than what it signals — the market rotating away from "who has the most GPUs" and toward "who actually captures the AI revenue." [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/17/apple-nvidia-aapl-nvda-market-cap.html) · [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/tylerroush/2026/07/17/apple-unseats-nvidia-as-worlds-largest-company/)

**Google's Gemini 3.5 Pro has slipped again, and this time it cost Alphabet real money.** Reports surfaced this week that the model — unveiled at I/O in May with a June rollout promised — is now months behind schedule because it's falling short of internal targets specifically on coding and complex reasoning, the two areas OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol and Meta's newer models have been pulling ahead on. Alphabet shares dropped roughly 4.4%, erasing close to $200 billion in market value, on the news alone. The delay is the clearest sign yet that the "who has the best frontier model" race has gotten genuinely uncertain: Google shipped Gemini 3 with fanfare, but shipping a coding-competitive follow-up on schedule is turning out to be a different problem than raw benchmark chasing. With no new launch date given, this is now a live wound rather than a rumor. [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/16/alphabet-stock-gemini-3-5-pro-ai.html) · [TechTimes](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/319877/20260708/gemini-35-pro-targets-july-17-deepseeks-july-24-deadline-hits-developers-now.htm)

**Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 just became the largest open-weight model ever released, and the benchmarks are the real story.** The 2.8-trillion-parameter MoE model (896 experts, 16 active per token, 1M-token context) posted a SWE-bench score around 67.5 and reportedly landed #3 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index — in the same tier as Anthropic's Opus 4.8 and OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol — while pricing out at roughly $2.31 per million tokens blended, a fraction of frontier closed-model pricing. Full weights are promised by July 27 under a modified MIT license. This lands the same week Thinking Machines shipped its own open model (see below), making the "open-weight offensive" — Kimi K3, DeepSeek's forthcoming release, Inkling — the single most disruptive trend of the summer: if free-to-run models keep matching frontier closed models, the subscription-based economics that OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google are all betting on get much harder to defend. [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/07/16/moonshot-kimi-ai-china-model-openai-anthropic) · [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/07/17/china-moonshot-kimi-k3-markets-china-ai/)

**China used this week's World AI Conference in Shanghai to launch a rival governance bloc, and Xi Jinping personally opened it** — a first. The new World AI Cooperation Organisation (WAICO), headquartered in Shanghai, launched with 29 founding members including Brazil, Indonesia, South Africa, Russia, and Pakistan, and drew UN Secretary-General António Guterres to the stage. Xi pledged 5,000 AI training slots for developing nations over five years and AI cooperation centers with ASEAN, the African Union, and BRICS, framing it — pointedly, amid tightening US export controls — as a rejection of AI being "a solo performance by a single country." This is Beijing explicitly building an alternative standards and governance track for the Global South rather than trying to win a seat at Western-led AI-safety tables, and it's happening in parallel with Chinese labs (Moonshot, DeepSeek) actually out-executing on open models — a pincer move on both the technical and diplomatic fronts. [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/17/chinas-xi-jinping-launches-new-ai-alliance-what-is-it) · [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/17/nx-s1-5897285/chinas-xi-calls-for-step-up-of-global-effort-in-ai-as-us-curbs-squeeze-chinas-tech-access)

**Mira Murati's Thinking Machines Lab shipped its first public model, Inkling, on July 15** — a 975B-parameter MoE (41B active) trained on 45 trillion multimodal tokens, open-weighted on Hugging Face and tunable via the company's Tinker fine-tuning platform. It's explicitly not positioned as a frontier-beating model; the pitch is that it's the best *base* for customization — efficient, natively multimodal, and free to adapt — which is a distinct strategic bet from OpenAI/Anthropic/Google's "biggest model wins" approach. Coming from a lab stocked with ex-OpenAI talent and sitting on a massive valuation with barely any public track record yet, this is Thinking Machines' first real product proof point, and it reinforces the same theme as Kimi K3: the frontier-vs-open-weight line is blurring fast, and the next competitive axis may be who builds the best fine-tuning ecosystem rather than who has the single best closed model. [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/15/thinking-machines-amps-up-its-bet-against-one-size-fits-all-ai-with-its-first-open-model-inkling/) · [Thinking Machines Lab](https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/introducing-inkling/)

**Microsoft is reportedly close to launching "Project Perception,"** an AI-driven vulnerability-detection platform that routes security work across Microsoft's own models plus OpenAI's and Anthropic's, positioned squarely as a cheaper rival to Anthropic's Mythos security model. The pitch is a smart-routing layer that matches each scanning job to whichever model handles it best, undercutting Mythos on cost while leaning on Microsoft's global distribution through Defender and Azure. It's being driven by Hayete Gallot, who took over Microsoft's security org in February and has been reorienting it around AI-native products at the expense of legacy tooling. The subtext is bigger than one product: it's Microsoft using its platform reach to commoditize a category — AI-assisted vulnerability discovery — that Anthropic had been treating as a premium differentiator, the same playbook Microsoft has run against category leaders for decades. [TechRepublic](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-microsoft-project-perception-ai-security-tool/) · [Neowin](https://www.neowin.net/news/recent-patches-for-windows-11-could-have-been-created-by-microsofts-new-mythos-rival/)

## STARTUP WORLD

**Fireworks AI locked in a $1.5 billion Series D at a $17.5 billion valuation this week**, more than doubling its worth from earlier this year as the inference-infrastructure race keeps minting winners. The round was led by Atreides Management, Index Ventures, and TCV, with Nvidia, Bessemer, Menlo, and Lightspeed among the piled-on backers — a rare show of consensus for a company that doesn't build models, it just runs them faster and cheaper for everyone else. Founder and CEO Lin Qiao, who co-created PyTorch at Meta, says Fireworks now processes 40 trillion tokens a day and has crossed $1 billion in annualized revenue, a 5x jump year-over-year, with more than 95% of that volume coming from models fine-tuned on customers' own data rather than generic frontier queries. That's the real signal here: enterprises are done renting raw intelligence and want it customized, and Fireworks has positioned itself as the toll booth for that shift. [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/16/fireworks-nvidia-cloud-ai-startup-value.html) · [Businesswire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260716264405/en/Fireworks-Raises-a-$1.5-Billion-Series-D-to-Lead-the-Specialized-Intelligence-Revolution)

**SAP this week completed its acquisition of Prior Labs**, the Freiburg-based tabular-data AI startup, committing more than €1 billion over four years to turn it into a frontier AI lab for Europe — a wild outcome for a company that raised just €9 million in pre-seed funding fifteen months ago. Founders Frank Hutter, Noah Hollmann, and Sauraj Gambhir built TabPFN, an open-source model for structured-data prediction (the financial forecasting, supply-chain, and risk-analysis workloads that actually run most enterprises) that's been downloaded more than 3 million times. SAP is letting Prior Labs keep its brand, leadership, and open-research posture, betting that the next enterprise-AI moat isn't chatbots but models purpose-built for the boring tabular data every large company already owns. It's also a rare instance of a European AI startup exiting to a European buyer rather than getting scooped up by a US hyperscaler — a talent-retention story Brussels will be happy to cite. [Tech.eu](https://tech.eu/2026/07/17/sap-acquires-prior-labs-just-18-months-after-launch-in-eur1b-deal/) · [EU-Startups](https://www.eu-startups.com/2026/07/germanys-prior-labs-raises-e1-billion-and-exits-to-sap-18-months-after-being-founded/)

**Marc Lore's Wonder closed a $650 million Series D at a $9 billion pre-money valuation**, with Accel, GV, and NEA returning alongside new entrants including ARK Invest and AllianceBernstein-managed funds — and Lore telling Fortune the automated-restaurant platform is now gearing up for an IPO. The company has tripled its footprint from 46 to 140 locations since its last raise in May 2025 and has now pulled in roughly $3 billion total since founding in 2018, leaning on its "Infinite Kitchen" robotic bowl-assembly system to make the unit economics of multi-brand delivery actually work. Notably, the round carries an IPO ratchet that hands investors extra shares if the public debut prices below 1.5x this round's share value — a hedge that tells you late-stage backers still don't fully trust food-tech valuations to hold. Still, in a public-offering environment starved for consumer tech names, Wonder looks like one of the more credible candidates to actually test it. [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/07/16/exclusive-marc-lore-says-wonder-is-gearing-up-for-an-ipo-650-million-9-billion-valuation-cathie-wood-grubhub-food-delivery/)

**On the retreat side of the ledger: BP is shutting down its 20-year-old corporate venture arm**, selling the bulk of its roughly $1.2 billion portfolio — stakes in more than a dozen startups spanning green hydrogen, e-mobility, ride-hailing, and geothermal — to Nordic private-equity firm Verdane. New CEO Meg O'Neill, three months into the job, is using the sale as a signal of the "financial discipline" pivot back toward core oil-and-gas operations, effectively declaring two decades of corporate clean-tech venture bets a distraction rather than a strategy. For founders who'd built relationships with BP Ventures as a patient strategic investor, it's a reminder that corporate VC money is only as durable as the parent company's current CEO and commodity cycle — and one more oil major stepping back from venture just as traditional VC is already pulling back from climate and clean-energy deals. ## VENTURE CAPITAL

**Abu Dhabi's MGX closed Fund I at $49 billion on July 1**, blowing past its already-massive $45 billion target and cementing the Mubadala/G42-backed vehicle as arguably the largest pool of dedicated AI capital on the planet. Chaired by Sheikh Tahnoon bin Zayed Al Nahyan, MGX has deployed across the full AI stack — chips, infrastructure, platforms — with a portfolio of just 14 companies that reads like a who's-who of frontier AI: OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, plus SpaceX and TikTok USDS. Its October 2025 acquisition of Aligned Data Centers, a roughly $40 billion deal done alongside BlackRock's Global Infrastructure Partners, shows the fund operating more like an infrastructure buyout shop than a traditional venture investor. For US VCs, MGX's scale is now a structural fact of the market — it can write checks that dwarf entire fund families, giving Gulf sovereign capital outsized leverage over which labs win the compute race. ## FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: Chester Carlson

Chester Carlson was born on February 8, 1906, in Seattle, into a poverty so persistent it shaped everything he later did with money. His father, Olof, was a barber whose eyesight was failing and whose spine was curling under the early stages of arthritis; his mother, Ellen, had tuberculosis. By age 14, Chester was effectively the family breadwinner, working before and after school to keep his parents housed while the family drifted around California in search of cheaper rent and better climate. His mother died when he was 17. His father died a few years later, while Chester was still a student. He put himself through Riverside Junior College and then the California Institute of Technology, graduating in 1930 with a degree in physics, weighed down by roughly $1,400 in debt — a small fortune during the Depression years that followed.

Jobs were scarce for a young physicist in 1930, and Carlson bounced between engineering posts before landing, in 1934, in the patent department of P.R. Mallory & Co., an electronics manufacturer in New York. There he found his real problem. Patent applications required multiple copies of drawings and text, and the only options were carbon paper, which produced smudged and limited runs, or expensive photostat machines that required sending documents out and waiting days for a print. Carlson, by then also enrolled at night in New York Law School, was drowning in duplication work. Making it worse, he had inherited his father's arthritis of the spine, and the physical strain of retyping and rewriting documents by hand was genuinely painful. "I thought the possibility of making an invention might kill two birds with one stone," he later said. "It would be a chance to do the world some good and also a chance to do myself some good."

He began teaching himself photoconductivity and electrostatics at the New York Public Library's reading room, then experimenting in the kitchen of his apartment in Jackson Heights, Queens — much to the irritation of his first wife, Elsa, whose patience for chemical smells and small explosions was limited. In 1938, working with an out-of-work Austrian physicist named Otto Kornei whom he paid a small fee, Carlson set up a makeshift lab above a bar in Astoria, Queens, using a zinc plate dusted with sulfur, a cotton handkerchief to generate static charge by friction, and lycopodium powder — a fine plant spore — to visualize the image. On October 22, 1938, the two men rubbed the plate, exposed it to light through a glass slide, dusted on the powder, and transferred it to wax paper. The resulting image read simply "10.-22.-38 ASTORIA." It was the first xerographic copy ever made, produced with equipment that amounted to little more than a cigar box, a hot plate, and a rabbit's foot for spreading powder.

Carlson patented the process and spent the next six years trying to sell it, and nearly everyone said no. Between 1939 and 1944 he pitched more than twenty companies, including IBM, General Electric, RCA, Kodak, and Remington Rand. Kodak and others already sold photostat and photographic copying equipment and saw no market for a strange dry process that, in its early demonstrations, was unreliable and produced smudgy results. Even the National Inventors Council, a wartime clearinghouse for new technology, passed on it. Carlson kept his patent attorney job through all of this, absorbing rejection after rejection while quietly refining the chemistry on the side.

The turn came in 1944, when Carlson persuaded the nonprofit Battelle Memorial Institute in Columbus, Ohio, to take on development work in exchange for a share of future royalties. Battelle's engineers spent three years improving the process, and in 1947 a small, struggling photographic-paper company in Rochester, New York — the Haloid Company — licensed the rights. Haloid's president, Joseph C. Wilson, had bet the modest firm's future on a technology most of the copying industry had already dismissed. Haloid's marketing team coined a new word for the dry process, borrowing from the Greek for "dry writing": xerography. It took another decade of engineering to make it into a machine people could actually buy or rent.

That machine, the Xerox 914, arrived on September 16, 1959 — twenty-one years, almost to the week, after the Astoria experiment. It weighed nearly 650 pounds, cost about $27,500 to build, and was prone to catching small paper fires (Xerox shipped every unit with a "scorch eliminator," a small fire extinguisher, tucked inside). Rather than sell the machines outright, Haloid-Xerox leased them for $95 a month, including 2,000 free copies, then charged four cents per copy after that. Offices that had photocopied a few dozen pages a month began running off thousands, and copy volumes across corporate America exploded almost overnight. Xerox's revenue nearly doubled from $31 million in 1959 to $59 million by 1961, and Fortune magazine later called the 914 "the most successful product ever marketed in America" measured by return on investment. By 1968, Xerox's annual sales topped $1 billion.

Carlson's royalties and stock made him extraordinarily rich — by some estimates one of the wealthiest men in the country by the late 1960s — and he responded to that wealth in a way almost nobody in his position does: he set out to give nearly all of it away, as quietly as possible. He funded civil rights organizations, world-peace and disarmament groups, Buddhist study centers (he had become a serious student of Zen and Vedanta philosophy in his later years), and the United Negro College Fund, almost always anonymously and often through intermediaries so recipients wouldn't know the source. When Fortune published a list of America's richest people in 1968 and estimated his fortune at over $150 million, Carlson wrote back correcting them: he said he belonged in the "0 to $50 million bracket," because he had already quietly given away more.

He died on September 19, 1968, of a heart attack in a movie theater on West 57th Street in Manhattan, watching a Japanese film. He was 62. By the time of his death he had given away more than $100 million — the vast majority of the fortune xerography had generated — leaving behind an estate that was, by his own design, a fraction of what the world assumed he had. The machine he built in a rented room above a bar, using a cigar box and a rabbit's foot, is arguably the reason the word "Xerox" is still used as a verb.
