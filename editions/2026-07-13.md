## TECH & AI NEWS

**SK Hynix pulls off the largest-ever foreign listing in U.S. history.** On Friday, the South Korean memory chip maker priced its Nasdaq IPO at $149 per ADS, raising roughly $26.5 billion and surpassing Alibaba's 2014 debut as the biggest U.S. listing by a non-American company. Shares jumped nearly 13% on their first day of trading. The offering is really a bet on the AI memory shortage: SK Hynix controls 56.4% of the high-bandwidth memory (HBM) market that feeds Nvidia's and AMD's AI accelerators, and demand from hyperscalers has outstripped supply for over a year. It matters because it's a signal that public markets — not just private mega-rounds — are now absorbing the AI infrastructure boom, and because it hands U.S. investors direct exposure to the chip layer of the AI stack for the first time. [CNN Business](https://www.cnn.com/2026/07/10/business/sk-hynix-us-listing-ai-chip-boom-intl-hnk) | [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/10/sk-hynix-raises-26-5b-in-the-biggest-foreign-ipo-in-us-history-is-urged-to-build-new-us-fabs/)

**OpenAI ships GPT-5.6 after a government-mandated delay.** GPT-5.6 — comprising flagship model Sol, mid-tier Terra, and low-cost Luna — went publicly available on July 9, roughly two weeks after the U.S. government asked OpenAI to restrict rollout to a small group of "trusted partners" while the Center for AI Standards and Innovation (part of the Commerce Department) reviewed its coding and cybersecurity capabilities. OpenAI paired the release with ChatGPT Work, an agentic productivity tool built on Codex. This is the first time a frontier model has gone through an explicit pre-release government security check outside a formal statutory regime, and OpenAI's own statement — "we don't believe this kind of government access process should become the long-term default" — signals a brewing fight over how much oversight the White House gets before frontier launches. On SWE-Bench Pro it resolves tasks using roughly a quarter of the output tokens Opus 4.8 uses for the same work (15,954 vs. 67,020 tokens), a real efficiency gap rather than just a benchmark score. Elon Musk called it "an Opus-class model, but faster, more token-efficient and lower cost" — the first time xAI has directly claimed parity with Anthropic's top tier rather than just OpenAI's. Sessions can move across devices — start a task on a laptop, check progress from a phone, pick up the finished result anywhere — and now run in the background without a persistent internet connection. Anthropic says data from 1.2 million Cowork sessions shows over 90% of usage isn't coding at all, but business operations and content creation, which is the more interesting data point: it suggests agentic AI's near-term center of gravity is knowledge work broadly, not just software engineering. [9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2026/07/07/anthropic-expanding-claude-cowork-to-mobile-and-web-details-here/)

**Governments spent two days in Geneva trying to catch up to frontier AI.** The UN's Global Dialogue on AI Governance ran July 6–7, bringing governments, companies, academics, and civil society together to debate how to regulate a technology moving faster than any existing rulebook, amid explicit warnings about "catastrophic harm." In parallel, the U.S. government is reportedly in advanced talks with AI companies over voluntary standards for releasing new models — a lighter-touch approach than statutory licensing, but one that (as the GPT-5.6 delay shows) already gives Washington real leverage over launch timing. Together the two threads point to 2026 as the year informal, negotiated oversight became the de facto regulatory model for frontier AI, ahead of any binding law. [UN News](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/07/1167862)

## FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: Lynn Conway

Long before "founder" became startup vocabulary, Lynn Conway built and rebuilt an entire career from scratch — twice — and each time reshaped how computers get made.

Conway joined IBM Research in the 1960s after earning her engineering degrees at Columbia, and by the middle of the decade had invented generalized dynamic instruction handling, a technique that let a processor issue multiple instructions out of their original order to keep its pipeline full. It's a foundational idea behind out-of-order execution, the trick that powers nearly every modern CPU. IBM fired her in 1968 the moment she told the company she intended to transition. She lost her job, her marriage, and contact with her children in the same year — and disappeared from the historical record as "Lynn Conway" entirely, rebuilding her career under a new identity as a contract programmer, hiding the fact that the engineer who'd just been let go from IBM and the one starting over were the same person.

She rebuilt from nothing: computer architecture work at Memorex, then in 1973 a research position at Xerox PARC, the lab that would also give the world the graphical user interface and Ethernet. There, working with Carver Mead, Conway co-created what became known as the Mead–Conway VLSI design methodology — a structured way of describing chip layouts that let engineers who weren't specialists design their own complex integrated circuits. Their 1980 textbook, *Introduction to VLSI Systems*, was in use at nearly 120 universities within three years and is credited with kicking off the 1980s boom in custom chip design that underlies the entire modern semiconductor industry — the same industry, not incidentally, that produced this week's SK Hynix IPO and the AI chip land-grab dominating this brief.

Conway spent decades of her career quietly stealth, unwilling to risk her second act by disclosing her past. In 1999, after learning a journalist was about to out her, she chose to come forward on her own terms and began speaking publicly about being transgender — becoming one of the most visible trans advocates in tech at a time when almost no one in the industry was. In 2020, 52 years after firing her, IBM formally apologized and presented her with a Lifetime Achievement Award. She died in June 2024 at age 86, having spent her final decades as a professor at the University of Michigan, mentoring the next generation of chip designers.

Her story is a useful corrective to how founder mythology usually gets told: no garage, no cap table, no exit. Just a body of technical work good enough that an industry rebuilt itself around it twice — once under a name that got erased, and once under the name she reclaimed.
