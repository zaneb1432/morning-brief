## 1. Tech & AI News

## 4. Founder Biography: An Wang

Long before "founder" was a job title people put on their LinkedIn before they'd built anything, An Wang was quietly rewriting what a computer's memory could be. Born in Shanghai in 1920, Wang trained as an engineer, served in the Chinese army's radio unit during World War II, and emigrated to the United States in 1945 with almost nothing — he would later say he arrived with $50 in his pocket. He talked his way into Harvard's doctoral program in applied physics and, working at Harvard's Computation Laboratory in the late 1940s, invented a method for storing binary data on tiny magnetized rings of ferrite — what became known as magnetic core memory.

Core memory would go on to be the dominant form of computer memory for roughly two decades, used in nearly every mainframe built through the 1960s and 70s, including the computers that guided the Apollo missions. But Wang didn't get rich off the invention directly — Harvard, not Wang, initially held rights to some of the underlying research, and in a move that says a lot about the era's patent landscape, Wang ultimately sold his core-memory patents to IBM in 1956 for $500,000, a fraction of what the technology was ultimately worth to the industry. Rather than treat that as a loss, he used the money as seed capital.

With those proceeds, Wang built Wang Laboratories, founded in 1951, into a genuine giant: by the 1970s and early 1980s it was one of the most successful computer companies in America, dominant first in desktop calculators and then in dedicated word-processing systems that sat on secretaries' desks in nearly every major office in the country before the IBM PC and Microsoft Word existed. At its peak, Wang Labs employed over 30,000 people and made An Wang the fifth-richest person in America by 1984 — an extraordinary trajectory for a refugee scientist who'd sold his signature invention for half a million dollars two decades earlier.

The company's collapse was as fast as its rise. Wang badly misjudged the shift from proprietary word processors to open, IBM-compatible personal computers, doubled down on incompatible in-house systems, and compounded the problem by insisting his son succeed him as CEO despite objections from the board. Wang Laboratories filed for bankruptcy in 1992, a year after An Wang's death, and was eventually absorbed into Getronics in 1999. His story remains one of the sharpest illustrations in tech history of a founder who was a genuinely brilliant inventor, a shrewd bootstrapper, and ultimately not immune to the same hubris that has ended plenty of dynasties since.
