## 🤖 TECH & AI NEWS

### 1. FCC approves SpaceX's 1-million-satellite "orbital data center" constellation
On Wednesday (Nov 25), FCC Chairman Brendan Carr announced conditional approval for SpaceX to deploy up to **1 million satellites** built to run AI computing in orbit. SpaceX calls the effort *Starmind*. It's the first time the FCC has approved an orbital data center constellation, and other applications are waiting behind it. Amazon, Viasat and the American Astronomical Society opposed the plan over orbital debris, radio interference and harm to astronomy. The FCC said many questions about whether the idea works or what it does long-term fall outside its mandate. Debris-mitigation rules for satellites flying above 600 km are still not approved. Outlets disagree on the build-out deadlines in the order, and SpaceX has given different launch dates in different places: 2027 to investors, "as early as 2028" in its IPO filing.
**Why it matters:** The AI compute race has run into a ceiling of power and land on Earth. This approval makes space-based compute a real (if speculative) option with regulatory cover. It also lets SpaceX claim scarce orbital capacity before rivals like Amazon's Kuiper can.
🔗 [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/25/spacex-fcc-satellite-approval.html) · [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/25/fcc-approves-spacex-plan-to-put-ai-data-centers-in-space/) · [Via Satellite](https://www.satellitetoday.com/connectivity/2026/11/25/fcc-approves-spacexs-1-million-satellite-orbital-data-center-constellation/)

### 2. Nvidia reportedly halts AI server shipments to four Southeast Asian countries
The Financial Times reported Thursday that Nvidia told server partners, reportedly including Dell and Supermicro, to stop shipping AI servers with its chips to **Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia**. The pause follows new U.S. export rules that require licenses for advanced AI chips sold to data centers in those countries (and reportedly Brazil). It's unclear whether the halt is temporary while partners sort out compliance. Nvidia, Dell and Supermicro hadn't commented.
**Why it matters:** These four countries have become the main back door for Chinese AI labs, which rent Nvidia compute from cloud providers there. Closing that route tightens the squeeze on Chinese frontier labs. It also hits a fast-growing Southeast Asian data center boom, and one report says lenders have already frozen ~$500M in credit tied to these shipments.
🔗 [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/siladityaray/2026/11/26/nvidia-reportedly-halts-ai-chip-shipments-to-southeast-asia-as-us-tightens-china-curbs/) · [TrendForce](https://www.trendforce.com/news/2026/11/26/news-nvidia-reportedly-suspends-ai-server-shipments-to-four-southeast-asian-countries-as-u-s-targets-chinese-ai-access/)

### 3. Bank of England: hyperscalers' AI exposure tops $4 trillion
The BoE's Financial Stability Report (Nov 26) estimates the hyperscalers' total potential exposure at **just over $4 trillion**. That counts debt plus off-balance-sheet commitments like leases, purchase agreements and contingent obligations. The Financial Policy Committee says these companies are on track to issue **$305B+ in bonds this year**, about 8% of all global investment-grade corporate issuance. It called the financing "increasingly complex, circular and opaque" and warned that stress in one corner of AI could spread into sovereign debt and equity markets. The report also flags fast-improving frontier AI as a growing cyber risk to the financial system.
**Why it matters:** Until now, worries about circular AI financing (chipmakers investing in customers who buy their chips) came mostly from short-sellers and commentators. Now a G7 central bank has put a number on it in an official stability report. Expect other regulators to follow, and expect more pressure for disclosure on hyperscaler leases and offtake deals.
🔗 [StockAnalysis summary](https://stockanalysis.com/news/boe-financial-stability-report-ai-november-2026/) · [The Intelligent](https://theintelligent.co.uk/technology/2026/bank-england-report-flags-opaque-ai-financing-systemic-risk)

### 4. OpenAI starts testing "Private Inference," where OpenAI can't read your prompts
Sam Altman announced Tuesday (Nov 25) that OpenAI is piloting **Private Inference** with a small group of business customers. Requests run on confidential-computing hardware, meaning isolated chips with controls outsiders can verify, so OpenAI staff can't see the content of prompts or responses. It's labeled alpha, with no price or availability date announced. It belongs to a broader "Private Intelligence" push alongside Zero Data Retention and Private Safety Processing, which checks for misuse without keeping data. Altman: *"Stronger privacy protections will make frontier AI useful for a lot more work."*
**Why it matters:** The biggest barrier to selling to banks, hospitals and law firms isn't how smart the model is. It's that customers have to trust the vendor with their data. Verifiable confidential compute turns "trust us" into "check us," which could open up the most regulated (and most profitable) enterprise workloads. Expect Anthropic and Google to respond with something similar.
🔗 [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/openai-private-inference-testing-customers) · [Primary: OpenAI Private Intelligence page](https://openai.com/business/private-intelligence)

### 5. Anthropic's stock slips back to its IPO price a week after a blockbuster debut
Anthropic (ANT) began trading Nov 19 at $111.50, about 35% above its **$83 IPO price**, and briefly carried a valuation above $2T. By Monday it had fallen to roughly $76–81. On Wednesday it erased an early 8% drop to close back near $83. Analysts point to the broader tech sell-off, worries about data center costs, and the **$900B+ in future commitments** disclosed in its prospectus. CNBC's Investing Club called it "a clunker post-IPO."
**Why it matters:** Public markets are now repricing AI's leading private-market stories in real time. How ANT trades will set the tone for the OpenAI and SpaceX listings and for late-stage private valuations (see Sierra below). It also connects directly to the BoE's warning about AI financing (story #3).
🔗 [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/investingclub/2026/11/25/anthropics-stock-has-been-a-clunker-post-ipo-why-that-matters-for-the-ai-trade.html) · [24/7 Wall St.](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/11/25/anthropics-sharp-reversal-up-4-ant-stock-reclaims-83-ipo-price-after-early-8-drop-coreweave-slides-2-iren-holds-steady/)

### 6. New York bans AI chatbot toys for five years
Gov. Kathy Hochul signed **S.9408A/A.11144B** on Nov 24. It bans making and selling internet-connected plush toys, dolls and robots with built-in chatbots for five years. Owning one is not banned. The Attorney General enforces the law, with fines of up to **$15,000 per day**. It takes effect in about 90 days (late February 2027), so this holiday season's inventory is unaffected. New York is the second state to act, after California's moratorium.
**Why it matters:** This is the clearest sign yet that state legislatures will regulate AI by product category ("no chatbots in kids' toys") instead of waiting for a broad AI framework. Any company building consumer AI hardware for children now faces a growing patchwork of state rules.
🔗 [Governor's office](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/consumers-begin-their-holiday-shopping-season-governor-hochul-signs-five-year-ban-ai-chatbot) · [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/new-york-governor-kathy-hochul-sign-ai-toy-ban-law-rcna610962)

## 🚀 STARTUP WORLD

### 1. Sierra raises $1.75B Series F at a $30B valuation, doubling in six months
Bret Taylor's customer-experience AI company announced a **$1.75B Series F** on Nov 23, led by **Greenoaks and Lightspeed**, at a **$30B valuation**. Its last round was $950M at ~$15B in May. Sierra says annualized revenue has passed **$400M**, which puts the valuation at about 75× run-rate. A big part of Sierra's pitch is outcome-based pricing: customers pay per resolved issue, not per seat. *(Slightly older than 48h, but the week's biggest startup round.)*
**Why it matters:** Sierra is the clearest example of "agents replacing a function" at scale. Doubling its valuation in six months, during a week when public AI stocks wobbled, shows how far private markets are still willing to pay ahead of revenue for category leaders.
🔗 [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/23/sierra-doubles-valuation-to-30b-within-six-months-as-demand-for-ai-in-cx-takes-off/) · [PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/startups/2026/ai-customer-support-startup-sierra-eyes-30-billion-valuation/)

### 2. 42 state AGs try to block Character.AI's bankruptcy auction of user chats
Character.AI is in bankruptcy and plans to auction its platform on **Dec 1**. The sale includes chat histories from more than **21 million U.S. accounts**, over 2.1 million of them belonging to minors, along with IP addresses and other metadata. On Nov 24 a bipartisan coalition of **42 state attorneys general** asked the bankruptcy court to reject the sale. They argue a buyer wouldn't be bound by Character.AI's privacy policy, which said it didn't sell personal data. They also point out that the only disclosed bidder is a Delaware entity formed on Nov 13, one day before it was named the stalking-horse bidder. The states want minors' chats and sensitive data removed from any sale.
**Why it matters:** This is the first major test of what happens to intimate AI-companion data when the company behind it fails. The outcome will set a precedent for every consumer AI startup's data, and it may push founders and investors to treat chat logs as a liability, not an asset.
🔗 [Notebookcheck](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Character-ai-plans-to-auction-chats-from-21-million-US-accounts.1460646.0.html) · [Illinois AG statement (via RiverBender)](https://www.riverbender.com/news/details/attorney-general-raoul-challenges-plan-to-auction-off-characterai-users-private-chats-to-the-highest-bidder-98619.cfm)

### 3. Every raises $17.5M Series A to test whether venture-backed media can work
Every, Dan Shipper's AI-focused publication and software studio, raised a **$17.5M Series A at a $110M post-money valuation**, led by **Jesse Beyroutey at IA Ventures**, with Betaworks and Nicholas Thompson among the participants. That's a big jump from the deliberately small $2M "sip seed" it used to describe. Every combines a subscription newsletter with in-house AI products, using its writing to distribute its software.
**Why it matters:** Media has mostly been off-limits for venture investors. Every's pitch is that in the AI era, an audience plus fast in-house product building is a real moat. If it works, expect more "media-led software companies."
🔗 [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/11/25/every-raises-17-5-million-110-million-valuation-betting-venture-backed-media-can-work/)

### 4. EU Inc. backers say member states have gutted Europe's "28th regime"
EU Inc. was meant to give startups a single, Delaware-style corporate form that works across the EU. Its founder backers now say member states have hollowed out the proposal so badly that "the startup reform no longer exists." The Council's changes include a 21-month implementation window instead of 12, tighter controls, and pressure to turn a uniform regulation into a directive each country writes into its own law. Founder groups, including Allied for Startups and the European Startup Network, warn the best founders will keep setting up U.S. holding companies.
**Why it matters:** European founders and their investors still deal with 27 sets of corporate law, employee stock option rules and funding paperwork. A weakened EU Inc. means the Delaware flip remains the default for ambitious European startups.
🔗 [Bloomberg (Nov 25 newsletter)](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-11-25/europe-s-answer-to-delaware-style-system-for-investment-hits-roadblock) · [TechRound](https://techround.co.uk/business/eu-inc-backers-say-the-startup-reform-no-longer-exists-what-has-been-taken-out/)

## 📖 FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: Ruth Handler (1916–2002)
*Co-founder of Mattel, creator of Barbie, and later founder of Nearly Me*

Timely, given that New York just banned AI chatbot toys. Ruth Handler is the founder who taught the toy industry how to sell straight to children, and she's one of the most dramatic rise-fall-redemption stories in American business.

**Early years.** Ruth Mosko was born in Denver in 1916, the youngest of ten children of Polish-Jewish immigrants. She was largely raised by an older sister, worked in her sister's drugstore as a teenager, and later said she preferred business to housework from the start. She married her high-school sweetheart Elliot Handler, and in 1938 they moved to Los Angeles, where she worked as a stenographer at Paramount Pictures.

**The garage company.** Elliot was a designer who made furniture from new plastics like Lucite. Ruth was the one who sold it. In 1945 they started a business in a garage with Harold "Matt" Matson, combining *Matt* and *Elliot* into **Mattel**. Matson left early, and the company moved from picture frames to dollhouse furniture made from the frame scraps, then to toys like the Uke-A-Doodle ukulele and a music box. Elliot invented and Ruth ran the business, a split that defined Mattel for 30 years.

**The TV gamble.** In 1955, Ruth committed about **$500,000**, then roughly the company's entire net worth, to a full year of sponsorship on Disney's new *Mickey Mouse Club*. Toy companies at the time advertised to parents, and only around Christmas. Mattel advertised **to kids, all year round**. The Burp Gun ad sold out its inventory, and the strategy became the toy industry's playbook for the next half-century.

**Barbie.** Ruth had watched her daughter Barbara play with paper dolls, giving them adult lives and careers instead of treating them as babies. Ruth believed girls wanted a three-dimensional grown-up doll; Mattel's mostly male executives thought mothers would never buy a doll with a woman's figure. On a 1956 trip to Switzerland she found **Bild Lilli**, a German novelty doll, brought several home, and pushed the redesign through. **Barbie** (named after Barbara) debuted at the New York Toy Fair on **March 9, 1959**. Buyers were skeptical, but kids weren't: roughly **350,000** sold in the first year. Her business insight was as important as the doll. The doll itself was cheap, and the money came from the endless stream of outfits, accessories and friends sold separately. **Ken** (named after son Kenneth) followed in 1961. Mattel went public in 1960 and reached the Fortune 500 within a few years.

**The fall.** In 1970 Ruth was diagnosed with breast cancer and had a mastectomy. During those same years Mattel's growth slowed after a series of acquisitions. An **SEC investigation** found falsified financial reports, and the Handlers were pushed out of the company they had founded by the mid-1970s. In **1978** Ruth was indicted on fraud and false-reporting charges. She pleaded no contest and was sentenced to a fine and **2,500 hours of community service**. She always maintained she hadn't known about the wrongdoing.

**The second act.** Unhappy with the crude prostheses available after her mastectomy, she started over in her 60s. In 1976 she founded **Ruthton Corp.**, which made **"Nearly Me,"** a realistic, size-matched breast prosthesis. She went to department stores and fitted customers herself, and talked about breast cancer openly at a time when few public figures did. Betty Ford was among her customers. She later sold the company and died in Los Angeles in **2002**, at 85.

**Lessons for founders:**
- **Watch the user, not the experts.** Barbie came from watching a child play, against the advice of everyone in the room.
- **Distribution can be the innovation.** The Mickey Mouse Club bet changed how an entire industry reached its customers.
- **The razor-and-blades model works in any category.** Barbie was a platform, and the outfits sold separately were the business.
- **Governance failures can undo a founder.** The same aggressiveness that built Mattel left it without the controls that would have prevented the fraud.
- **A second act can come from your own problem.** Nearly Me began with Handler's own frustration as a patient.
