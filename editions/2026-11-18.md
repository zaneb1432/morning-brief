## 4. FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY — Tope Awotona (b. ~1981), Calendly

**Three failed startups, one drained 401(k), and a link that sells itself.**

Tope Awotona was born in Lagos, Nigeria, the son of a microbiologist father and a mother who ran a pharmaceutical business. When he was **12**, his father was shot and killed in front of him during a carjacking. The family left Nigeria a few years later and settled in the Atlanta area when Tope was about 15. He has said the move made him determined to build something that would justify his mother's sacrifice.

At the **University of Georgia** he started out in computer science and then switched to management information systems. He has said he left programming partly because he didn't think he was good enough at it, which matters later in the story. After graduating he became a salesman, and a good one, selling software and hardware at IBM, Dell, EMC and Vocus. Sales gave him two things he would use later: he could sense what a busy buyer would actually pay for, and he spent years going back and forth over email trying to book meetings.

He was also trying to start companies on the side, and they kept failing. One was a **dating site** that never launched. Another was an e-commerce store selling **projectors**. A third sold **grills**. Awotona later admitted he didn't care about any of these products. He had chosen markets because they looked like they could make money, not because he understood a problem. Each one cost him savings and confidence.

Calendly came out of one of those scheduling frustrations. In 2012, trying to set up a meeting, he went through the usual chain of "Does Tuesday at 3 work? No? How about Thursday?" emails. He looked at the scheduling tools that existed and found them clunky and built for the person *receiving* the invite rather than the one sending it. This time he had a problem he had lived with every working day for a decade.

He went all in. In 2013 he took **his life savings and his 401(k)**, about $200,000 by most accounts, maxed out his credit cards, quit his job at EMC and set up at **Atlanta Tech Village**. He still couldn't code, so he hired an outside development shop to build the first version. That meant every dollar was going out the door with no product yet, at a time when Atlanta was not considered a place to build software companies.

The product was simple. You share a link, the other person picks a time from your real availability, and the meeting is booked. The **business model** was the clever part. Every Calendly link a user sent was a demo of the product for the recipient. Recipients saw how easy it was, signed up, and started sending their own links. This is now the textbook case of **product-led growth**: users did the selling, so Calendly spent very little on marketing for years.

That allowed something unusual for a software company: Awotona **mostly bootstrapped it**. Calendly raised only about **$550,000** in its first seven years, from Atlanta Ventures (David Cummings, who also founded Atlanta Tech Village) and OpenView, over two small rounds in 2014 and 2017. It was **profitable by around 2016** and kept growing for years without venture money. By late 2020 it had about 10 million users. In January 2021 it took its first large check, **$350 million from OpenView and Iconiq at a $3 billion valuation**. Awotona still owned a large majority of the company, and Forbes put him among the richest immigrants in America. He was one of very few Black founders to have built a billion-dollar software company largely on his own terms.

The years since have been harder. The pandemic boom in video meetings faded, and scheduling became a feature that Microsoft, Google, HubSpot and Zoom all bundled into their products. Calendly made two rounds of layoffs, about 60 roles in 2023 and about 70 in a later round. It moved upmarket into enterprise sales teams, routing and recruiting workflows, and brought in a full C-suite of big-company executives. Awotona has remained CEO, and he now spends much of his time on the problem he used to have as a salesman: getting enterprises to buy.

**Lessons for founders:**
1. **Domain beats ambition.** Three businesses chosen because they looked like money failed. The one chosen because he had personally lived with the problem for years succeeded.
2. **You don't have to be technical. You do have to understand the problem.** He couldn't code, and he outsourced the build. What he knew better than almost anyone was the person who sends a meeting invite.
3. **Build the distribution into the product.** Every Calendly link spreads Calendly. If using the product shows it to new people, you may not need a sales team, or much venture capital.
4. **Bootstrapping gives you leverage on the terms.** Because Calendly took so little money early, Awotona negotiated a $3B round from a position of strength, as a profitable company, and kept control.
