**[Nvidia](https://www.nvidia.com)'s board reinstates Jensen Huang as CEO, and the stock jumps 31% in one day (Nov 23).** Twelve days after Huang **resigned on Nov 11**, Nvidia's board voted **unanimously** to reinstate him as **president, CEO and board director**, effective immediately. In its release, the board said it was "satisfied with his candor and ability to lead" and that Huang had been **"the target of a foreign intelligence effort."** It also said it took **input from senior levels of the U.S. government** and concluded his return poses **no national-security risk**. The **Nasdaq rose about 6.5%**, the **S&P 500 4.7%** and the **Dow 3.0%**, which recovers just over half of last week's rout. **Why it matters:** The market valued Huang as a person far above anything a governance process could assure it of. Most of last week's decline came from the leadership vacuum, and the governance overhang hasn't gone away. Still open: the internal investigation, the **California AG subpoena**, the **reported SEC accounting review**, and bipartisan pressure from Congress, which CNN reports is already criticizing the reinstatement. Rehiring a CEO while investigators are still looking into his conduct is very unusual, so expect proxy advisers and Senate China hawks to keep pressing. For the AI trade, the leading risk factor today is political rather than financial. **[OpenAI](https://openai.com)'s chief scientist calls for industry-wide limits on AI development, and Altman backs it (Nov 22).** **Jakub Pachocki** published a proposal asking frontier developers to agree on **capability thresholds** that none of them would cross until **new monitoring and safety mechanisms** are in place. It also arrives as Washington and Beijing are working on their own AI-risk channel (see quick hits). Skeptics will point out that thresholds tied to a metric OpenAI helps define could favor whoever is ahead. The test is whether Google DeepMind, Anthropic, Meta and xAI sign on to anything specific. ## 4. FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY — Yvon Chouinard (b. 1938), Chouinard Equipment & [Patagonia](https://www.patagonia.com)

**The blacksmith who sold climbing pitons out of his car, built a billion-dollar apparel company he never quite wanted, ran an ad telling customers not to buy his jacket, and then gave the whole company away.**

*The 2022 ownership transfer and the early forge story are confirmed by search results (Patagonia's company history, Axios, Fortune, National Geographic, Wikipedia). Other details come from Chouinard's memoir* Let My People Go Surfing *(2005) and standard profiles, and I couldn't check each one this run. Those are marked "per his memoir" or "reported." Yesterday's founder (John H. Johnson) was a lesser-known media pioneer, so today's is a well-known name, chosen because Black Friday is this week.*

**Origins.** Chouinard was born in **1938 in Lisbon, Maine**, to a French-Canadian family, and moved to Southern California as a child. He came to climbing through a teenage falconry club: the members rappelled down cliffs to reach hawk nests. Climbing gear at the time was mostly soft-iron European pitons that were left in the rock after one use. In **1957**, according to Patagonia's own company history, he went to a junkyard and bought a **used coal-fired forge, a 138-pound anvil, tongs and hammers**, and taught himself blacksmithing. His hard **chrome-molybdenum steel pitons** could be hammered in and pulled out hundreds of times. They were proven on the big walls of **Yosemite** during its golden age.

**The dirtbag business.** For years the business consisted of Chouinard forging in a shed and selling gear **from the trunk of his car** between climbs, reportedly at about **$1.50 a piton**, and living on very little in between. In **1965** he formed **Chouinard Equipment** with climber **Tom Frost**, and a shop in **Ventura, California** became the center of American climbing hardware. By about 1970 it was reportedly the largest climbing-hardware supplier in the U.S.

**The first pivot: destroying his best product.** Around the early 1970s, Chouinard and Frost saw that their own steel pitons were **scarring the cracks of Yosemite** from repeated hammering. Their **1972 catalog** led with an essay arguing for **"clean climbing"** with removable aluminum **chocks and hexes**, which wedge into cracks without hammering. Pitons were most of their business, and they talked their customers out of buying them anyway. Chock sales rose quickly *(per his memoir and climbing histories)*. It's the first instance of what became his signature move: **let the environmental case cut into the product line, and trust customers to come along.**

**Clothing by accident.** A **rugby shirt** brought back from Scotland around 1970 sold well to climbers, who liked the tough collar under gear slings. Soft goods made better margins than hardware, so a clothing line was spun off and named **Patagonia** in **1973**, with the first store in Ventura. Through the 1970s and '80s it became known for **synthetic pile** and later **Synchilla fleece** and bright colors on the trail. In **1989**, product-liability lawsuits pushed **Chouinard Equipment into bankruptcy**. Employees led by **Peter Metcalf** bought its assets and founded **[Black Diamond Equipment](https://www.blackdiamondequipment.com)** *(widely reported; not confirmed in this run's searches)*.

**The near-death lesson.** In the **early-1990s recession**, Patagonia had grown too fast on the assumption that growth would continue, and it ran short of cash. Chouinard has described laying off about **a fifth of the staff** in **1991** as the worst day of his business life. He concluded that a company should **grow only as fast as it can sustain**, and he reorganized Patagonia around a written mission that was later shortened to "**We're in business to save our home planet.**"

**Doing things the "wrong" way on purpose.** Among the practices that set Patagonia apart:
- **On-site child care** at headquarters from the early 1980s, years before it was common.
- A self-imposed **environmental tax**: first a share of profits, then **1% of sales** regardless of profit. That turned into **[1% for the Planet](https://www.onepercentfortheplanet.org)** (co-founded in **2002**), which now has thousands of member companies.
- In **1996**, a switch of all cotton to **organic** after learning about the pesticide load of conventional cotton. It raised costs and forced Patagonia to help build a supply chain from scratch.
- On **Black Friday 2011**, a full-page *New York Times* ad showing a Patagonia fleece over the words **"DON'T BUY THIS JACKET,"** urging customers to buy less and repair what they had. Sales reportedly *rose* afterward, which critics pointed out and Chouinard acknowledged.
- **"Let My People Go Surfing"**: flexible hours so employees could leave when the waves were good, on the theory that people who own their time do better work.

**Giving it away.** In **September 2022**, at 83, Chouinard announced that his family had transferred ownership of Patagonia, then valued at about **$3 billion**. **100% of the voting stock (about 2% of equity)** went to the **Patagonia Purpose Trust**, which the family guides, and **all non-voting stock (about 98%)** went to the **Holdfast Collective** ([Patagonia ownership page](https://www.patagonia.com/ownership)), a 501(c)(4) nonprofit that receives Patagonia's profits not reinvested in the business, projected at around **$100M a year**, to spend on climate and conservation. The family reportedly paid roughly **$17.5M in gift tax** on the trust shares. Because Holdfast is a 501(c)(4), it can fund political work, and critics noted that the structure also avoided the large capital-gains bill a sale would have triggered. His summary: **"Earth is now our only shareholder."** He had considered selling or going public and rejected both, because either would mean losing control of the mission.

**Why he matters to a founder today.**
1. **Willingness to undercut your own revenue for a principle builds lasting trust.** The clean-climbing reversal and the "Don't Buy This Jacket" ad each risked the core business, and each made the brand stronger.
2. **Grow at the pace your balance sheet allows.** The 1991 layoffs led to a philosophy of deliberate, sustainable growth, a useful counterpoint in a week when a seed round can be $277M.
3. **Ownership structure is a product decision.** Chouinard didn't just make a donation. He built a legal structure, a purpose trust plus a nonprofit, that keeps the company private and on mission after he's gone. As AI labs argue over public-benefit charters, nonprofit control and IPOs, Patagonia is one of the few examples where the mission and the cap table actually line up.
4. **Be a reluctant businessman, but take the business seriously.** Chouinard has always called himself a climber and surfer first. He also built a company with **$1B+ in sales**, which gave him enough leverage to change an industry.
