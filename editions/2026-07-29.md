## 1. Tech & AI News

**Moonshot AI ships Kimi K3, likely the largest open-weight model ever released.** The Beijing-based lab released full weights for Kimi K3 on July 27 — a roughly 2.8-trillion-parameter mixture-of-experts model with native visual understanding, a 1-million-token context window, and an always-on "thinking mode." Moonshot says it lands close behind Anthropic's and OpenAI's frontier systems on reasoning and knowledge benchmarks while actually beating many of them on coding tasks. The release landed hard: it hit #1 on Hugging Face's trending chart within 30 minutes, racked up thousands of downloads within a day, and Moonshot says its daily revenue has grown at least sixfold since launch, with ARR climbing from $200M in April to $300M in June. It's the clearest signal yet that the gap between Chinese open-weight labs and the closed US frontier is narrowing fast, and it's rattling Silicon Valley's assumption that frontier capability requires a closed model. [Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/91581267/what-to-know-about-moonshot-ai-and-its-new-open-weight-model-kimi-k3) · [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/chinas-moonshot-ai-releases-kimi-k3-the-largest-open-source-model-ever-rivaling-top-u-s-systems) · [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-27/china-s-moonshot-to-release-breakthrough-ai-model-for-download)

**Nvidia launches the Open Secure AI Alliance, but OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic are notably absent.** On July 27, Nvidia and 52 founding partners — including Microsoft, IBM, Dell, Red Hat, CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, Cloudflare, Hugging Face, Databricks, GitHub, Salesforce, SAP, and the Linux Foundation — launched a coalition to build and share open-source tools for AI cybersecurity, including Nvidia's own agent-auditing framework, NOOA. The timing is pointed: Nvidia's announcement explicitly cites the July 9–13 intrusion into OpenAI's infrastructure, during which Hugging Face's security team says closed AI tools "unable to distinguish attackers from defenders" blocked forensic analysis, forcing Hugging Face to fall back on an open-weight model (GLM 5.2) to trace roughly 17,000 attacker actions and contain the breach. That the three largest closed-model labs sat out a security alliance built partly around their own incident is a notable industry rift. [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/timkeary/2026/07/28/nvidia-alliance-backs-open-source-ai-after-hugging-face-breach/) · [The Hill](https://thehill.com/policy/technology/5991875-nvidia-launches-open-secure-ai-alliance/) · [BetaNews](https://betanews.com/article/nvidia-open-secure-ai-alliance/)

**MCP gets its biggest overhaul since launch, dropping stateful sessions entirely.** The Agentic AI Foundation (an AAIF/Linux Foundation directed fund) shipped the 2026-07-28 Model Context Protocol spec, which finalizes a move to a fully stateless architecture — the old initialize/initialized handshake is gone, replaced by required headers on every request — hardens authentication against OAuth "mixup" attacks via a new issuer-validation requirement, and formalizes a 12-month deprecation policy with a public registry for tracking sunsetting features. It also graduates server-rendered UI and long-running async tasks from experimental extensions into the official spec. For anyone building on MCP, this is a real migration, not a point release — but it's also a sign the protocol underpinning most AI-agent tool use is maturing into something enterprises can build production security policy around. [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/infrastructure/mcp-just-got-its-biggest-update-ever-heres-what-changes-for-ai-agents) · [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/07/29/mcp-gets-an-enterprise-makeover/5280027) · [SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/07/28/biggest-ever-mcp-update-brings-metadata-cybersecurity-enhancements/)

**The EU AI Act's August 2 deadline is now days away, and enforcement is shifting from writing rules to testing systems.** High-risk-system and transparency obligations under the Act become binding for most already-deployed systems on August 2, and the European Commission has rolled out a Cybersecurity Action Plan that moves the bloc's posture from drafting regulation to actively red-teaming AI within secure pan-European testing environments. It's arriving alongside a broader global hardening: China began enforcing companion-AI rules on July 15, and the UK's AI Regulation and Safety Bill passed its second reading. Together they mark a shift from voluntary AI-ethics language to enforceable legal duties across all three major regulatory blocs at roughly the same moment. [Lumenova AI](https://www.lumenova.ai/blog/eu-ai-act-delays-july-2026/) · [Cubbbix](https://cubbbix.com/blog/ai-regulation-july-2026-global-update/)

**Anduril rolls its first autonomous fighter jet off the line just four months after the factory opened.** The YFQ-44A semi-autonomous fighter came off Anduril's Arsenal-1 production line in Pickaway County, Ohio — a facility designed for 150 aircraft a year that only opened in March. It's a fast validation of Anduril's bet that autonomous defense hardware can be manufactured at genuine scale rather than built as one-off prototypes, and another marker of how quickly AI-driven autonomy is moving from software demos into physical, mass-produced systems.

## 2. Startup World

**Act Security emerges from stealth with $60M to fight "access sprawl" before AI attackers can exploit it.** The Tel Aviv-based company launched July 28 with a $20M seed (Team8, Bessemer Venture Partners, Hetz Ventures, Claltech) and a $40M Series A (Notable Capital, Startpoint Capital, SVCI). Its pitch: traditional patch-and-posture management is losing ground because cloud environments have accumulated more access paths than humans can remediate, and AI-driven attackers can now exploit that sprawl faster than defenders can respond — so Act is building an "action-centric" platform instead. The founders previously built Medigate, acquired by Claroty for $400M, giving the raise real credibility with cybersecurity investors. [SecurityWeek](https://www.securityweek.com/act-security-emerges-from-stealth-to-fight-the-patch-problem/) · [Axios Pro](https://www.axios.com/pro/enterprise-software-deals/2026/07/28/act-security-cloud-bessemer)

**Hush Security raises a $30M Series A to govern AI agents as a new class of "non-human identity."** Announced July 28, the round targets a problem enterprises are only now grappling with seriously: AI agents now hold credentials, take actions, and access systems the way employees do, but most identity-and-access infrastructure was never built to authenticate, scope, or audit non-human actors at this scale. It's part of a broader wave of agent-governance startups emerging as companies push more autonomous AI into production.

**Businessnext raises $40M Series C from ServiceNow at a $700M valuation for autonomous banking software.** The strategic investment (rather than a pure financial round) signals ServiceNow is betting directly on AI-driven back-office automation for financial institutions, and it's a reminder that large enterprise software incumbents are increasingly writing checks into the AI-agent startups they'll eventually want to integrate with or acquire.

**Claros closes a $55M Series B to commercialize PFAS destruction technology.** The round pushes "forever chemicals" remediation — long a research-stage problem — toward broader deployment, part of what Crunchbase and other trackers describe as investors funding "ambitious science" this year only when there's a credible path to real-world deployment and revenue, not just a lab result. [TechStartups roundup](https://techstartups.com/2026/07/28/venture-capital-startup-funding-roundup-july-28-2026-battery-ventures-bessemer-gradient-team8-y-combinator-more/)

**Poppy shuts down after an orderly wind-down, a reminder that not every ending is a flameout.** Founder Avni Patel Thompson closed the childcare-marketplace startup after reviewing scalability, unit economics, runway, and staffing, and concluded a deliberate shutdown was the right call rather than forcing a pivot or a fire sale. It's a useful counterpoint to this year's more dramatic collapse stories — sometimes the founder-responsible move is simply to stop.

## 4. Founder Biography — An Wang

Long before "AI infrastructure" was a venture category, there was An Wang — a Shanghai-born engineer whose invention quietly underpins nearly all modern computing, and whose own company's collapse remains one of the sharpest cautionary tales in tech about succession and denial.

Wang was born in Shanghai in 1920, the oldest of five children, taught English at home by his father and drawn early to science and math. He graduated from Shanghai Jiao Tong University with a degree in electrical engineering in 1940 — into a country at war. When Japan occupied Shanghai, Wang fled to Guilin, where he led a small team scavenging spare parts to build radios for the Chinese military, improvising hardware out of whatever they could find. That scrappy, parts-scavenging instinct would define his whole career.

In 1945 he came to the US to study at Harvard, earning a physics PhD in 1948 and staying on to work on Howard Aiken's Mark IV computer project. There, working with classmate Wo Weidu, Wang invented a pulse-transfer control device enabling "read-after-write" magnetic memory — the technical breakthrough that made magnetic core memory practical. Core memory became the dominant form of computer RAM for roughly two decades, from the mid-1950s through the mid-1970s, and Wang's invention sat underneath it. In 1955, IBM bought the patent rights outright for $500,000 — a fortune at the time, and the seed capital Wang used to build the company that would spend the next three decades competing directly with the buyer of his own invention.

Wang Laboratories, founded in 1951, started with calculators and pivoted hard into word processors and office computing, going public in 1970 and growing from roughly $1M in value into a company with $2.5B+ in annual revenue by the height of the 1980s — one of the era's genuine American tech giants, employing tens of thousands of people.

Then it collapsed, fast and almost entirely self-inflicted. Wang badly misjudged the shift to the IBM PC and open architectures, staying too committed to Wang's own proprietary systems while the market moved to interoperable standards. Compounding the technology miscall, he handed the presidency to his son Fred Wang in 1986 — reportedly over the objections of experienced executives — despite Fred's inexperience running an organization at that scale. Fred announced a slate of 14 ambitious new products that, as customers gradually discovered, largely didn't exist yet. Customers defected to IBM in droves. An Wang, dying of cancer, replaced his son as CEO in 1989 in a last attempt to save the company, but it was too late: Wang Laboratories filed for bankruptcy in 1992, two years after An Wang's death in 1990.

He left behind more than 35 patents and a company that no longer exists — but also a template that still resonates in 2026's AI infrastructure race: the founder who invents the foundational technology, gets outcompeted by the very giant he sold it to, and whose empire's undoing is ultimately a story about people and succession as much as technology.
