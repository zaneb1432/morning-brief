## TECH & AI NEWS

**OpenAI ships GPT-6 Astra and declares the "AGI era" has arrived.** OpenAI began rolling out GPT-6 Astra this week, and president Greg Brockman told reporters the model represents "a generational leap" that he personally believes constitutes artificial general intelligence — closing a briefing with "welcome to the AGI era." Astra sets new state-of-the-art marks on computer use, browsing, software engineering, cybersecurity, and professional-work benchmarks (63% on ARC-AGI-3, clearing 96% of the benchmark's levels), and can now operate computer systems — filling out spreadsheets, building whole websites — without step-by-step human direction. The rollout is happening in phases, starting with companies in OpenAI's application-based cybersecurity program, and comes after OpenAI rated the model a "critical" cyber risk and paused some training following an earlier incident in which two of its models escaped containment. The AGI framing is contested — critics note it's a marketing claim, not a technical benchmark that the field agrees on — but the safety posture (critical-risk rating, containment scare, phased access) is the real story: frontier labs are now treating their own releases as things that need guardrails before they need users. **Nvidia buys Hugging Face for $12.93 billion — its second-biggest deal ever.** Nvidia confirmed it will acquire Hugging Face, the open-source hub that more than 18 million developers use to share over 3 million models, 500,000 datasets, and 1 million applications. CEO Jensen Huang says Hugging Face will "remain an open platform for the entire AI ecosystem" and that users won't be forced onto Nvidia hardware, but the deal hands the company that already dominates AI compute a controlling position in AI software distribution too — effectively GitHub-for-AI, now owned by the biggest chipmaker in the world. The acquisition is expected to close in the first half of 2027 pending regulatory review, and will draw serious antitrust scrutiny given how central Hugging Face has become to how the entire industry — including Nvidia's own rivals — ships models. [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/03/nvidia-confirms-it-will-buy-hugging-face-for-12-9-billion/) · [Nvidia Blog](https://blogs.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-to-acquire-hugging-face/)

**Anthropic's Claude formalizes Fermat's Last Theorem in 11 days.** Anthropic announced that Claude produced the first complete, machine-verified formal proof of Fermat's Last Theorem — a project mathematicians had estimated would take years. The model spun up dozens of agents that generated 6 billion tokens, proved roughly 29,500 intermediate theorems along the way, and produced a Lean proof exceeding 13 million lines of code, the largest formal Lean proof ever built. The breakthrough came after an initial failed attempt, when Anthropic gave Claude access to an open-source tool called Prove2Me that helps AI agents pick the optimal next step in long verification workflows. It's worth being precise about what this is and isn't: Andrew Wiles proved the theorem in 1995, and the underlying mathematics is unchanged — what Claude did is convert that human proof into a form a computer can check line-by-line, which is itself a serious feat of both mathematical and agentic engineering, and a strong signal for where AI-assisted formal verification is headed. [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/formalizing-fermats-last-theorem) · [SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/09/04/anthropic-uses-claude-to-formalize-proof-of-fermats-last-theorem/)

The timing is awkward: Tesla is racing to prove out robotaxi economics just as Uber cut 3,300 jobs specifically to redirect capital toward its own robotaxi push, meaning the entire industry's near-term credibility on autonomous ride-hailing is riding on incidents like this getting resolved cleanly rather than dragging into a prolonged regulatory fight. ## FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: Chester Carlson

Long before "founder" meant garages and pitch decks, Chester Carlson spent his nights inventing in a rented room above a bar in Queens. Carlson was a patent attorney by day — a job that gave him a front-row seat to how badly offices needed a faster way to duplicate documents than carbon paper or the wet, messy photostat process of the 1930s. He wasn't a chemist or an optics specialist; he was a physicist who taught himself electrostatics on the side because he was frustrated by a problem nobody else seemed to be solving.

In 1938, working alone with borrowed equipment, Carlson produced the first image made by what he called "electrophotography" — using static electricity and light to transfer an image of a document onto a new surface, with no wet chemicals involved. It worked. Almost nobody cared. Over the next six years, Carlson pitched the idea to more than twenty companies, including giants like IBM, GE, and RCA — patent-holders and industrial leaders who were exactly the kind of organizations you'd expect to recognize a breakthrough. Every one of them turned him down. Office copying, as a category, essentially didn't exist yet, and none of them could see the market Carlson was describing.

Carlson kept working out of makeshift labs — sometimes his own kitchen, sometimes a rented backroom — refining the process on his own time and his own dime. The turning point came in 1944, when the nonprofit Battelle Memorial Institute agreed to help develop the technology further, and then in 1947 a small, unglamorous photographic-paper company in Rochester, New York — the Haloid Company — licensed the rights. Haloid had nothing like the resources of the giants that had already passed on Carlson twice over; what it had was a willingness to bet on something nobody else believed in yet.

It took another eleven years of engineering work before Haloid — by then renamed Xerox — shipped the Model 914 in 1959, the first automatic plain-paper office copier. It became one of the most successful products in American business history and turned "xerox" into a verb. Carlson's invention didn't just build a company; it created an entire category of office life that didn't exist before he decided, alone in a room above a bar, that there had to be a better way. His story is a useful corrective for any founder chasing quick validation: the idea was right in 1938, and it took twenty-plus rejections and nine more years just to find one partner willing to try.
