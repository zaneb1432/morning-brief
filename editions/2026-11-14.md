**OpenAI launches ChatGPT Wallet so its agents can check out for you (Nov 13).** OpenAI's agents, called "dots," can now find products, fill a cart and pay. Before each purchase, the user sees the merchant, the items, the total and the card, then approves with Face ID or a fingerprint. The card is replaced by a payment token, so neither the agent nor the merchant sees the card number. The launch covers only US ChatGPT Pro subscribers aged 18 or older, with eligible Amex, Mastercard, Visa or some Chase cards. Fully delegated buying, where the agent purchases without approval each time, is promised for a later version. OpenAI also published a Commerce Partner Playbook for merchants and is working with fraud firms so agent purchases aren't flagged as fraud. **Why it matters:** This builds on Monday's "Businesses in ChatGPT" launch. OpenAI now handles discovery, merchant listings and payment for a purchase, which puts it up against Google, Amazon and Shopify in agent-driven shopping. OpenAI admits some sites may still block these purchases. **Four nonprofits sue to freeze frontier AI at today's capability level (filed Nov 12).** Public Citizen, Heat Initiative, the Irreplaceable Education Fund and the Alliance for Secure AI sued in San Francisco Superior Court. The defendants are Anthropic, Google, Meta, OpenAI (both the company and the OpenAI Foundation) and SpaceX's AI entities. The suit asks for an injunction barring each company from building or deploying anything more capable than its most advanced model as of the filing date. It brings a single claim under California's Unfair Competition Law, on a public-nuisance theory. Much of the evidence comes from the companies themselves: executives' public warnings about catastrophic risk, and reports of models escaping their test environments. No court has acted yet. **Why it matters:** This is the opposite of September's antitrust class action, which accused the same labs of illegally agreeing to *slow down*. The labs are now being sued in one case for coordinating restraint and in another for not restraining themselves. A broad injunction is unlikely, but discovery could force internal safety evaluations into the public record just as Anthropic goes public. ## 4. FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY — Strive Masiyiwa (b. 1961), Econet

**The engineer who sued his government for five years to get a phone licence, and wired a continent.**

Strive Masiyiwa was born in 1961 in Rhodesia, now Zimbabwe. When he was about seven his family left for Zambia, and he went to primary school in Kitwe, a copper-mining town where his mother ran small businesses. At 12 he was sent to Scotland to finish secondary school. He then studied electrical and electronic engineering at the University of Wales (now Cardiff University), graduating in 1983. He went home to a newly independent Zimbabwe and joined the state telephone monopoly, the Zimbabwe Posts and Telecommunications Corporation (ZPTC), where he rose quickly to principal engineer.

In 1988 he quit and started Retrofit Engineering, an electrical contracting business, with about $75. It did well: in 1990 he became Zimbabwe's youngest-ever Businessman of the Year. At ZPTC he had seen that mobile phones would let African countries skip the decades of copper-wire networks that the state had never managed to build. In 1993 he applied for a licence to run a private cellular network. The government refused. Mobile telephony, it argued, was the state's constitutional preserve.

What followed was one of the longest licensing fights in African business history. Masiyiwa went to court. He won in the High Court in January 1994 and lost on appeal in the Supreme Court that June. In 1995 he won again in the Supreme Court, which struck down the state monopoly as a violation of free expression. Then President Robert Mugabe's government imposed a moratorium on private cellular licences. Masiyiwa has said he refused bribe demands throughout, and he sold other assets to keep paying lawyers. The fight took him close to bankruptcy. The courts finally ruled for him in late 1997, and Econet Wireless switched on in 1998.

He then had to raise the money to build the network. Econet's 1998 listing on the Zimbabwe Stock Exchange was heavily oversubscribed, with ordinary Zimbabweans buying in. That made it, in effect, a crowdfunded telecom company. Political pressure didn't stop, and around 2000 Masiyiwa moved to South Africa, then London, and ran a growing pan-African group from outside the country. Over the next two decades the business grew into several parts. Liquid Telecom, later Liquid Intelligent Technologies, built what became Africa's largest independent fibre network, more than 100,000 km across over a dozen countries. Cassava Technologies grew into a data-center and cloud business. In 2025 it announced plans for an "AI factory" in Africa using Nvidia GPUs. EcoCash, launched in 2011, became Zimbabwe's dominant mobile-money platform. During cash shortages it handled most of the country's payments, and that made it a target for regulators and politicians, along with the court fights that followed.

Masiyiwa's money has also gone into philanthropy. In 1996 he and his wife Tsitsi started what became the Higherlife Foundation, which has paid school fees and supported education for more than 250,000 children, many of them orphaned by HIV/AIDS. During COVID-19 he served as the African Union's special envoy and set up the Africa Medical Supplies Platform to buy vaccines and protective equipment for the continent. In 2020 he became the first African on Netflix's board. He has also sat on the boards of Unilever, the National Geographic Society and the Gates Foundation. He is widely reported as Zimbabwe's first billionaire.

**Lessons for founders:**
- **The regulator can be the moat.** The five-year fight nearly broke him, but winning it gave Econet a protected position that competitors who arrived later could not easily copy.
- **Customers as shareholders.** Econet's crowd-backed IPO gave the company a base of ordinary citizens who had a stake in its survival. That counted for something in a hostile political climate.
- **Build the layer underneath.** Masiyiwa started in mobile, then moved into fibre, data centers, payments and now AI compute. Each step went one layer further down the stack, so the next company in the market would have to rely on his network.
- **Integrity can be a strategy.** Refusing to pay bribes cost him years, but it gave him a public reputation that later helped him raise capital.
