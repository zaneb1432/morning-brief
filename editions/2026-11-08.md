## 4. FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY — Madam C.J. Walker (1867–1919)

*(Written from background knowledge, not this session's searches. This is the first edition on file, so there are no prior founders to avoid.)*

Sarah Breedlove was born on a Louisiana cotton plantation in 1867, the first child in her family born free after emancipation. Orphaned at seven, she married at 14, was widowed by 20 with a young daughter, A'Lelia, and worked as a washerwoman in St. Louis for about $1.50 a day. In the 1890s she developed severe scalp problems and hair loss, common among women who lacked plumbing and used harsh products.

She worked briefly as a sales agent for Annie Turnbo Malone's Poro hair-care company, then moved to Denver in 1905, where she developed her own hair-growing formula and treatment system. She remarried Charles J. Walker, a newspaper advertising man, and took the name Madam C.J. Walker, a brand identity she built deliberately.

Her insight was distribution. Rather than rely on retail, she trained Black women as commissioned "beauty culturists," selling door to door and offering a path out of domestic labor. She founded Lelia College to train agents, organized the National Negro Business League's women's contingent, and built a factory and headquarters in Indianapolis. By the 1910s she employed thousands of agents, and she is widely cited as one of the first self-made female millionaires in America, though historians debate the exact figure.

She spent heavily on philanthropy: the NAACP's anti-lynching campaign, Black schools and the YMCA. She also challenged Booker T. Washington's reluctance to acknowledge her at the 1912 Business League convention by standing and speaking. She died in 1919 at 51. The lesson for founders: build the sales channel and the brand together, and turn customers into an economic network.

Sources: [National Women's History Museum](https://www.womenshistory.org/education-resources/biographies/madam-cj-walker) · [Biography.com](https://www.biography.com/inventors/madam-cj-walker)
