## 1. TECH & AI NEWS

**California's attorney general subpoenas Nvidia over China exposure and investor disclosures (Nov 19, coverage continuing).** **Rob Bonta's** office served Nvidia an **investigative subpoena** covering two questions: whether **advanced chips or confidential information reached China**, and whether Nvidia **misled investors or the public** about those risks. According to Reuters-syndicated coverage, Bonta tied the probe to reports about former CEO **Jensen Huang's** alleged relationship with a woman U.S. intelligence officials accuse of spying for China. Politico reports the investor-disclosure angle. No charges have been filed, Nvidia has denied wrongdoing, and Newsquawk notes that a subpoena is a procedural step, not a complaint. This adds to two federal actions: the reported **SEC enforcement review of Nvidia's accounting** (covered yesterday) and a Nov 12 letter from Senators **Warren and Coons** asking the administration to **preserve Nvidia export records**. **Why it matters:** Nvidia now faces state, SEC and congressional scrutiny at once, all on the same theme: was the China business what investors were told? A state AG can't set export policy, but California's consumer- and investor-protection laws give it subpoena power over a California company, and the discovery process can bring out documents that federal agencies then use. For anyone pricing the AI-infrastructure trade, the key risk has moved from demand to governance. **Riemann follow-up: a 17-million-line Lean proof, a 250,000-page explanation that hasn't been released, and leading number theorists split (Nov 21–22).** Further coverage of **Levent Alpöge's** claim that he and Claude proved the Riemann hypothesis (lead story yesterday) adds scale and sharper reactions. The formal proof is reportedly about **17 million lines of Lean**. Alpöge says an AI-generated natural-language explanation runs to **over 250,000 pages** and has **not been released**. **Andrew Granville** (Montréal) told Nature that "what he released is **garbled**" and that Alpöge should have prepared a clearer write-up. Granville also linked the timing to Anthropic's stock slide, which Alpöge rejects: "There is an obvious cynical view, to do with the timing, but it is false." Fields medalist **James Maynard** says he **suspects the proof will turn out to be correct**. Sources differ on the formal check: one explainer and Wikipedia say the **Lean/Mathlib community has accepted** the formalization, while Tom's Hardware and IBM describe independent verification as **still pending**. The **Clay Institute still lists the problem as open.** *Science* asks the central question in its headline: "Can humans understand the answer?" **Why it matters:** The dispute is no longer mainly about whether the proof is correct. A Lean check is strong evidence of that. It's about what counts as mathematical knowledge when a proof is too large for any person to read. If Maynard is right, the field gets its biggest result in a century without anyone yet understanding *why* it's true. Expect pressure on Anthropic to release the long-form explanation, and an effort by human mathematicians to extract the key ideas from the Lean code. ## 2. STARTUP WORLD

**Marlowe, six months old, raises a $277M seed round at a $1.15B valuation to get more out of non-Nvidia chips (Bloomberg, Nov 23).** The startup was co-founded by **two Microsoft vice presidents** (Bloomberg's headline; I couldn't confirm their names). **Accel** led, with **Bessemer, Altimeter, Atreides, Valor Ventures and Neo**. Marlowe's goal is to extract more computing power from processors that compete with Nvidia's, and Crypto Briefing reports it plans to build infrastructure on **AMD accelerators** *(single source)*. It's better described as an AI-compute company than a chip designer. **Why it matters:** Investors are making a large, early bet *against* Nvidia's dominance the same week the Nvidia story turned into a governance story. A $277M seed is essentially a Series B-sized round given to a team with hyperscaler experience. The bet is on the software that makes AMD and custom silicon practical for large training and inference workloads, the same reason Amazon wants Decart for Trainium. If the software gap closes, Nvidia's pricing power weakens faster than its unit sales do. **Humyn Labs raises $47M to film people doing everyday tasks, as training data for U.S. robotics companies (Nov 18).** The Bengaluru company was founded in **February 2026**. The round is **$26M in equity led by Accel** (with Prosus Ventures and Bajaj-linked funds reported) plus **$21M in venture debt** from Lighthouse Canton. Humyn pays people to record real-world tasks (cooking, folding, sorting, assembly) and sells the footage to robotics and physical-AI developers in the U.S. It has **51 full-time staff**. That favors labor-cost arbitrage: Indian startups selling physical-world data to U.S. labs, much as Scale AI's early business relied on offshore labelers. The venture-debt component is unusual for a company this young and suggests the lenders see contracted revenue they're comfortable lending against. ## 4. FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY — John H. Johnson (1918–2005), Johnson Publishing Company (*Negro Digest*, *Ebony*, *Jet*)

**The insurance-company clerk who borrowed $500 against his mother's furniture and built the largest Black-owned publishing company in America.**

*Main facts were checked against search results (Britannica, Encyclopedia of Arkansas, Encyclopedia.com, the Johnson Publishing Company Wikipedia entry). Some details, flagged below, come from Johnson's 1989 memoir* Succeeding Against the Odds *and standard obituaries, and couldn't be verified this run. Yesterday's founder was in aviation; today's is in media, and he isn't well known outside business-history circles.*

John Harold Johnson was born in **1918** in **Arkansas City, Arkansas**, a Mississippi River town where the local school for Black children stopped at eighth grade. His father died in a sawmill accident when he was a child. His mother, **Gertrude**, a cook and washerwoman, saved for years to move the two of them to **Chicago** in **1933**, partly so he could attend high school. They arrived during the Depression and were on public relief for two years, which Johnson later said made him determined never to be poor again. At **DuSable High School** he edited the yearbook and school paper, and a speech he gave at an Urban League dinner caught the attention of **Harry Pace**, president of **Supreme Liberty Life Insurance**, the largest Black-owned business in the North. Pace hired him part-time and paid his way to the University of Chicago. Johnson dropped out in **1939** to work full time.

At Supreme Life, one of his jobs was to collect news clippings about Black Americans for Pace each week. Johnson realized that Black readers had no equivalent of *Reader's Digest*, a single magazine collecting the best writing about their own lives. Banks wouldn't lend to a 24-year-old Black man with an idea, so in **1942** he persuaded his mother to let him use her **new furniture as collateral for a $500 loan**. He used it to pay postage on a letter to Supreme Life's policyholders offering a **$2 charter subscription**. About **3,000** people subscribed in advance, and that **$6,000** in cash paid for the first issue of ***Negro Digest*** in **November 1942**. When newsstands wouldn't carry it, he reportedly had friends ask for it at Chicago newsstands, then bought back the copies himself so distributors would see demand. Circulation reached about **50,000** within a year. A 1943 issue in which **Eleanor Roosevelt** wrote the "If I Were a Negro" column helped push it toward 100,000 *(memoir detail)*.

The bigger idea came in **1945**. *Life* and *Look* were the dominant magazines of the time, and they almost never showed Black Americans except as criminals or servants. Johnson launched ***Ebony*** on **November 1, 1945**: a large-format photo magazine showing Black doctors, entertainers, soldiers, families and businesspeople. His wife, **Eunice Walker Johnson**, named it. The first press run of about 25,000 sold out *(memoir figure)*. The hard part was advertising. White-owned national brands didn't think Black consumers were worth reaching. In the story Johnson told for the rest of his life, he spent weeks trying to see **Commander Eugene McDonald** of **Zenith Radio**. Learning that McDonald was an Arctic explorer, Johnson found a book signed by **Matthew Henson**, the Black explorer who had reached the North Pole with Peary, and opened the meeting with it. He left with Zenith's account, and McDonald helped bring in others *(memoir detail)*. Johnson's long-term contribution was creating the Black consumer market as a category national advertisers recognized. He brought data, demographics and editorial quality to rooms that had refused to consider it.

In **1951** he launched ***Jet***, a pocket-size weekly of news and entertainment. In **1955**, *Jet* published the open-casket photographs of **Emmett Till** that his mother, **Mamie Till-Mobley**, insisted the world see. The images were reprinted widely and are often cited as a catalyst of the civil-rights movement. *Ebony* and *Jet* went on to cover Montgomery, Selma and the March on Washington for readers who couldn't find that coverage anywhere else. The company kept expanding: **Ebony Fashion Fair**, a touring runway show produced by Eunice Johnson, which raised money for charity and brought couture to Black audiences across the country; **Fashion Fair Cosmetics** (**1973**), started because the show's models couldn't find department-store makeup for darker skin; book publishing; and radio stations. Johnson eventually became **chairman and CEO of Supreme Life**, the company where he had started as a clerk. In **1982** he became the **first African American on the Forbes 400** *(widely reported; not verified this run)*. He received the **Presidential Medal of Freedom** in **1996**.

Johnson handed the CEO role to his daughter **Linda Johnson Rice** in **2002** and died in Chicago in **2005**, at 87. The company had a hard time adapting to digital media. It **sold *Ebony* and *Jet* to a private-equity firm in 2016**, the photo archive of roughly four million images went to a group of foundations for **$30M in 2019**, and the remaining company filed for Chapter 7 liquidation that year. Fashion Fair has since been relaunched under new owners.

**Takeaways for founders:**
- **Use the distribution you already have.** Johnson's first customers were Supreme Life's policyholders, a list he could reach as an employee. Many companies are started by someone who can already reach an underserved market from their current job.
- **Presell to fund the product.** He collected $6,000 in subscriptions before printing an issue. Charging for a commitment before building is still the cheapest form of capital.
- **Sometimes you have to prove the market exists.** Johnson's real product wasn't the magazine. It was proof to advertisers that Black consumers were a market worth paying to reach. Founders serving an overlooked market often have to do the market research the incumbents never did.
- **Durable brands still need to adapt.** *Ebony* lasted 70 years, but the company didn't make the shift to digital. Being culturally important doesn't protect a business whose distribution model is changing.
