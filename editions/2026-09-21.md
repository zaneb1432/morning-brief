## TECH & AI NEWS

**StepFun ships a 600-billion-parameter frontier model at a third of the price.** The Chinese AI lab StepFun released Step 5 Preview on September 20, a sparse mixture-of-experts model with roughly 600 billion total parameters (about 27 billion active per token) and a 1-million-token context window built for long-horizon agent work — coding, software engineering, financial analysis, and dense professional documents. StepFun is pricing API access at roughly a third of what comparable frontier models charge and has committed to open-sourcing the weights on October 15. It's the latest sign that Chinese labs are no longer just chasing benchmark parity with the US frontier — they're using aggressive pricing and open weights as a competitive wedge, pressuring OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google to defend margins on inference. [Pandaily](https://pandaily.com/stepfun-step-5-preview-600b-moe-1m-context)

**Alibaba's Qwen team pushes omni-modal AI toward real-time agentic use.** On September 18, Alibaba launched Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash, an API-only model that natively ingests text, images, audio, and video with a 1-million-token context window, plus native tool-calling and web search. The pricing is the real headline: audio input costs over 98% less per hour than Alibaba's previous omni model, and audio-visual input is down more than 93%. That price collapse matters because always-on, multimodal agents (voice assistants that watch a screen while listening to a call, for instance) have been cost-prohibitive at scale — this is Alibaba explicitly trying to make that economically viable first. [TechNode](https://technode.com/2026/09/18/alibabas-qwen-releases-qwen3-8-omni-flash-with-1m-token-context/)

**"Plugin4Shell" exposes a shared zero-click flaw across every major AI coding agent.** Security researchers disclosed on September 18 that Claude Code, OpenAI Codex, GitHub Copilot, and Google's Gemini CLI all shared a vulnerability that defeats SHA pinning — the mechanism meant to lock an installed plugin to a specific, reviewed commit. An attacker who controls a plugin's repo could make the agent's checkout silently resolve to malicious code while the pin still appeared honored, enabling zero-click remote code execution with no user interaction required. Anthropic patched Claude Code in version 2.1.179 and OpenAI fixed Codex in 0.146.0; GitHub Copilot remains unpatched as of this writing, and Google chose to deprecate the Gemini CLI outright rather than patch it, pushing users to its newer Antigravity tool instead. This is a serious wake-up call about supply-chain risk in the rapidly growing AI coding-agent ecosystem, where plugin marketplaces have scaled faster than their security review processes. [Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/09/18/plugin4shell-ai-coding-agents-vulnerability/)

**Anthropic bets on scientific credibility and embedded oversight as OpenAI's IPO looms.** On September 18, Anthropic announced a $1B+ "embedded evaluation" partnership with Accenture's Faculty AI division that gives independent evaluators employee-level access inside Anthropic's model development pipeline — an unusually deep transparency arrangement for a frontier lab. The same day, Anthropic Research published work showing Claude can accelerate open-source biomolecular modeling roughly 4x across 30+ models and launched a $1M protein-design competition with Adaptyv Bio. Reuters also reported Anthropic is weighing a new flagship model release as OpenAI gains commercial ground ahead of its expected IPO. Together, the moves read as Anthropic doubling down on safety-and-science positioning as a competitive differentiator rather than trying to out-ship OpenAI feature-for-feature. [Reuters via Investing.com](https://ng.investing.com/news/company-news/anthropic-weighs-new-ai-model-launch-as-openai-gains-ground--reuters-2701917)

**A new antitrust lawsuit accuses the top AI labs of colluding to slow development.** Filed around September 19, the suit alleges Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI, and Google reached an illegal agreement to coordinate a slowdown in AI development pace, framing safety-motivated coordination among frontier labs as a potential antitrust violation. Whatever the suit's merits, it puts a pointed legal spotlight on a real tension the industry has been dancing around all year: multilateral safety commitments and information-sharing between competing labs can look, from a competition-law standpoint, uncomfortably like coordination. Expect this to become a talking point in Washington's broader AI policy debate. [Washington Times](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2026/sep/19/lawsuit-says-openai-anthropic-spacexai-google-made-illegal-agreement/)

## STARTUP WORLD

**Watney raises $80M to put humanoid-adjacent robots to work inside AI data centers.** The San Francisco robotics startup announced on September 18 an $80 million Series A co-led by the Valor Atreides AI Fund and Hummingbird Ventures, pushing total funding past $100 million. Watney builds dual-arm robots that handle cable replacement and server maintenance inside hyperscale data centers; Meta is reportedly trialing the robots at facilities in Iowa and Ohio, with internal estimates suggesting the tech could eventually replace up to 80% of certain routine technical tasks. As AI infrastructure buildout strains skilled labor supply, "robots that maintain the robots' homes" is becoming its own investable category. [WOWTALE](https://en.wowtale.net/2026/09/18/235152/)

**Raindrop raises $35M to catch AI agents failing silently in production.** Announced September 17, the CRV-led Series A (with Lightspeed and Y Combinator participating) brings Raindrop's total funding to $50 million. The company monitors production AI-agent trajectories to flag hallucinations, tool misuse, and behavior drift caused by silent model upgrades, and used the round to launch "Simulations," which tests agent changes against real production traffic before deployment. Customers reportedly include Vercel, Framer, and Clay. As more companies put agents into real workflows, "agent observability" is emerging as a genuine infrastructure category rather than a nice-to-have. [Axios](https://www.axios.com/pro/enterprise-software-deals/2026/09/16/raindrop-crv-lightspeed-datadog)

**Rune raises $40M to turn stranded solar power directly into AI compute.** The Series A, led by Spark Capital with Union Square Ventures and Lowercarbon Capital participating, brings Rune's total funding to $53.5 million. Announced around September 16, the round accompanied the launch of RELIC, a modular data-center unit installed directly at solar generation sites that can bring GPUs online in as little as six weeks. Rune's pitch: up to 20% of solar plant output goes to waste when it can't be transmitted or stored, and siting compute directly at generation sites turns that waste into usable capacity — a clever answer to the power-constraint problem that's throttling AI data-center growth nationwide. [Data Center Dynamics](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/rune-launches-solar-sited-modular-compute-system-raises-40m-series-a/)

**Relay, once a Zapier challenger, completed its shutdown this month.** Relay's paid-customer access formally ended September 14, closing out a wind-down that began in August. Founder and CEO Jacob Bank is returning to Google as VP of Product for Chrome, where he'll lead product and developer relations for the browser. Relay had raised $8.1 million across two rounds since its 2021 launch. It's a reminder that not every AI-adjacent automation startup finds a durable wedge — and a notable case of a founder cycling back into Big Tech rather than raising a next round. [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/17/ai-automation-startup-relay-shuts-down-staff-joins-googles-chrome-team/)

## VENTURE CAPITAL

**Former NBA player Omri Casspi closes a $250M third fund for Swish Ventures.** Announced around September 3, Fund III brings Swish's total assets under management to roughly $800 million. The fund — backed by Sequoia Capital, U.S. pension funds, and several American universities among others — plans to write roughly a dozen checks averaging $20 million each, focused on cybersecurity, infrastructure, physical AI, defense, and govtech. Casspi, the first Israeli to play in the NBA, built his early track record on wins including Cognition, Wonderful, and Upwind. It's a good snapshot of where "non-AI-labs" venture capital is actually pointed right now: defense-adjacent, infrastructure-heavy, and increasingly comfortable underwriting government as a customer. [Calcalist](https://www.calcalistech.com/ctechnews/article/bj11x7a800ge)

**Sequoia upsizes its bet on grid-scale batteries as AI power demand bites.** Sequoia joined Form Energy's $750 million Series G (led by T. Rowe Price) with a $100 million check — reportedly upsized after the company's pitch impressed the partnership — alongside Janus Henderson, Franklin Templeton, and PEAK6. Form Energy makes iron-air batteries designed for roughly 100 hours of grid storage. The investment is a clear marker that top-tier venture firms increasingly see energy infrastructure as inseparable from the AI investment thesis: you can't scale compute without scaling power, and traditional VC is now writing growth-stage checks into hard infrastructure to make sure that bottleneck doesn't cap AI's growth. [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/12/form-energy-raises-750m-to-build-more-100-hour-batteries-for-the-grid/)

**Bill Gurley keeps warning that some AI "revenue" is circular — and the industry is listening.** In ongoing commentary this year, Benchmark's Bill Gurley has flagged deals where a cloud provider invests in an AI startup that then spends much of that capital back on the same provider's cloud — inflating apparent revenue growth without proving independent demand. He's also noted that institutional capital has become so AI-obsessed that solid non-AI companies are struggling to raise follow-on rounds "out of neglect," even as AI-labeled companies raise at what he calls "crazy valuations." Gurley isn't predicting collapse so much as urging investors to keep a price list ready for quality software stocks once a correction hits — a useful counterweight to the more triumphalist "durable and profitable" case firms like JPMorgan have been making about AI capex this year. [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/03/16/bill-gurley-ai-bubble-get-rich-quick.html)

## FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: Chester Carlson

Long before "founder" was a job title people put in their Twitter bio, there was Chester Carlson — a patent attorney with bad arthritis, a garage in Queens, and an idea that more than twenty companies told him was worthless.

Carlson was born in 1906 in Seattle to parents too poor to keep him reliably fed or housed; by his teens he was effectively supporting his chronically ill parents while working multiple jobs and putting himself through Caltech, graduating in the depths of the Depression into a job market with almost nothing for a young physicist. He eventually landed in the patent department of an electronics firm in New York, where he spent his days retyping and hand-copying patent applications and drawings — tedious, repetitive work made worse by his arthritis, and made worse still by the fact that the only copying technologies available (carbon paper, photostat machines, mimeographs) were slow, messy, or required a wet chemical process.

Convinced there had to be a better way, Carlson started experimenting in his apartment kitchen and then a rented room above a bar in Astoria, Queens, teaching himself photoconductivity and electrostatics in public libraries at night. On October 22, 1938, working with a hired assistant in that makeshift lab, he produced the first xerographic copy: he wrote "10-22-38 ASTORIA" on a microscope slide, dusted a charged photoconductive plate with lycopodium powder, and transferred a dry image onto paper — no liquid chemicals, no wet process, just static electricity and powder. He called it electrophotography.

Then came the part of the story most founders will recognize: nobody wanted it. Carlson spent the better part of six years pitching the invention to more than twenty companies, including IBM, GE, Kodak, and RCA — the biggest, best-resourced technology firms in America — and all of them passed. They didn't see the market; carbon paper and photostats seemed to be doing the job fine. It wasn't until 1944 that a nonprofit research institute agreed to help refine the process, and not until 1947 that a small, struggling photographic-paper manufacturer called Haloid — run by Joseph C. Wilson, who had taken the company over from his father — licensed the technology and bet the company's future on it.

It took another twelve years of engineering work before Haloid shipped the Xerox 914 in 1959: the first automatic, plain-paper office copier simple enough to use with the push of a button. It was, at the time, one of the most successful product launches in American business history — within six months, sales matched what the company had projected for the machine's entire lifetime. Haloid renamed itself Xerox Corporation, Carlson became independently wealthy, and — notably for a story about founders — spent much of his later fortune anonymously giving it away to civil rights organizations, environmental causes, and Zen Buddhist study, reportedly working to give away nearly all of his wealth before his death in 1968.

The lesson tech founders still cite Carlson for isn't really about the invention — it's about the twenty-plus "no"s. Xerography sat as a solved technical problem for the better part of a decade because nobody could see the market until Wilson did. It's a reminder that in hard-tech founding, the invention is often the easy part; finding the one partner willing to underwrite the unglamorous work of commercializing it is what actually takes the discipline.
