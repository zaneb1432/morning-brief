## 4. Founder Biography: An Wang

Long before Silicon Valley had a name for the "immigrant founder" archetype, there was An Wang. Born in Shanghai in 1920, the son of a schoolteacher, Wang earned a science degree from Chiao-t'ung University in 1940 and arrived in the United States in 1945 with $50 to his name, intent on studying at Harvard. He earned a PhD in applied physics and engineering from Harvard in 1948 — and within his first month working at Harvard's Computation Laboratory, he had already conceived one of the foundational inventions of modern computing.

Wang's insight was magnetic core memory: tiny magnetized rings that could store a bit of data and be read *and immediately rewritten* without losing the information — solving a problem that had made early computer memory both unreliable and painfully slow. It became the standard memory technology for computers throughout the 1950s and 60s, used in virtually every mainframe of the era, until semiconductor chips eventually replaced it. When Harvard shut down its computer research program in 1951, Wang didn't look for another academic post — he started a company. Wang Laboratories began as a one-person operation selling magnetic cores out of a small office in Boston. In 1956, he sold his core-memory patents to IBM for $500,000 (after IBM had already built products using the technology and initially resisted paying for it) and poured the proceeds back into his own company.

Wang Labs spent the 1960s and 70s reinventing itself repeatedly — from magnetic cores to desktop calculators, and then, decisively, into word processors. By the early 1980s, Wang's dedicated word-processing systems were the standard in offices worldwide, and the company employed more than 30,000 people, generating billions in annual revenue as one of the great American technology success stories — an immigrant engineer's one-man patent shop turned Fortune 500 giant, built without ever taking his company through a hostile buyout or losing control of it.

That same instinct to hold onto control became his undoing. Wang structured the company's stock so his family retained voting power long after professional management might have argued for a course correction, and as the standalone word processor gave way to the IBM-compatible personal computer, Wang Labs was slow to pivot. In 1986, as he was dying of cancer, Wang bypassed experienced outside executives and named his son, Fred Wang, president of the company — a decision even close colleagues considered a mistake, and one the board reversed only in 1989, too late to save the business. An Wang died in 1990; Wang Laboratories filed for bankruptcy in 1992. The company's rise remains one of the era's great immigrant-founder stories — a man who invented a piece of computing's foundation and built an empire on it. Its fall is a durable lesson for founders of any era: the same conviction that lets you build something from nothing can, held too tightly for too long, be the thing that lets it slip away.
