## Tech & AI News

**A federal judge rules the Pentagon's blacklisting of Anthropic was illegal.** U.S. District Judge Rita Lin issued a 59-page order finding that the Department of Defense violated the First Amendment when it designated Anthropic a "supply chain risk" after the company refused to let the military use Claude for mass surveillance of Americans or fully autonomous weapons. Lin called the move "illegal and baseless," writing that "the empty invocation of national security is not a blank check to punish and retaliate against government critics" — and noted the Pentagon kept pursuing work with Anthropic even after blacklisting it, undercutting its own stated rationale. The government is expected to appeal, and Anthropic is still fighting a separate designation under a different statute in D.C. The ruling is an early, concrete test of how far the government can go in punishing an AI lab for setting usage limits on its own models. [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/08/28/judge-blocks-pentagon-anthropic-blacklist)

**Nvidia posts one of the biggest quarters in corporate history — then pumps the brakes on its cloud-financing push.** Revenue hit $96.2 billion, up 106% year-over-year and above the $92.2 billion Wall Street expected, with hyperscale revenue alone up 102% to $48.7 billion; Nvidia is guiding to roughly $108 billion next quarter. Yet within days of reporting, Nvidia quietly paused the revenue-sharing financing program it had unveiled less than two months earlier, in which it offered credit support to AI cloud providers in exchange for a cut of their revenue. Employees reportedly flagged that the arrangement — effectively Nvidia bankrolling the buildout of the data centers that buy its own chips — could draw antitrust scrutiny given how much leverage it hands the chipmaker over customers' businesses. It's a reminder that the AI infrastructure boom is now big enough to attract the kind of regulatory attention usually reserved for monopolists. [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/nvidia-nvda-pulls-back-financing-153423894.html)

**Google ships Gemini Omni 1.1 Flash, pushing generated video toward real production use.** The update to Google's "anything in, anything out" world model adds first-and-last-frame controls for storyboarding shots precisely, video style matching, and a chainable 10-second window that lets creators extend clips up to 40 seconds cumulatively. Pricing follows a draft-then-upscale model — $0.03 per second at 360p, with optional upscaling to 4K — live now in Google AI Studio, Flow, and the Gemini app. The shift from novelty clips to directable, extendable sequences with real cost controls signals Google is now competing for actual video-production workflows, not just demos. [XenoSpectrum](https://xenospectrum.com/en/google-gemini-omni-flash/)

**More than 80 UK performers demand legal protection against AI voice cloning.** Actors including Hugh Bonneville and Matt Lucas, plus musicians like Sandi Thom, signed an open letter — "Save Our Voices Now" — asking the UK government to recognize a person's voice as a protected part of their statutory identity, alongside a companion government petition. Derry Girls actress Siobhán McSweeney said she was "deeply worried" the government is lagging behind the technology: "Behind that voice is the emotion, the life, the pain, the tears and joy that I've felt along the way." It's the clearest sign yet that voice-cloning backlash, long simmering in the US since the ScarJo/OpenAI dispute, has become an organized policy fight in Britain. [Digital Music News](https://www.digitalmusicnews.com/2026/08/28/uk-campaign-against-voice-cloning-ai/)

**MIT engineers build an AI that predicts disasters it's never seen.** Published August 20 in *Nature Communications*, the "Extreme Event Aware" (η-learning) method from grad student Kai Chang and Professor Themis Sapsis generates statistically plausible worst-case weather scenarios — complete with likely duration, intensity, and affected area — without needing historical examples of that specific catastrophe to train on. That solves a core problem in disaster modeling: catastrophes are outliers by definition, so tools have always had to learn from the very events they see least often. Real-world use could mean better early warning for floods, heatwaves, or storms with no local precedent. [MIT Schwarzman College of Computing](https://computing.mit.edu/news/generating-scenarios-for-extreme-events-without-extreme-data/)

**Meta closes another loophole letting people record covertly with its smart glasses — and launches a billboard campaign to explain the recording light.** The software update stops the camera from working if the LED indicator is covered mid-recording, the second such privacy patch in under two months. Alongside it, Meta is rolling out an "education and awareness campaign," starting with a Los Angeles billboard reading the glasses are "designed for everyone... not just the people wearing them," with more markets to follow. As smart glasses emerge as a leading contender to succeed the smartphone as the default AI interface, the always-on camera-and-microphone form factor is forcing Meta to litigate bystander consent in public before regulators do it for them. [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2245776/meta-closing-loophole-that-allowed-people-to-record-with-smart-glasses-light-covered/)

## Startup World

**Castelion raises $1 billion Series C to mass-produce hypersonic missiles, hits $13 billion valuation.** The round — $800 million in equity plus a $250 million credit facility — was co-led by Andreessen Horowitz, Carlyle, and JPMorgan Chase, with T. Rowe Price joining as a first-time backer. Founded in 2022 by former SpaceX executives, Castelion has already banked more than $500 million in U.S. military contracts and is expanding its 1,000-acre New Mexico manufacturing campus — the largest dedicated hypersonic missile facility in the country — to scale production of its Blackbeard strike missile. It's the latest sign that mainstream venture capital, not just defense-focused funds, is now comfortable writing billion-dollar checks for weapons manufacturing. [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/20/castelion-hits-13b-valuation-to-mass-produce-hypersonic-missiles/)

**Emerald AI raises an oversubscribed $150 million Series A at a $1.05 billion valuation to help data centers throttle their own power use.** Co-led by Energize Capital and DCVC, with Nvidia, Samsung, Siemens, Aramco Ventures, Salesforce Ventures, and a dozen other Fortune Global 500 strategics participating, the round brings Emerald AI's total funding past $220 million. Its software lets AI data centers dynamically flex their power draw to match grid conditions — already deployed at multi-megawatt scale — targeting more than 100 gigawatts of untapped U.S. grid capacity. As AI's electricity appetite becomes the industry's binding constraint, "give some power back when the grid needs it" is turning into a venture category of its own. [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/business/emerald-ai-raises-150-million-series-a-at-105-billion-valuation-to-scale-power-flexible-ai-data-centers)

**Gatik raises $200 million as driverless trucks start running Walmart and PepsiCo routes for real.** The Series D, led by Qatar Investment Authority and Koch Disruptive Technologies with ARK Invest and Millennium Management also participating, brings Gatik's total raised to roughly $500 million. The autonomous middle-mile freight company says it has completed 85,000 fully driverless deliveries at 99% on-time performance and more than $600 million in contracted revenue, and is targeting over 100 driverless trucks on the road by year-end. Unlike the robotaxi wars, Gatik's fixed, repetitive B2B routes have made autonomy commercially boring in the best way — which is exactly why investors are piling in. [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-25/gatik-ai-raises-200-million-in-its-largest-funding-series)

**AusperBio Therapeutics closes $120 million Series C for its hepatitis B therapy.** The round, led by RA Capital, funds continued clinical development of the company's RNA-based approach to a functional cure for chronic hepatitis B — a reminder that amid the AI-infrastructure gold rush, biotech is still quietly raising nine-figure rounds for hard science with a clear clinical endpoint. [TechStartups roundup, Aug 27, 2026]

**Navi lands its first institutional funding round, valuing the Indian fintech near $1.3 billion.** The Bengaluru-based lender and insurance app, founded by Flipkart co-founder Sachin Bansal, had previously been funded almost entirely by Bansal himself; bringing in outside institutional capital for the first time marks a notable shift for one of India's more idiosyncratic fintech bets. [StartupTalky, Aug 27, 2026]

In a fundraising climate where most African fund managers have struggled to raise anything at all, an oversubscribed close is itself the headline. [Disrupt Africa](https://disruptafrica.com/2026/08/27/vc-firm-ventures-platform-closes-pan-african-fund-ii-at-84m/)

**The gap between what VCs are funding is narrowing to a single script.** Recent thesis writing out of Sequoia and a16z has converged on nearly identical language — both firms are backing AI "agents" and "autopilots" designed to replace service-industry labor rather than just sell software seats, with foundation-model deals absorbing roughly 40% of AI deal activity even as vertical, outcome-based AI startups pick up velocity. AI now accounts for well over half of all U.S. venture dollars deployed, with a16z alone credited with more than a third of AI-specific deal share. When the biggest funds in the industry are pitching nearly the same idea, the interesting question stops being "who has the best thesis" and becomes "who executes it before the field commoditizes."

## Founder Biography: An Wang

Long before Silicon Valley had a name for the "immigrant founder," An Wang was living it. Born in Shanghai in 1920, Wang showed enough of a gift for mathematics that he was studying at the elite Shanghai Provincial High School by age 13, and by 1940 had graduated from Chiao Tung University — then regarded as "the MIT of China." When Japan occupied Shanghai during World War II, Wang fled to Guilin, where he led a scrappy team that scavenged parts to build radios for the Chinese military — an early, high-stakes lesson in building working technology out of whatever was on hand.

He arrived in the United States in 1945 and earned a PhD in applied physics from Harvard, where he made a discovery that would quietly underpin an entire generation of computing: a method for magnetic core memory, a way of storing information reliably using tiny magnetized rings, which became the standard memory technology for computers throughout the 1950s and 60s. IBM eventually paid Wang $500,000 for the rights — good money, but a fraction of what the invention was ultimately worth to the industry.

In 1951, with that money and little else, Wang founded Wang Laboratories out of a single room above an electrical fixtures store in Boston. He was the company's only employee. Over the next three decades he built it into one of the most successful American technology companies of its era — first with electronic calculators, then with word-processing systems that, for a stretch in the late 1970s and early 1980s, made "Wang" as synonymous with office computing as "Xerox" was with copying. By the mid-1980s, Wang Laboratories employed more than 15,000 people across factories and offices worldwide, and An Wang himself had become a billionaire — one of the first self-made immigrant tech billionaires in American history, and a prominent philanthropist in the Boston area.

Then came the part of the story that startup founders still study as a cautionary tale. In 1986, Wang stepped back as president and installed his son, Frederick Wang, as his successor — not because Frederick was the strongest candidate, but because An Wang was determined to keep control of the company within his family. It was a catastrophic misjudgment. As IBM's PC platform swept the office-computing market that Wang Laboratories had helped pioneer, Frederick's leadership failed to adapt, and revenue collapsed. An Wang returned in 1989, fired his own son, and tried to right the ship — but it was too late. He died in March 1990, and Wang Laboratories declared bankruptcy in 1992, eventually being absorbed by the Dutch firm Getronics in 1999.

An Wang's story endures less as a tragedy than as a template — one that plenty of founders since have relearned the hard way: a garage-to-billions run built on genuine invention, undone not by a competitor but by a succession decision made for family loyalty instead of the company's survival. It remains one of the sharpest lessons in Silicon Valley folklore on the difference between founding a company and knowing when to let go of it.
