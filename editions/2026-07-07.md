## 1. TECH & AI NEWS

**Security researchers document the first fully autonomous "agentic ransomware" attack.** Cloud security firm Sysdig published research on a campaign it calls JADEPUFFER — what it believes is the first end-to-end ransomware operation run entirely by an LLM agent rather than a human operator following a fixed playbook. The agent broke into an internet-facing Langflow instance via a known vulnerability (CVE-2025-3248), then independently handled reconnaissance, credential theft, lateral movement, privilege escalation, and a destructive database-extortion sequence — firing off more than 600 distinct, purposeful payloads in a compressed window, with one sequence going from a failed login to a working exploit fix in 31 seconds. It ultimately encrypted 1,342 Nacos service configuration items, though in an ironic twist the AES encryption key was generated with true randomness and only ever printed to stdout — never saved or transmitted anywhere — meaning the victim can't recover the data even by paying. This matters because it moves "AI-powered cybercrime" from an industry talking point into a forensically documented case study, and will intensify pressure on AI labs and infrastructure vendors to harden agentic tools against exactly this kind of misuse. [Sysdig](https://www.sysdig.com/blog/jadepuffer-agentic-ransomware-for-automated-database-extortion) · [The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/07/ai-agent-exploits-langflow-rce-to.html) · [BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/jadepuffer-ransomware-used-ai-agent-to-automate-entire-attack/)

**Anthropic has overtaken OpenAI in revenue — a genuine reshuffling of the AI industry's pecking order.** Fortune reports Anthropic is now projecting a run rate near $47 billion and profitability by 2029, a year ahead of OpenAI's own target, versus OpenAI's disclosed $25–33 billion annualized revenue range. Anthropic also overtook OpenAI in business/enterprise subscriptions as of May (per Ramp data), and Similarweb figures show ChatGPT's share of monthly generative-AI visits fell below a majority for the first time that same month — meaning users are increasingly willing to switch models rather than default to ChatGPT. The shift follows Anthropic becoming the most valuable AI startup by private valuation (nearing $1 trillion) in a late-May funding round, and reporting suggests Sam Altman has been recalibrating OpenAI's strategy as it loses ground to both Anthropic and Google simultaneously. This is the clearest evidence yet that OpenAI's early-mover dominance is eroding rather than compounding. **The FTC opens a comment period on a policy statement targeting state laws that force AI models to alter "truthful" outputs.** Released July 1 with continued coverage this week, the proposed statement — issued per a December executive order — argues that AI companies implicitly represent their systems as designed to produce the most accurate output possible, that consumers rely on this without independent fact-checking more than 90% of the time (per the FTC's own citation), and that secretly tuning systems to pursue undisclosed objectives that distort outputs could violate Section 5 of the FTC Act. The statement singles out Colorado's Artificial Intelligence Act as an example of a state law that could pressure companies into suppressing output accuracy to avoid disparate-impact liability, and argues such state requirements may be impliedly preempted where they conflict with the federal scheme — though it leaves an escape valve via clear, conspicuous disclosure of a system's prioritized objectives. This is a real federal-vs-state preemption fight brewing over AI content moderation and "bias mitigation" rules, with direct stakes for how model providers must talk about system prompts and alignment tuning. The comment period runs through July 31. On SWE-bench Pro it scored 59.5%, edging out GPT-5.5's 58.6% and Gemini 3.1 Pro's 54.2% (though still behind Claude Opus 4.7/4.8). Pricing is aggressive too ($0.75/$2.95 per million input/output tokens, discounted at launch), and the model had already taken the #1 usage spot in several agentic coding workspaces before its origin was even known — real developer adoption, not just a benchmark headline. ## 2. STARTUP WORLD

**Bespoke Labs raises $40M to build the "training environments" that make AI agents reliable.** The Mountain View startup closed a $31.75M Series A led by Wing VC, layered on an earlier $8.25M seed backed in part by Google DeepMind chief scientist Jeff Dean, with Series A participation from Mayfield, The House Fund, dbt Labs CEO Tristan Handy, and angel investors from Anthropic, OpenAI, and Meta. Founded in 2024 by Mahesh Sathiamoorthy and Alex Dimakis, Bespoke builds infrastructure that lets AI developers and enterprises test, measure, and refine autonomous agents in realistic simulated environments before production deployment — a direct answer to the widely cited problem that agent demos look great and then fall apart outside curated conditions. As frontier labs shift emphasis from pretraining to post-training and reinforcement learning, "environment building" has become one of the more contested infrastructure layers underneath the whole agent economy, and the mix of institutional VCs alongside individual angels from every major lab is a good tell for where that conviction sits right now. ## 4. FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: Sara Blakely

Sara Blakely didn't set out to reinvent women's underwear — she set out to be a lawyer. Born February 27, 1971, in Clearwater, Florida, to an artist mother and a personal-injury-attorney father, she grew up with an unusual family ritual: her dad would ask her every week, "What did you fail at?" If she had nothing to report, he was disappointed — to him, an empty answer meant she hadn't tried anything hard enough. That reframing of failure as a natural byproduct of effort, rather than something shameful, became the philosophical bedrock of everything she built later. Blakely graduated from Florida State University with a communications degree, dreaming of law school, but bombed the LSAT twice. She briefly tried stand-up comedy and even auditioned to play Goofy at Walt Disney World (she was cast as the chipmunk Chip instead, and quit after a day). She then spent seven years selling fax machines door-to-door for Danka Business Systems, grinding through Florida heat in pantyhose she despised — sweaty, uncomfortable, and visible through white pants.

In 1998, on her way to a sales call, Blakely cut the feet off a pair of control-top pantyhose so she could wear open-toed shoes while keeping a smooth line under her slacks. The hack worked, and a business idea was born. She spent two years developing it nights and weekends, plowing her entire $5,000 in savings — money saved from selling fax machines — into the venture. She cold-called hosiery mills across the Southeast trying to find someone willing to manufacture her product; most turned her down until a mill owner in North Carolina, reportedly persuaded by his own daughters after hearing Blakely's pitch, agreed. Unable to afford a lawyer and unable to even find a female patent attorney in Georgia, she wrote the patent application herself, hiring an attorney only to finalize it for $750. She named the company Spanx — adding the "x" because she'd read invented, slightly edgy words tested well with consumers — and, with zero retail contacts, cold-called a buyer at Neiman Marcus until she landed a meeting. She closed the sale by taking the buyer into a bathroom and modeling the product on herself, before and after. Neiman Marcus placed an order for seven stores.

The turning point came almost immediately: in November 2000, Oprah Winfrey named Spanx one of her "Favorite Things" of the year. Orders exploded, and Blakely quit her fax-machine job for good. Spanx did roughly $4M in sales its first year and $10M its second — all without taking a dollar of outside investment or debt, a point of fierce pride for Blakely, who retained 100% ownership of the company for nearly two decades while it expanded from shapewear into leggings, bras, swimwear, and activewear. In 2012, Forbes named her the world's youngest self-made female billionaire, and Time put her on its "Time 100" list the same year. In 2006, she finished runner-up on Richard Branson's reality show "The Rebel Billionaire," and Branson's $750,000 consolation gift seeded the Sara Blakely Foundation, which funds women entrepreneurs. In 2013, she became the first woman to sign the Giving Pledge, committing the bulk of her fortune to philanthropy — "to invest in women because I believe it offers one of the greatest returns on investment."

In October 2021, Blakely sold a majority stake in Spanx to private-equity giant Blackstone in a deal valuing the company at $1.2 billion, becoming executive chairwoman while stepping back from day-to-day CEO duties. The deal restored her billionaire status after her paper wealth had dipped during the pandemic; as of 2025–26 her net worth sits around $1.1–1.3 billion. She hasn't stopped inventing: in 2024 she launched Sneex, a direct-to-consumer line of "comfortable" high-heeled sneakers, and in May 2026, Florida State University — the school that once watched her flunk her way out of law-school plans — awarded her an honorary degree at commencement. She remains a fixture on the speaking circuit, telling founders that the things you don't know can be your greatest asset, because you're not boxed in by "the way it's supposed to be done."
