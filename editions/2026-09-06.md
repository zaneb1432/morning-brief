## 1. TECH & AI NEWS

### OpenAI Launches GPT-6 Astra, Its First GPT-6 Model
OpenAI released GPT-6 Astra on September 3 as a limited partner preview, opening it to ChatGPT Plus/Pro/Business/Enterprise users and the API the next day. OpenAI is billing it as "the most intelligent and aligned model in the world," citing a 98% score on FrontierMath Tier 4, 99.9% on ARC-AGI-3, and a 100% on ExploitBench, along with a 1.05M-token context window and a knowledge cutoff of April 30, 2026. Its flagship new capability is "computer use" — navigating a computer interface the way a person would — alongside strong marks in software engineering, science, and cybersecurity. Notably, OpenAI says Astra meets its internal "Critical" capability threshold for cybersecurity, so the publicly shipped version has safeguards that reject certain prompts in that domain, and OpenAI has acknowledged those filters can misfire on legitimate security work. API pricing is $10/million input and $50/million output tokens. This is the most significant frontier model release of the week and sets a new bar OpenAI's rivals will be measured against.

Source: [GPT-6 Astra: A new generation of intelligence (OpenAI)](https://openai.com/index/gpt-6-astra/) | [OpenAI announces rollout of GPT-6 Astra (CNBC)](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/03/open-ai-astra-gpt-6-cyber.html)

### Anthropic Ships Claude Fable 5.1 and Mythos 5.1
On September 1, Anthropic released two versions of its newest flagship model: Claude Fable 5.1, generally available to all users, and Claude Mythos 5.1, a version with different safeguards released only through Anthropic's trusted-access programs for sensitive work in cybersecurity and life sciences. Anthropic says Fable 5.1 matches or beats Fable 5's performance while cutting typical workload costs by roughly 25% and highly agentic workload costs by up to 45%, driven largely by a big reduction in cache-read pricing. Alongside the models, Anthropic introduced Enterprise Frontier Safeguards (EFS), which pairs zero-data-retention privacy with automated misuse detection while giving enterprise customers control over how their data is reviewed and stored. Both models are available via Anthropic's API, AWS, Google Cloud, and Microsoft Azure. The split-tier release (open model vs. gated "high-safeguard" model) reflects a broader industry pattern of restricting the most capable systems to vetted users rather than releasing one model to everyone.

Source: [Introducing Claude Fable 5.1 and Claude Mythos 5.1 (Anthropic)](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-fable-and-mythos-5-1)

### Google Debuts Gemini 3.8 Flash and a Gated "Cyber" Model via Its New Fairwind Program
Google released Gemini 3.8 Flash on September 2 — its third Flash-tier model in six weeks — alongside Gemini 3.8 Flash Cyber, described as Google's most capable cybersecurity model. Rather than releasing Flash Cyber broadly, Google is distributing it only to vetted government agencies, critical-infrastructure operators, and software maintainers through a new "Fairwind Program." Google says the model found real-world vulnerabilities across 20+ programming languages at a 71% success rate, and that its own Chrome Security team found it produced 2.6x more correct vulnerability patches than the best commercial models, including larger ones. The gated rollout illustrates the tension the whole industry is now navigating: the same capabilities that let defenders find and patch bugs faster could also automate offensive cyber operations if misused, so Google, Anthropic, and OpenAI are all now shipping "cyber" model variants with restricted, trusted-access programs rather than open releases.

Source: [Introducing Gemini 3.8 Flash and 3.8 Flash Cyber (Google Blog)](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/3-8-flash-and-3-8-flash-cyber/) | [Fairwind Program (Google DeepMind)](https://deepmind.google/fairwind-program/)

### DeepSeek Orders 160,000 Huawei Ascend Chips for a Gigawatt-Scale Data Center in Inner Mongolia
Reports emerging around September 4 indicate DeepSeek plans to deploy at least 160,000 Huawei Ascend 950DT accelerators at a roughly 1-gigawatt data center in Ulanqab, Inner Mongolia, which would be among the largest known Huawei AI chip clusters. Unlike Huawei's positioning of the 950DT for training, DeepSeek reportedly intends to use the cluster primarily for inference — serving already-trained models to API and consumer traffic — rather than training new frontier models. The facility is expected to come online in stages starting late 2027 or early 2028, and Huawei's own component shortages (particularly high-end memory) mean fulfilling the full order could take well over a year. The move is a concrete signal of China's push toward domestic AI chip self-sufficiency in the face of continued U.S. export restrictions on Nvidia hardware, and it matters for the industry because it shows a leading Chinese lab making a long-term bet on non-Nvidia infrastructure at real scale.

Source: [DeepSeek turns to Huawei for 160,000 AI chips as Nvidia stays locked out of China (tech-ish)](https://tech-ish.com/2026/09/04/deepseek-turns-to-huawei-for-160000-ai-chips-as-nvidia-stays-locked-out-of-china/)

### EU AI Act Enforcement Escalates: Formal Information Requests Sent to OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google
On August 29, European Commission tech chief Henna Virkkunen confirmed the EU AI Office had sent its first formal requests for information under the AI Act to more than 30 general-purpose AI model providers worldwide — reportedly including OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google — covering model security, independent external evaluations, and post-market monitoring. Companies that stonewall or mislead regulators face fines of up to €15 million or 3% of global turnover. In a follow-up interview published September 4, Virkkunen told Axios that while the U.S. is publicly resisting a broad EU-style AI law, American guardrails are emerging anyway through state laws and court cases, and that the two regions are "often" converging on similar underlying safety concerns despite different regulatory cultures. This marks the shift of the EU AI Act from a paper framework into active enforcement against the three biggest U.S. frontier labs, making it one of the more consequential policy developments of the year for how those companies operate in Europe.

Source: [EU tech chief: AI guardrails inevitable for U.S. (Axios)](https://www.axios.com/2026/09/04/eu-tech-chief-ai-guardrails-inevitable-us) | [The EU AI Act gets real (Axios)](https://www.axios.com/2026/08/28/eu-ai-act-gets-real)

### McKinsey's 2026 AI Survey: A Third of Companies Are Skipping Software Purchases to Build In-House With AI
McKinsey's "State of AI in 2026" global survey (1,719 respondents across 97 countries, fielded May–June 2026) found that 32% of organizations have decided against buying at least one software product or feature because they could build it internally using agentic coding tools instead. The trend is sharpest in tech (41%) and healthcare payers/providers (39%), and among "high performers" — companies attributing at least 5% of EBIT to AI — nearly half report skipping purchases in favor of building. This finding, which continued circulating in AI-industry coverage through this week, points to a structural threat to the traditional SaaS business model: as coding agents get cheap and capable enough to replace off-the-shelf features, enterprise buyers are increasingly treating "build vs. buy" as a live question rather than a foregone conclusion, which could reshape enterprise software spending over the next several years.

## 2. STARTUP WORLD

### Gimlet Labs Raises $300M Series B, Hits $3B Valuation for Multi-Chip AI Inference
Gimlet Labs, a San Francisco-based AI infrastructure startup, raised a $300 million Series B led by Andreessen Horowitz, tripling its valuation to $3 billion and bringing its total funding to $392 million. The round drew new investors Arm, Microsoft's M12, and Sapphire Ventures alongside existing backers Menlo Ventures and Factory. Gimlet builds what it calls the industry's first "multi-silicon inference cloud" — software that disaggregates AI model workloads and routes different inference phases to whichever chip (GPU, TPU, or otherwise) handles them most efficiently, aiming to boost latency, throughput, and power efficiency for agentic AI applications. The company says it has tripled its customer base since March 2026, adding one of the top three frontier AI labs and one of the top three hyperscalers as customers. This matters because it signals investor appetite for "picks and shovels" infrastructure plays that reduce dependence on any single chipmaker as AI compute costs balloon.

### AI Cloud Firm Nscale Seeks $3.5B in Pre-IPO Financing Ahead of Public Listing

The notes are being offered at a double-digit discount to Nscale's eventual IPO price, with the discount scaling up to a $30 billion valuation ceiling, and the IPO itself could raise a further $3 billion. This deal illustrates how AI infrastructure financing is increasingly blending traditional venture capital, strategic chipmaker investment, and public-market mechanics before companies even IPO — a structure that's becoming a template for capital-hungry AI compute providers.

## 4. FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY

### Chester Carlson

Chester Carlson did not set out to build a company — he set out to solve a problem that annoyed him personally, and it took him more than a decade of rejection to convince anyone else it mattered.

Born in Seattle in 1906, Carlson grew up poor. His father, a barber, was disabled by tuberculosis and arthritis, and by his teens Chester was the family's main breadwinner, working before and after school to support both parents. He put himself through Caltech studying physics, graduating in 1930 into the teeth of the Great Depression, and eventually landed a job in the patent department of P.R. Mallory, an electronics manufacturer in New York. It was tedious work that planted the seed of his invention: he had to make copies of patent drawings and documents by hand or by expensive photostat, and he became convinced there had to be a faster, cheaper way to duplicate the printed page.

Working alone in his apartment and later in a rented room above a bar in Astoria, Queens — because his landlady objected to the chemical smells — Carlson spent years experimenting with photoconductivity and electrostatics on nights and weekends. On October 22, 1938, with the help of a hired physicist named Otto Kornei, he produced the first successful copy: a sulfur-coated zinc plate rubbed with a cloth, exposed to light through a slide, and dusted with powder, which transferred the words "10-22-38 ASTORIA" onto a piece of wax paper. He called the process electrophotography; the world would later know it as xerography, from the Greek for "dry writing."

The breakthrough should have been the hard part. It wasn't. From 1939 to 1944, Carlson pitched his invention to more than twenty companies, including IBM, General Electric, Kodak, and RCA — the very firms best positioned to see its value. Every one of them turned him down. Carlson later described the response as "an enthusiastic lack of interest." Existing carbon paper and mimeograph technology seemed adequate, and no one could picture ordinary offices needing a machine to make copies of copies. It is the turning point that defines his story: a genuinely transformative idea, sitting unwanted, because the market couldn't yet imagine the need it would create.

The break came in 1944, when the nonprofit Battelle Memorial Institute in Ohio agreed to fund further development in exchange for a majority share of future royalties. Three years later, a small, struggling photographic paper company in Rochester, New York, called the Haloid Company, took a licensing gamble on the technology. It would prove to be one of the most consequential bets in American business history. After more than a decade of further engineering, Haloid — renamed Xerox Corporation — released the Xerox 914 in 1959, the first automatic plain-paper copier. It became one of the best-selling industrial products ever made and turned "xerox" into a verb.

Carlson, who held the founding patents, became immensely wealthy — his royalties eventually totaled well over $100 million. But the more remarkable second act of his life was what he did with the money. Having lived through real poverty, he gave almost all of it away, much of it anonymously, to causes including civil rights organizations, education, world peace initiatives, and Zen Buddhist study, having helped found the Rochester Zen Center in 1966. Asked shortly before his death what he wanted most, he said simply that he hoped "to die a poor man." He died of a heart attack in 1968 at age 62, having spent his final years quietly emptying a fortune he'd spent twenty years failing to convince anyone to help him build.
