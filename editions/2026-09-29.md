## TECH & AI NEWS

**1. Anthropic ships Claude Sonnet 5.5 — faster, cheaper, same sticker price.**
Anthropic released Sonnet 5.5 on Sept 28, the second model in its Claude 5.5 family. It generates output 30%+ faster and, by Anthropic's testing, costs up to 30% less per task because it uses fewer tokens and tool calls, while list pricing stays at $2/$10 per million input/output tokens. It is positioned as the everyday workhorse next to Opus 5.5 (aimed at complex, judgment-heavy work), and reportedly scores 70.6% on Terminal-Bench 4.0 versus 10.3% for Sonnet 5. Why it matters: the competitive axis is shifting from raw capability to cost per completed task, which is what enterprise buyers of agentic coding tools actually pay for. Sources: [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/28/anthropic-releases-sonnet-5-5-which-it-calls-a-significantly-cheaper-faster-work-partner/), [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/anthropic-launches-claude-sonnet-5-5-with-30-cost-reduction-per-task-due-to-faster-speeds-and-fewer-tool-calls), [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-sonnet-5-5).

**2. Nvidia launches an Open Agent Safety Platform with 100+ partners.**
Nvidia unveiled a platform that enforces agent safety outside the model itself. It pairs OpenShell (open-source software that bounds what an agent can access and traces its actions) with Sentry, an independent monitor running on BlueField-4 DPUs that can isolate or halt an agent that steps outside its allowed boundaries. Partners reportedly include Anthropic, SpaceXAI, Scale AI and SAP. Why it matters: prompt-level guardrails are easy to bypass; putting enforcement in hardware and infrastructure is an attempt to make agent containment auditable, and it gives Nvidia a foothold in the security layer, not just compute. Sources: [Nvidia Newsroom](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/open-agent-safety-platform), [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/28/nvidia-launches-new-platform-for-reining-in-rogue-ai-agents/), [CSO Online](https://www.csoonline.com/article/4227843/nvidia-releases-open-agent-safety-platform-to-monitor-and-govern-agentic-ai.html).

**3. Sanders and Casar introduce the "Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act."**
Sen. Bernie Sanders and Rep. Greg Casar introduced legislation (Sept 23–24) that would permanently ban superintelligent systems (cognitive capability superior to humans in most domains), pause advanced AI development until a new cabinet-level federal AI agency sets safety rules and a model-review process, and pursue international agreements. Violations would carry a "corporate death penalty" and up to 20 years in prison. Passage is very unlikely in the current Congress, but it marks how far the policy debate has moved, and critics such as Gary Marcus are already arguing against it. Sources: [Sanders press release](https://www.sanders.senate.gov/press-releases/news-sanders-casar-introduce-legislation-to-create-new-federal-agency-to-ban-artificial-superintelligence-pause-advanced-ai-development/), [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/congress/bernie-sanders-greg-casar-propose-ai-superintelligence-ban-20-year-jai-rcna599460), [Gary Marcus](https://garymarcus.substack.com/p/the-new-sanders-casar-ban-artificial).

**4. SoftBank borrows ~$11B in junk bonds to fund its OpenAI bet.**
SoftBank sold roughly $11B of dollar and euro high-yield bonds at record-high yields for the issuer, partly to fund a follow-on OpenAI investment expected to close next month. It has committed close to $65B to OpenAI in total and is raising debt on multiple fronts. Why it matters: frontier-AI funding is moving from equity toward leveraged balance sheets, which raises the stakes if OpenAI's growth or compute economics disappoint. Sources: [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-21/softbank-seeks-over-11-billion-in-junk-bond-deal-for-openai-bet), [Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/business/2026/09/24/companies/softbank-junk-bond-steep-price-pay/).

**5. OpenAI turns off the Sora API (Sept 24).**
OpenAI ended developer access to Sora with no named replacement, following the April shutdown of the consumer app. OpenAI gave no reason; reporting points to compute shortages, cost pressure and a pivot toward enterprise products. Why it matters: a reminder of platform risk for anyone building on a lab's non-core modality. Sources: [OpenAI Help Center](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/20001152-what-to-know-about-the-sora-discontinuation), [Futurum](https://futurumgroup.com/insights/openai-sora-discontinuation-what-the-end-of-a-platform-means-for-enterprise-ai-strategy/).

## FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: Ida Rosenthal (1886–1973), co-founder of Maidenform

Ida Kaganovich was born in 1886 in the Russian Empire (in what is now Belarus) and emigrated to the United States in 1904, settling in Hoboken, New Jersey, and then New York. She worked as a seamstress and married sculptor William Rosenthal. In the early 1920s she was working in a small Manhattan dress shop run by Enid Bissett. Fashion of the day favored a flat, boyish silhouette, and the dresses hung badly on women who did not fit it. Rosenthal and Bissett began sewing a separate support garment into the dresses, and customers started asking to buy the garment on its own.

In 1922–23, Rosenthal, Bissett and William Rosenthal turned that bandeau-style brassiere into a business, and the company became Maidenform. Her key insight, and the one that separated Maidenform from competitors, was sizing: rather than treating a brassiere as one-size-fits-all, Maidenform offered graduated cup sizes, an innovation that made fit a selling point and let mass production serve real bodies. William designed while Ida ran the operation, selling and managing the company. She was the driving business force for decades and became president after William's death in 1958, running the company through its growth into a national brand, including the famous "I dreamed I..." ad campaign of the 1940s and 50s.

Her lesson for founders: she started from a customer complaint about a product that did not fit, standardized the fix through sizing, and scaled it. She died in 1973. (Written from general knowledge, not fetched sources; check dates against a biography before quoting.)
