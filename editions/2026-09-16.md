## TECH & AI NEWS

**The industry's slowdown debate spilled into geopolitics this week, as Chinese state media accused U.S. AI leaders of crying wolf for competitive advantage.** Following Dario Amodei's essay last weekend warning that swarms of AI agents could effectively "take over the internet" within months absent tighter coordination — a call Sam Altman publicly backed and Elon Musk endorsed on X — Chinese state media dismissed the appeal as a "self-serving" attempt by American labs to slow down rivals just as China closes the capability gap. **Cloudflare flipped the default on AI crawlers this week, in one of the clearest moves yet to make AI companies pay for the data they consume.** As of September 15, Cloudflare blocks "mixed-use" crawlers — bots that won't declare whether a given request is for search, AI training, or live agent browsing — by default on any page carrying ads, which covers most of the ad-supported web. The policy, announced in July, gave AI firms a two-month window to split their crawlers into clearly declared categories; those that didn't now lose access to a huge swath of publisher content unless site owners manually opt back in. It applies to new customers, new sites, and all existing free-tier accounts, though paid customers keep their current settings. **Apple's rebuilt Siri finally shipped this week — running on models built jointly with Google.** With the iOS/macOS "27" releases reaching the public on September 14, Apple opened its long-delayed Siri AI overhaul to English-language users, powered by Apple Foundation Models developed in collaboration with Google's Gemini team. The assistant remains explicitly a beta: daily usage caps apply to server-side features (Siri AI, intelligent photo editing, Image Playground), expanded access will reportedly cost extra once Apple sets pricing, and support for French, Japanese, Korean, Portuguese, and Spanish isn't expected until October. It's a notable admission from a company that usually waits for polish — shipping a capped, beta-labeled assistant rather than a finished one — and a reminder of how deep the Google partnership now runs inside Apple's AI stack. **A single attacker used hundreds of AI agents to autonomously breach 440 servers across 48 countries, with almost no human hands-on-keyboard.** Security firm GreyNoise reported that a likely Russian-speaking threat actor built working exploits for two PaperCut NG/MF vulnerabilities — an authentication bypass and an unsafe-reflection remote-code-execution flaw — in a private lab, then handed the actual intrusion campaign to swarms of AI agents powered by OpenAI Codex, a DeepSeek model, and off-the-shelf offensive tools. Once launched, the automation moved fast: 11 organizations were compromised in 26 seconds at one point, and a U.S. high school went from initial access to full domain admin in seven minutes. At least 440 PaperCut instances across 395 organizations in 48 countries were hit, with education institutions taking the brunt (204 victims) mostly because of PaperCut's customer mix rather than deliberate targeting. It's one of the starkest real-world demonstrations yet of AI agents doing the labor that used to require a large human hacking crew. ## FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: Brian Chesky

In October 2007, Brian Chesky and his roommate Joe Gebbia couldn't make rent on their San Francisco apartment. A design conference was in town, every hotel in the city was booked solid, and the two — who had met years earlier at the Rhode Island School of Design, where Chesky studied industrial design and ran the school's ice hockey club to Gebbia's basketball club — spotted an opening. They bought three air mattresses, threw them on the living-room floor, and offered strangers a place to sleep and a home-cooked breakfast for $80 a night. They called it "Air Bed and Breakfast" and built a bare-bones website to advertise it. It worked, if barely: three guests showed up for that first design conference, and Chesky and Gebbia decided there might be a real business underneath the gimmick.

Nathan Blecharczyk, a Harvard-trained engineer and another of Chesky's former roommates, joined as the technical co-founder in 2008 to help build the site out properly. But the business nearly died before it found traction. The founders pitched roughly fifteen angel investors and were turned down, or simply ignored, by most of them; personal savings ran out fast. To stay afloat during the 2008 presidential campaign, Chesky and Gebbia designed and sold limited-edition breakfast cereal — "Obama O's" and "Cap'n McCain's" — for $40 a box, moving over a thousand boxes and pulling in roughly $30,000, just enough runway to keep going. The cereal stunt caught the eye of Y Combinator's Paul Graham, who accepted the fledgling company into its startup accelerator in early 2009 on the strength of the founders' scrappiness as much as the idea itself. Sequoia Capital led a $600,000 seed round shortly after, and Air Bed and Breakfast was renamed Airbnb.

What followed was one of the more improbable scaling stories in tech: a company built by two designers and an engineer, with no hospitality-industry background, that eventually persuaded millions of ordinary homeowners to rent out spare rooms — and eventually entire homes — to strangers, professionalizing an activity that had previously existed mostly as couch-surfing and classified ads. Airbnb went public in December 2020 in the middle of a pandemic that had gutted travel and briefly threatened to kill the company outright — the same year Chesky, in a memo now taught in business schools, laid off a quarter of Airbnb's staff and personally apologized for the human cost of a crisis entirely outside the company's control, then rebuilt the business around a leaner, more resilient version of its original idea.

Chesky remains CEO today, and Airbnb's founding myth has become a fixture of startup lore precisely because it wasn't glamorous: no elite technical breakthrough, no venture-backed head start, just two broke designers, an air mattress, and a willingness to sell novelty cereal to keep the lights on for one more month.
