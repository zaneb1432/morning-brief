**Google's Gemini 4 Argon moves from restricted preview to developers.** Announced September 30, Argon first went only to Google's internal teams, the US Government and defenders in its Fairwind cybersecurity program, then reportedly became generally available around October 13. Launch pricing is $2/$10 per million input/output tokens until November 30, doubling December 1. Google's benchmark table (18 tests against GPT-6 Astra, Claude Opus 5.5 and Claude Fable 5.1) shows Argon leading or tying on roughly 12–13, including a state-of-the-art 77.9% on DeepSWE v1.1, but it trails on FrontierSWE v2 and Terminal-Bench 4.0. Why it matters: it is a credible Google comeback bid, but the benchmark-vs-practice gap is the story to watch. ## 4. FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY — Madam C.J. Walker (1867–1919)

Sarah Breedlove was born on a Louisiana cotton plantation to formerly enslaved parents, orphaned at seven, married at fourteen, and widowed by twenty. She worked as a washerwoman in St. Louis for about $1.50 a day, keeping her daughter A'Lelia in school. In the 1890s she developed a scalp condition that caused hair loss; after working as a sales agent for Annie Turnbo Malone's Poro Company, and by her account after a dream, she developed her own hair-care formula.

In 1906 she married Charles J. Walker, a newspaper advertising man, and rebranded as Madam C.J. Walker. The business model was as innovative as the product: a "Walker System" of treatments sold door to door by trained agents, plus mail order, with a factory in Indianapolis from 1910. She built a network of tens of thousands of Black women "beauty culturists" who earned independent incomes at a time when almost no other path existed, and she funded Walker colleges to train them.

She was also an organizer and philanthropist: she hosted national conventions of her agents, gave to the NAACP's anti-lynching campaign, and supported Black education and the YMCA. Widely reported as the first self-made female millionaire in America, her wealth is debated by historians, but her business and influence are not. She died in 1919 at her Irvington, New York estate, Villa Lewaro. Lesson for founders: she built distribution, training and brand as one system, turning customers into a sales force and a community.
