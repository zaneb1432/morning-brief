## 4. FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY — Margaret Rudkin (Pepperidge Farm)

*(Written from background knowledge, not fresh research. First edition in the archive, so no repeats.)*

Margaret Rudkin (1897–1967) was a New York-born stockbroker's secretary who, in her late thirties, built one of America's best-known food brands from a home kitchen. In the 1930s she and her husband Henry, a stockbroker, lived on a Connecticut estate they named Pepperidge Farm after its pepperidge (black gum) trees. When one of her sons developed severe allergies and asthma, a doctor suggested a diet free of commercial additives. Unable to find bread she trusted, she baked stone-ground whole wheat loaves herself.

The family and the doctor liked it, and she began selling loaves to a local grocer, then to specialty stores in the region. Demand outran the kitchen. She moved production into a converted garage in the late 1930s, and by the 1940s the company had a proper bakery and a growing reputation for premium, no-preservatives bread priced well above rivals. The pitch worked because the product matched it: she insisted on quality ingredients and refused to cut corners as sales grew. Henry joined the business, and Pepperidge Farm expanded into other baked goods and later cookies.

In 1961 she sold the company to Campbell Soup in a stock deal reported at roughly $28 million, staying on as a leader of the business. She also wrote a bestselling cookbook. *Lesson:* the strongest consumer brands often begin as a founder's solution to a personal problem, and premium positioning only holds if quality never slips as you scale.
