**Apple is building an autonomous agent that uses apps, browses the web and writes code (reported Nov 19).** Bloomberg's Mark Gurman found the project in a job listing for a **senior engineering manager**, posted **Nov 17**. The listing describes an agent that would navigate devices, work across apps, browse the web, run commands, and **write and debug code** for the user. The role works with the **Siri**, UI frameworks and **Apple Foundation Models** teams. The listing calls for clear permissions, **sandboxing** and visibility into what the agent is doing. Bloomberg says the work is early and that Apple has considered launching **as soon as late 2027**. The listing gives no date and doesn't say whether the agent will ship inside Siri. **Why it matters:** Agents that operate a device are where consumer AI competition is now: Meta's Muse, OpenAI's Dots, Anthropic's Claude Duet and Google's Gemini "Tasks" are all already shipping. Apple controls the operating system and the permission layer on more than 2 billion devices. If it builds a first-party agent, third-party agents on iPhone and Mac could end up depending on whatever access Apple allows them. That is the threat investors priced into ANT and Meta on Thursday night. The timeline is long, though, and Apple's recent AI launches have mostly come late. **Trump threatens a "tremendous tax" on U.S. companies that build AI data centers overseas (Nov 20).** On Friday the President said American companies that build data centers abroad will face heavy financial penalties, framing it as keeping the U.S. ahead of China in AI. Reports differ on how far he went. NBC quotes him as saying "we're not going to allow our companies to build data centers outside of our country." CNBC's version is softer: companies "can, but we're going to put a tremendous tax on them." He gave **no rate, legal mechanism or timeline**, and one report says his own advisers hadn't been briefed. The threat lands on **Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta and Oracle**, which run foreign facilities partly because data-residency and sovereignty rules (in the EU, India, the Gulf and elsewhere) require local processing. **Why it matters:** For now this is a statement, not a policy. Combined with this week's chip rule closing the offshore-rental loophole, though, the direction is clear: Washington wants frontier compute built on U.S. soil. If it turns into an actual tax, it would conflict with the "sovereign AI" deals hyperscalers have been signing abroad, including the Gulf campuses, and with EU data-boundary commitments. Expect lobbying immediately, and expect foreign governments to ask whether their U.S. data-center agreements still hold. **Gemini on Mac can now operate your desktop apps, in a background window (Nov 19).** Google added **computer use** to the Gemini app for macOS. Users add **"@Computer use"** to a prompt, and Gemini runs the task in an **isolated background window**, so the user's own screen and cursor stay free. Users choose which apps Gemini can access, can stop it at any time, and must approve sensitive actions. Google warns it can make mistakes, including deleting or sharing files. It requires **Apple Silicon and macOS Sequoia 15+**, and it's limited to **Google AI Plus, Pro and Ultra** subscribers in the **U.S., in English**. Google didn't name the underlying model, publish usage limits or give success-rate benchmarks. **Why it matters:** Google is shipping a desktop agent on Apple's own hardware a day before reports that Apple is still hiring someone to build one. The background-window design is the interesting part: the agent doesn't take over the user's machine while it works, which addresses the biggest usability problem with earlier computer-use tools. Until Google publishes reliability numbers, assume it's best for repetitive, low-stakes chores. ## 4. FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: Earl Bakken (Medtronic)

**A TV-repair kid, a garage, a city-wide blackout, and the first wearable pacemaker, built in four weeks.**

*Written from background knowledge, not this run's searches. Dates and figures are as I remember them from standard histories of Medtronic, so check them before citing. After yesterday's software founder, today's is a hardware engineer whose company most people have never heard of, even though millions of people have its products in their bodies.*

Earl Bakken was born in Minneapolis in 1924. As a child he watched the 1931 *Frankenstein* over and over. What stayed with him wasn't the monster but the idea that electricity could start a life. He became the neighborhood electronics kid, fixing radios and building gadgets (by family legend, including a device that shocked classmates who touched his locker). He served as a radar technician in the Navy during World War II, then studied electrical engineering at the University of Minnesota while working at Northwestern Hospital, where he was regularly asked to fix broken lab equipment.

That side job was the business. In **1949**, Bakken and his brother-in-law **Palmer Hermundslie** started **Medtronic** in Hermundslie's family garage in northeast Minneapolis, repairing and reselling hospital electronics. By the company's own telling, revenue in the first month was **$8**. For most of the 1950s Medtronic was a small service and distribution shop that built custom devices when researchers asked for them.

The researcher who asked most often was **Dr. C. Walton Lillehei**, the University of Minnesota's pioneering open-heart surgeon. Many of his young patients came out of surgery with heart block and needed external pacing, from large AC-powered units on carts that had to be plugged into the wall. On **October 31, 1957**, a power failure hit Minneapolis. The outage became the founding story of the medical-device industry: patients depended on wall current, and there was no backup. Lillehei asked Bakken for something better. Bakken found a transistorized metronome circuit in *Popular Electronics*, modified it, and within about **four weeks** had a **battery-powered pacemaker** small enough to tape to a child's chest. It was used on a patient the next day. Bakken didn't patent it. He saw it as a custom job.

The bigger opportunity came in **1960**, when Medtronic got rights to the first reliable **fully implantable pacemaker**, developed by Buffalo surgeon William Chardack and engineer **Wilson Greatbatch**. Demand jumped, and Medtronic nearly went under. Its finances weren't built for a product business, and by **1962** it was close to bankruptcy until local investors stepped in. Around then Bakken wrote down what became the **Medtronic Mission**, a six-point statement starting with "to contribute to human welfare by application of biomedical engineering in the research, design, manufacture, and sale of instruments or appliances that alleviate pain, restore health, and extend life." It's still in use. Every new employee gets a **medallion** engraved with it, often from Bakken himself while he was alive, and each year the company's holiday party brings in patients whose lives its devices saved to speak to employees.

Bakken ran the company until the mid-1970s and stayed chairman into the late 1980s. During that time Medtronic grew from pacemakers into heart valves, neurostimulators, insulin pumps and spinal devices. Bakken also helped establish the idea that a startup could build a medical-device business through close collaboration with clinicians, which is how Minneapolis's "Medical Alley" cluster began. He retired to Hawaii's Big Island, where he helped found **North Hawaii Community Hospital**. He also founded **The Bakken Museum** in Minneapolis, which is devoted to electricity and life, Frankenstein included. He received several pacemakers and other Medtronic devices himself, a point he enjoyed making. He died in **2018** at 94. Medtronic, now headquartered in Ireland after its 2015 Covidien deal, has annual revenue in the tens of billions of dollars.

**Takeaways for founders:**
- **A service business can be the way to find a product.** Medtronic's repair work put Bakken in the room when a surgeon needed something new. Its "custom jobs" were how it learned what customers needed.
- **A crisis shows what's missing.** The 1957 blackout didn't invent the need for portable pacing. It made the risk impossible to ignore. Founders who are close enough to see what breaks during an emergency find the most urgent problems.
- **Growing fast can nearly kill you.** The implantable pacemaker almost bankrupted Medtronic. A breakthrough product needs working capital and operations ready to support it.
- **A written mission works best when people use it.** Bakken's statement lasted because it was used every year: in the medallion, at the patient-story party, and in decisions about what to build.
