## FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: An Wang

Long before Silicon Valley became shorthand for tech founders, An Wang built one of America's great postwar technology companies — and then watched it collapse almost as fast as it had risen, in a cautionary tale about succession and paradigm shifts that still resonates in the AI era.

Wang was born in 1920 outside Shanghai, the son of an English teacher. He graduated from Shanghai Jiao Tong University with a degree in electrical engineering in 1940, during the Japanese occupation of China, and in 1945 traveled to the United States to pursue graduate study at Harvard, earning a PhD in applied physics in 1948. At Harvard he worked with Howard Aiken on the Mark IV, one of the earliest fully electronic computers — hands-on experience with computing hardware at a moment when the entire field was barely a decade old.

In 1951, with little capital and no institutional backing, Wang founded Wang Laboratories as a sole proprietorship, working out of a small office in Boston. His breakthrough came from research into magnetic-core memory, a method of storing computer data using tiny magnetized rings that became the dominant memory technology of the 1950s and '60s. In 1955, Wang sold the core-memory patent to IBM for $500,000 — enough capital to incorporate the company properly with a former schoolmate, Ge-Yao Chu.

Wang Laboratories grew steadily through the 1960s on the back of desktop calculators, then made its defining bet in the 1970s: word processing. Wang's dedicated word-processing systems, built around the Zilog Z80 processor, became a fixture of American offices, letting secretaries and lawyers edit and format text on a screen before printing — a genuine productivity leap over typewriters. By the early 1980s, Wang Laboratories was doing $3 billion a year in revenue with more than 33,000 employees, and An Wang himself was one of the richest immigrants in America, a celebrated example of technological achievement against the backdrop of a country still absorbing waves of Asian immigration.

But the company's success was built entirely on a category — dedicated word processors — that the general-purpose personal computer was about to make obsolete. Wang was slow to pivot the company fully toward PCs and PC-compatible software, and when he prepared to retire in 1986, he made the decision, over the objections of his board, to hand the company to his son Fred Wang rather than a professional outside successor. Fred struggled to navigate the PC transition and the company's mounting debt; An Wang was forced to remove his own son from the CEO role in 1989, just a year before his death in 1990. Wang Laboratories filed for bankruptcy in 1992 and was eventually acquired by Getronics in 1999.

An Wang's story endures as one of the sharper lessons in tech history: brilliant, patentable engineering and a genuinely dominant product category are not enough to survive a platform shift, and succession decisions made for personal rather than competitive reasons can undo decades of work in a few years. It's a story worth remembering at a moment when today's AI labs — many led by their own founders, racing toward IPOs, betting billions on which architecture wins — are making analogous bets about which technology paradigm will still matter in a decade.
