## 1. Tech & AI News

**OpenAI ships GPT-6-Sol, begins rollout to ChatGPT Work and Codex.** OpenAI made GPT-6-Sol available through its API on September 22 and started a gradual rollout to eligible paid ChatGPT Work and Codex plans. The release lands amid an unusually crowded month for frontier models — Anthropic shipped Claude Fable 5.1 and Mythos 5.1 on September 1, Google released Gemini 3.8 Flash on September 2, and Meta followed the same evening with Muse Spark 1.3 — underscoring how compressed the frontier-model release cadence has become as labs race to ship incremental capability gains rather than wait for headline-grabbing generational leaps. ([llm-stats.com](https://llm-stats.com/ai-news))

**OpenAI will let outside groups audit models *during* training, not just before release.** In a Tuesday announcement, OpenAI said it will open technical safety assessments to third-party organizations — reportedly including METR and Redwood Research — across training, evaluation, and deployment, rather than restricting outside review to pre-release capability testing as has been standard practice. The company named four priority areas: safety cases spanning training and deployment, evaluation of critical safeguards, capability evaluations tied to its Preparedness Framework, and independent investigation of misalignment incidents. This matters because it's a meaningful concession on transparency from the lab that has faced the most pressure over opaque safety practices, and it sets a precedent other labs will now be measured against. ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-22/openai-to-let-outside-groups-evaluate-ai-models-at-earlier-phase))

**UN Security Council convenes a rare AI-safety session with the labs building the technology.** France, holding the Council's rotating presidency this month, has convened all 15 members for a Wednesday, September 23 session on AI and international security. Sam Altman and Anthropic's Dario Amodei (appearing remotely) are both expected to brief the Council, alongside Hugging Face CEO Clément Delangue and Yoshua Bengio in his role co-chairing the UN's Independent International Scientific Panel on AI. Notably, China's DeepSeek and Moonshot have also been invited to make statements — though DeepSeek founder Liang Wenfeng is not expected to attend personally. It's a striking moment: the first time this specific cross-section of US and Chinese AI labs has been asked to jointly address the world's top security body on catastrophic and geopolitical AI risk. ([Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/379008/un-security-council-ai-risks-anthropic-openai-deepseek), [Quartz](https://qz.com/altman-amodei-un-security-council-ai-safety-092226))

**Alphabet's Intrinsic open-sources its industrial robotics stack.** At ROSCon 2026 in Toronto, Google's robotics unit Intrinsic released Intrinsic Core — a ROS 2-compatible set of capabilities including real-time robot control, pose estimation, motion and grasp planning, simulation, camera calibration, and preconfigured drivers — under the permissive Apache 2.0 license. Crucially, these are described as the same components Intrinsic uses in live manufacturing deployments today, not a stripped-down demo version. Intrinsic also introduced an Open Machine Tending reference design adaptable to hardware from Fanuc and Universal Robots. This is a significant bet that commoditizing the robotics software substrate — the way Android did for mobile — will accelerate the "physical AI" buildout faster than Alphabet could manage by keeping the stack proprietary. ([Unite.AI](https://www.unite.ai/intrinsic-open-sources-core-robotics-capabilities-at-roscon-2026/), [Intrinsic](https://www.intrinsic.ai/blog/posts/introducing-intrinsic-core))

**OpenAI to shut down the Sora API on September 24.** OpenAI confirmed it will discontinue developer API access to its Sora video-generation tool this Thursday, ending third-party integrations built on it. The move is a reminder that even at frontier labs, product lines get pruned quickly when they don't fit the current strategic focus — likely folding video capability more tightly into ChatGPT/Sora's consumer app rather than supporting it as a standalone developer platform. ## 3. Venture Capital

## 4. Founder Biography: An Wang

Most people who've never heard of An Wang have still used something he made possible. Wang was the co-inventor of magnetic-core memory — for decades, the way every computer on Earth stored data before semiconductor memory took over — and the founder of Wang Laboratories, one of the defining American technology companies of the 1970s and early 1980s.

Wang was born in Shanghai in 1920, the second of five children in a middle-class family, during a period of enormous upheaval in China. His father taught him English at home, and he entered formal schooling at age six, straight into the third grade. He earned an engineering degree from Chiao Tung University in Shanghai in 1940, then left for the United States, where he completed a master's and a PhD in applied physics at Harvard by 1948. At Harvard, he worked under Howard Aiken on the Mark IV computer — one of the earliest fully electronic computing machines — and it was there, wrestling with the problem of how to make a computer remember information reliably, that Wang co-invented the "pulse transfer controlling device" with a former schoolmate, Way-Dong Woo. The innovation — writing data immediately after reading it — was the missing piece that made magnetic-core memory practical, and it would become the standard memory technology of the computing industry for the next two decades.

In 1951, Harvard scaled back its computing research program, and Wang found himself without an institutional home for his work. Rather than look for another job, he started his own company. With just $600 in savings, he founded Wang Laboratories in June 1951 — for a while, he was its only employee. Five years later, in 1956, he sold his core-memory patent to IBM for $400,000, capital that let him grow the business into calculators and, eventually, office computers and word-processing systems.

That bet paid off spectacularly: by the 1970s and into the early 1980s, Wang Laboratories was one of the most successful technology companies in America, a pioneer of dedicated word-processing machines that briefly made "Wang" synonymous with office computing the way "Xerox" was synonymous with copying. Wang himself held around 40 patents and became one of the wealthiest and most prominent Chinese-American businessmen of his generation, a significant figure in Massachusetts civic life and philanthropy.

The company's decline is as instructive as its rise. Wang badly misjudged the shift toward IBM-compatible personal computers, and as revenue eroded through the 1980s, the succession decision he made — handing the presidency to his son Frederick in 1986 — is widely cited as a costly misstep. By 1989, with the company in serious trouble, An Wang returned and fired his own son in an attempt to save it. It wasn't enough: Wang Laboratories filed for bankruptcy in 1992, two years after An Wang's death, and was eventually acquired by the Dutch firm Getronics in 1999.

Wang's story doesn't have the clean arc of a Silicon Valley founder myth — it's a story of a brilliant inventor who built a genuine industrial giant from $600 and a physics insight, then watched it come apart partly through a very ordinary founder's mistake: choosing loyalty over readiness in deciding who comes next. It's worth remembering both halves.
