## FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: Madam C.J. Walker (1867–1919)

Sarah Breedlove was born December 23, 1867, on a Louisiana cotton plantation, the first child in her family born free after Emancipation. Orphaned at seven, married at 14 to escape an abusive brother-in-law, and widowed at about 20 with a toddler daughter, A'Lelia, she worked as a washerwoman in St. Louis for roughly $1.50 a day.

In the 1890s she began losing her hair to scalp disease, common among women who lacked running water and washed rarely. She experimented with remedies, worked as a sales agent for Annie Malone's Poro hair-care company, and in 1905 moved to Denver, where she married newspaperman Charles J. Walker and rebranded as "Madam C.J. Walker." Her formula and scalp-care system, sold first door to door, took off.

Her real innovation was distribution and training. She built a network of thousands of "Walker Agents," mostly Black women, and trained them in the Walker System at her Lelia College and, from 1910, at a factory and headquarters in Indianapolis. Agents earned far more than the domestic and agricultural work otherwise available to them, and she held conventions to reward the top sellers, among the first national gatherings of businesswomen of any race. She also spent heavily on philanthropy, including the NAACP's anti-lynching campaign, the YMCA, and the Tuskegee Institute.

By her death on May 25, 1919, she was among the wealthiest self-made women in America, and often described as the first self-made female millionaire in the country. Historians note that the "millionaire" claim is debated and her estate's value was likely below that mark at her death, though A'Lelia and the business grew afterward. The lesson for founders: she sold a product, but built a distribution and training system that was the true moat, and she did it while barred from most conventional capital.
