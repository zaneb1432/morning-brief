## 1. 🤖 TECH & AI NEWS

### Jensen Huang is back as Nvidia CEO, 12 days after leaving
Nvidia's board voted unanimously on Monday, Nov. 23, to reinstate Jensen Huang as president, CEO and director, effective immediately. His exit had followed a scandal over his personal conduct. CFO Colette Kress, who had been interim CEO for 11 days, goes back to running finance only. The White House said "President Trump was totally involved" in bringing Huang back. That is an unusual amount of political involvement in the leadership of the world's most valuable chipmaker, and it suggests Washington now treats Nvidia's leadership as a national-interest matter. Fortune points out that Nvidia still faces an SEC probe and other investigations, so the story isn't over. Kress was reportedly left out of the board's Sunday discussions about reinstatement, and her position is now the one to watch.
🔗 [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/23/nvidia-jensen-huang-returns-colette-kress-cfo.html) · [Fortune: the SEC probe and Kress](https://fortune.com/2026/11/23/with-jensen-huang-back-ceo-nvidias-sec-probe-puts-cfo-colette-kress-in-spotlight/)

### Amazon buys Decart's optimization stack for a reported $7.5B
Amazon agreed to acquire the technology behind Israeli startup Decart's Optimization Stack, along with its founders and engineering team. Amazon did not take Decart's models or its world-model work. Amazon didn't disclose a price; Bloomberg and others put it at about $7.5B. The tech will go into Annapurna Labs, Amazon's chip unit, to make AI workloads run faster on Trainium. The bet is that Nvidia's real advantage is its software (CUDA and the tooling around it) more than its silicon, so better compilers and kernels could close much of the gap. Amazon is also preparing to sell Trainium chips more widely, so the timing fits. Anthropic had reportedly looked at buying Decart for about $6B. The deal still needs regulatory approval.
🔗 [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/23/amazon-snaps-up-decarts-ai-optimization-tech-for-7-5b-as-it-prepares-to-sell-trainium-chips/) · [TechCrunch analysis](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/24/amazons-decart-acquisition-is-a-bet-on-using-software-to-narrow-the-gap-with-nvidia-in-the-ai-chip-race/)

### Anthropic launches Claude Ship, a set of agents for pull requests
Anthropic bundled its existing multi-agent **Code Review** (already generally available) with two new beta agents. **PR Steward** fixes failing CI checks, resolves merge conflicts and responds to review feedback. **Claude Approvals** approves routine changes based on team policies, which teams can write as natural-language rules. A new `/ship` command in Claude Code starts the workflow. It's available to Team and Enterprise customers whose code is on GitHub, and a review costs roughly $15–25 on average, billed by tokens. The notable part is that AI now sits on both sides of code review: it writes the code and can also approve it. That raises the question of who is accountable for what gets merged. Anthropic itself says Ship can miss bugs and should be one layer of review, not the only one.
🔗 [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/23/anthropic-launches-claude-ship-letting-ai-agents-review-fix-and-approve-code-changes/) · [Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/11/24/anthropic-claude-ship-beta/)

### DeepMind's agents find quantum algorithms up to 290× cheaper, with proofs
Google DeepMind and Google Quantum AI put teams of Gemini 4 agents to work on quantum algorithms. The teams worked independently and met at scheduled "virtual conferences" to share ideas. Every candidate circuit had to come with a machine-checked proof in the Lean theorem prover. The largest gain was a 290× cost reduction for an arcsin routine used to load numbers into quantum states. A fusion simulation became about 11.8× cheaper, and photochemistry simulations about 8× cheaper. Formal verification matters here because quantum circuits are hard to test by running them, so a proof does the job testing normally would. The caveat is that even the improved algorithms still need more resources than early error-corrected machines are expected to have.
🔗 [Google DeepMind blog](https://deepmind.google/blog/finding-faster-quantum-algorithms-with-agents-and-formal-verification/) · [arXiv paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.09822)

### Oregon and Virginia sue SpaceX over Grok's sexualized deepfakes
Virginia AG Jay Jones and Oregon AG Dan Rayfield filed separate consumer-protection suits on Nov. 23 over Grok's sexualized deepfakes. Grok's maker, formerly xAI, is now SpaceX Super Intelligence. The states say the company called Grok and X safe when the safeguards it described weren't working. Virginia cites research estimating Grok posted millions of sexualized images on X over two months, more than 20,000 of them depicting children. Both states want injunctions, civil penalties and disgorgement, and Virginia also wants restitution for consumers. They are reportedly the first states to sue, and the legal approach matters: they're using deceptive-practices law instead of waiting for AI-specific statutes. Other states could copy that approach quickly.
🔗 [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/23/oregon-virginia-spacex-grok-deepfake-lawsuits.html) · [CyberScoop](https://cyberscoop.com/virginia-oregon-sue-spacex-grok-deepfakes/)

## 2. 🚀 STARTUP WORLD

### Sierra raises $1.75B at $30B, double its valuation from May
Sierra, Bret Taylor's AI customer-service company, announced a $1.75B Series F at a $30B valuation, led by Greenoaks and Lightspeed. Benchmark, Sequoia, GV and Tiger Global also took part. Its last round in May valued it at $15.8B. Sierra says annualized recurring revenue topped $400M in October, after adding more than $150M in Q3 alone. That figure isn't audited. The money will go to its agent platform and to hiring more salespeople. This is the strongest evidence yet that customer service is where enterprises are actually paying for AI agents at scale. Bloomberg described the round as "finalizing," so it may not have formally closed.

### Cognition is in talks to raise at $75B, two months after a $48B round
Bloomberg reports that Cognition, the maker of the Devin coding agent, is in talks to raise $2B or more at a $75B valuation. Avenir, Accel, Lightspeed, Lux and Bessemer are named as possible investors. That would make three step-ups in one year: $26B in May, $48B in September, and now $75B. Investors are paying that because they believe AI coding is the first market where agents can take over entire jobs. The risk is that compute costs eat into margins. ## 4. 📖 FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: Ole Kirk Christiansen, the carpenter who started LEGO

In 1916, Ole Kirk Christiansen, a 25-year-old carpenter from rural Jutland, bought a woodworking shop in the Danish village of Billund. For more than a decade he made doors, windows, stepladders and ironing boards for local farmers. Then the Great Depression hit, and farmers stopped buying. In 1932 his wife Kirstine died, leaving him with four sons and a business that was failing.

To use up scrap wood, he started making toys: yo-yos, piggy banks, and pull-along ducks. His family thought it was a waste of time. In 1934 he gave the toys a name taken from the Danish words *leg godt*, meaning "play well": **LEGO**. Later it turned out that *lego* also means "I put together" in Latin, though he hadn't intended that. He was obsessive about quality. Company lore says that when his son Godtfred saved money by using two coats of varnish on a batch of toy ducks instead of three, Ole sent him back to the station at night to recover the shipment and finish the job. The motto he hung in the workshop is still the company's motto: *"Only the best is good enough."*

The business nearly ended more than once. A 1924 fire, started by his sons while playing in the shop, destroyed the workshop. On the night of March 20, 1942, an electrical fire burned down the factory along with all its stock and every drawing and template. Ole, by then in his fifties, rebuilt the factory by 1943 and redrew the designs by hand.

His biggest decision came in 1947. Plastic was new and widely seen as cheap, and Ole bought an injection-moulding machine imported from Britain. It reportedly cost about a year's profit, and it was the first of its kind in Denmark. In 1949 the company began selling "Automatic Binding Bricks," an adaptation of the Kiddicraft Self-Locking Bricks designed by Briton Hilary Fisher Page. Retailers sent them back, and critics said plastic would never replace wood. Ole kept making them anyway.

Godtfred took over more and more of the business. He came up with the idea of a "system of play," in which every piece fits with every other. In January 1958 the company filed a patent for the stud-and-tube coupling that makes LEGO bricks hold together firmly. Ole Kirk Christiansen died two months later, in March 1958, just as the invention that would make his company famous was finished. In 1960 the wooden-toy warehouse burned down too, and the family stopped making wooden toys altogether.

**What founders can take from this:** Ole changed his business three times: from carpentry to wooden toys, from wood to plastic, and from individual toys to a system of parts that fit together. Each change came in response to a crisis or to a new technology that most people dismissed. He held onto his standard of quality the whole time, but not onto any particular material or product. Being picky about quality while being willing to change everything else is a good lesson for anyone building today.
