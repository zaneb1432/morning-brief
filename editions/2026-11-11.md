## 4. FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY — Herb Kelleher (1931–2019), Southwest Airlines

The founding legend is that in 1966 a San Antonio lawyer and a client sketched a triangle on a cocktail napkin connecting Dallas, Houston and San Antonio. Kelleher later said the napkin was embellished, but the idea was real. The client, Rollin King, a pilot and investment adviser, wanted a cheap, frequent intrastate airline. Because it would fly only within Texas, it could avoid the federal Civil Aeronautics Board, which then set routes and fares for interstate flights. Kelleher, a New Jersey-born lawyer who had moved to Texas for his wife's family, reportedly answered, "Rollin, you're crazy. Let's do it."

Southwest's first battles were in court. Air Southwest was incorporated in 1967 and won Texas regulatory approval. The incumbents (Braniff, Trans-Texas and Continental) then sued to keep it out of the air. Kelleher argued the case himself, at times reportedly covering legal fees personally, and lost in the lower courts before winning at the Texas Supreme Court; the U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear an appeal. Southwest flew its first flights on June 18, 1971, with three Boeing 737s. Within months, cash pressure forced it to sell one of its four planes while keeping the same schedule. The fix became the airline's operating model: the 10-minute gate turnaround. One aircraft type, no assigned seats, point-to-point routes and fast turns let a small fleet fly far more hours per day than competitors' fleets.

Kelleher became chairman and then CEO in 1981, and he made culture a competitive weapon. His rule was employees first, customers second, shareholders third, on the logic that happy employees produce happy customers, who in turn reward shareholders. He showed up to hangars in costume, knew mechanics by name and smoked and drank Wild Turkey in public. In 1992, rather than fight a trademark dispute over the slogan "Just Plane Smart," he settled it with an arm-wrestling match against Stevens Aviation's CEO, which was promoted as "Malice in Dallas." He lost, and Stevens let Southwest keep using the slogan anyway. He also accepted constraints others would have fought, operating for decades under the Wright Amendment's limits on Love Field.

Results: Southwest was profitable every year from 1973 until the pandemic, a 47-year streak with no equal in US aviation. It avoided layoffs through 9/11 and became the largest US domestic carrier by passengers. Economists named the "Southwest effect" after the way fares fell when it entered a market. Kelleher stepped down as CEO in 2001 and died in January 2019 at 87.

**Lessons for founders:** (1) A regulatory or legal fight can be the moat: the years the incumbents spent trying to keep Southwest grounded also hardened its resolve and identity. (2) A constraint forced on you, like selling a plane, can become your structural advantage if you redesign the system around it. (3) Culture is cheapest to build early and hardest to copy later. Competitors could copy the 737s, but they never copied the people.
