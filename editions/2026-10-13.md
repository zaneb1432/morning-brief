## 1. TECH & AI NEWS

**Meta ships "Muse Wonder," its bid for frontier-model parity (Oct 12).** Meta released Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full, saying they perform at the level of OpenAI's ChatGPT and Anthropic's Claude models. They are available now, with the paid tier getting the strongest model. CNBC frames the launch as a test of whether consumers will pay for Meta's AI. Mistral calls it the strongest open-weight model from outside China. Independent testing is less flattering: Vals AI ranks it #32 of 44 on its index. Sources: [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/06/mistral-ai-model-le-chonk.html), [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/mistral-releases-large-4-a-1-trillion-parameter-open-weight-ai-model), [Vals AI](https://vals.ai/models/mistralai_mistral-large-4).

Why it matters: it comes amid a US fight over federal preemption of state AI laws and an EU AI Act regime whose general-purpose-model obligations reportedly began Aug 2. ## 4. FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY — Madam C.J. Walker (1867–1919)

Sarah Breedlove was born on a Louisiana cotton plantation in December 1867, to parents who had been enslaved; she was the first in her family born free. Orphaned at seven, she worked in the fields, married at 14, and was widowed by 20 with a young daughter, A'Lelia. She moved to St. Louis and worked for years as a washerwoman, earning about a dollar a day.

In the 1890s she began losing her hair to scalp disease, common among women at the time because of poor sanitation, harsh products and infrequent washing. She experimented with remedies, worked as a sales agent for Annie Turnbo Malone's Poro Company, and in 1905 moved to Denver. There she developed her own hair-care formula and, after marrying newspaper man Charles J. Walker, rebranded herself Madam C.J. Walker, a name that sounded more like a business than her own.

Her model combined product, system and people. She sold door to door, trained a network of "beauty culturists" in her method, and built a mail-order business. She set up headquarters and a factory in Indianapolis in 1910, and founded the Lelia College to train agents. Her sales force of thousands of Black women earned independent incomes at a time when few other jobs paid as well. She ran national conventions, among the first for women entrepreneurs, with prizes for sales and for charitable giving. She also gave generously to the NAACP, the Black YMCA and anti-lynching efforts, and she used her wealth to advance civil rights.

By 1919 she had built a large business and a mansion, Villa Lewaro, in Irvington, New York. She died that May at 51. She is widely described as one of the first self-made female millionaires in America, though historians note the claim is debated, and her fortune's exact size at death is uncertain. Lesson for founders: she built a distribution network and brand, and trained her customers to become salespeople, long before platforms made that standard.
