## FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY — Chester Carlson (1906–1968), inventor of xerography

Chester Carlson grew up poor in California; his father was crippled by arthritis and his mother died of tuberculosis while he was a teenager, and he worked through school to fund his physics degree at Caltech. Graduating in 1930 into the Depression, he mailed out some 82 job applications and landed at Bell Labs, then moved to a patent department, where the drudgery of hand-copying documents (and his own arthritis) convinced him there had to be a better way to duplicate paper.

Working evenings, he developed "electrophotography." On October 22, 1938, in a rented room in Astoria, Queens, with physicist Otto Kornei, he charged a sulfur-coated zinc plate, exposed it to a glass slide inked "10-22-38 ASTORIA," dusted it with lycopodium powder and pressed wax paper to it. The first xerographic copy was born. Then came years of rejection: more than 20 companies, including IBM and Kodak, passed, seeing no need to replace carbon paper.

In 1944 the nonprofit Battelle Memorial Institute took it on, and in 1947 a small Rochester firm, Haloid, licensed it. The company, renamed Xerox, launched the 914 copier in 1959, which became one of the best-selling industrial products in US history and turned Xerox into a giant. Carlson became wealthy but lived modestly, quietly giving away a large share of his fortune (reportedly more than $100M) to causes including peace and civil-rights groups, and explored Zen Buddhism. The lesson: a clear problem, a decade of persistence through institutional indifference, and the patience to find the one partner who says yes.
