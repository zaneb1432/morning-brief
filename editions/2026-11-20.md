**Google launches Gemini 4 Neon, a mid-tier model it says matches Opus 5.5 on coding at a lower price (Nov 18).** Yesterday this was a code-string leak. Now it's official. **Gemini 4 Neon** sits between **Carbon**, the flagship, and **Hydrogen**, the fast and cheap tier, and replaces Argon. It's generally available in the Gemini API as `gemini-4-neon` with a **1M-token context window**, priced at **$3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output tokens**. In the Gemini app it's available to AI Pro and Ultra subscribers. Google's own comparisons show Neon ahead of **Claude Opus 5.5** and **GPT-6.2 Sol** on most listed benchmarks, including a score of 61 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index. Opus 5.5 still leads on **Terminal-Bench 4.1** and **FrontierCode 2.0**. VentureBeat describes it as Opus-level coding at about 25% lower token prices. Glean, a launch customer, says Neon matched or beat Carbon on quality at **3x the speed and 60% of the cost**. *(All benchmarks are Google-reported. OpenAI's GPT-6.2 Sol and Microsoft's MAI Code 2 came first. The "good-enough frontier" tier is now around $3/$15 or less, and that puts pressure on the margins Anthropic's new public shareholders just paid for. For builders, the default model for agent loops is now a price-performance decision you'll need to revisit every month. **Palo Alto Networks will buy Applied Compute for $4.5B in stock, about 1.4x the price set in its funding round two weeks ago (Nov 18).** Palo Alto announced the all-stock deal alongside its fiscal Q1 earnings. **Applied Compute** was founded in 2025 by former OpenAI researchers **Yash Patil, Rhythm Garg and Linden Li**. It builds a platform for **post-training and serving open-weight models**. It reportedly passed a **$100M annualized revenue run rate** in under 18 months, and on **Nov 2 it raised $350M at a $3.25B post-money valuation**. Patil will become Palo Alto's **co-Chief AI Officer**. Nikesh Arora's pitch is that enterprises can fine-tune and deploy open models "with security built in," in public cloud or sovereign environments. The deal is expected to close in Palo Alto's fiscal Q3, pending regulatory approval. Palo Alto also raised its full-year revenue guidance to **$14.52–14.57B**, up from $14.10–14.20B. **Why it matters:** Security vendors want to own the model layer, not just protect it. Open-weight models trained on a customer's own data, in a controlled environment, are what regulated and sovereign buyers want, and Palo Alto is making a direct bid for that business. Investors in the November round got a 1.4x return in about 16 days, and the deal will be cited as proof that AI-infrastructure M&A can pay off quickly. ## 2. STARTUP WORLD

**Applied Compute's exit, from founding to a $4.5B sale in about 18 months, is the startup story of the week.** It's covered in full above. The founder-level takeaway: three ex-OpenAI researchers chose a narrow, unglamorous niche, post-training and inference for *open* models, reached $100M ARR, and sold to a strategic buyer instead of chasing a frontier-lab valuation. For the many "post-training as a service" startups that raised in 2025–26, there is now a clear exit path and a price for it.

**Boltz raises a $110M Series A to sell drug-discovery foundation models "by the token" (Nov 18).** **Bessemer** led, with **a16z** (which led the seed), **Amplify** and **Zetta** participating. The valuation wasn't disclosed. Boltz started as an MIT research project. Its co-founders **Gabriele Corso, Jeremy Wohlwend and Saro Passaro** built the open-source **Boltz** structure-prediction models, then **BoltzGen** for binder design. The company now sells **BoltzMol-1** and **BoltzProt-1** through an API and a **Boltz Lab** workspace for chemists and biologists, and it charges by "molecular tokens." a16z says weekly molecular-token use has grown about **50x in four months**. It already has a data partnership with **Pfizer**. *(The growth figures are investor-reported.)* **Why it matters:** Isomorphic's $5B round this week was the vertically integrated version of AI drug discovery, where you own the pipeline. Boltz is the opposite model: be the picks-and-shovels model provider, priced like an LLM API, and sell to every pharma company. If usage-based pricing works in biology, it's a much more capital-efficient business than running clinical trials. ## 4. FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY — N. R. Narayana Murthy (b. 1946), Infosys

*After yesterday's Hollywood cookie promoter, today's founder is an engineer who built an Indian software company with roughly $1,100 of seed money borrowed from his wife and a rule that every deal had to be clean. Infosys helped make offshore IT services a global industry.*

**A schoolteacher's son with a scholarship he couldn't use.** Nagavara Ramarao Narayana Murthy was born on **August 20, 1946**, in Sidlaghatta, in what is now Karnataka. His father was a schoolteacher, and the family was large and short of money. He qualified for admission to IIT but couldn't afford to go, so he studied electrical engineering at the **National Institute of Engineering in Mysore** (1967). He later earned a master's degree at **IIT Kanpur** (1969), where, as he tells it, a visiting computer scientist's talk about software convinced him that computing was the future.

**Paris, a border jail and a change of politics.** His first notable job was as chief systems programmer at **IIM Ahmedabad**, where he worked on one of India's early time-sharing systems. He then worked in **Paris** in the early 1970s, when he was, by his own account, a committed leftist. On the overland trip home he was detained for several days at a communist-bloc border, an episode he has often described. He came away convinced that wealth has to be created before it can be shared, a view he later called "compassionate capitalism." *(He tells the story with some variation over the years. The border is usually given as the Yugoslav–Bulgarian one.)*

**A failed first company, then Rs 10,000 from Sudha.** His first company, **Softronics**, closed after about a year and a half. He then joined **Patni Computer Systems** in Pune. In **1981** he left with six colleagues, **Nandan Nilekani, S. Gopalakrishnan, S. D. Shibulal, K. Dinesh, N. S. Raghavan and Ashok Arora**, to start **Infosys**. The seed money was **Rs 10,000**, then about $1,100, from his wife **Sudha Murty**, an engineer and later a well-known author. She has said she had Rs 10,250 saved, kept 250 and gave him the rest, "a little bit of risk" given that Softronics had failed. The company started in the couple's flat in Pune and moved to Bangalore in 1983.

**The licence-raj decade.** Running a software company in 1980s India was very hard. It reportedly took about a year to get a telephone line and years to get import licences for computers, and every foreign trip needed central bank approval for currency. Infosys survived on onsite contract work for U.S. clients. Around 1989, after a joint venture ended and co-founder Ashok Arora left, the remaining founders discussed selling the company for about $1M. Murthy told them that if they wanted out, he would buy them out himself, and none of them left. India's **1991 economic liberalization** then removed most of the restrictions that had held the company back.

**Building a model, not just a company.** Infosys went public in India in **1993**. The IPO was **undersubscribed**, and Morgan Stanley picked up shares. Murthy used the listing to introduce **broad employee stock options**, which created thousands of rupee millionaires among drivers, clerks and engineers and became part of Indian IT's culture. In **1999**, Infosys became the **first India-registered company listed on Nasdaq**. It popularized the **Global Delivery Model**, which splits work between client sites and lower-cost development centers in India, and that model became the template for the whole offshore services industry. Murthy also made transparency a brand: early adoption of U.S. GAAP reporting, detailed disclosures, and a reputation for refusing bribes in a business culture where they were common. He was CEO from **1981 to 2002**, then chairman until **2011**, and came back as executive chairman from **2013 to 2014** to steady the company. Infosys now has well over 300,000 employees and annual revenue of roughly **$19B**.

**Complications.** His legacy is debated. His 2013 return, with his son **Rohan** as his executive assistant, drew criticism for nepotism. In **2017** he publicly challenged the board over CEO Vishal Sikka's tenure and severance payments, and Sikka resigned. Supporters saw it as governance oversight and critics saw it as a founder who couldn't let go. In 2023 he said young Indians should be willing to work **70 hours a week** to build the nation, which set off a long national debate about work culture. The family now invests through **Catamaran Ventures**. His daughter **Akshata Murty** is married to former UK Prime Minister **Rishi Sunak**, and Sudha Murty was nominated to India's upper house of Parliament in 2024. He received the **Padma Vibhushan** in 2008.

**Lessons for founders:**
1. **A failed first company is tuition.** Softronics failed. What Murthy learned there about clients, cash and partners went into Infosys.
2. **Make the co-founders' commitment explicit.** The 1989 "I'll buy you out" moment turned an unstated question into an explicit choice, and it kept the team together through the hardest years.
3. **Share equity widely, early.** Infosys's broad option program was a recruiting and retention advantage that rivals had to copy, and it built loyalty that salaries alone couldn't.
4. **Governance can be part of the brand.** In a low-trust market, being visibly clean (audited, transparent, no bribes) helped Infosys win Western clients.
5. **Know when the founder's job is finished.** Murthy's later interventions show how hard it is to let go once your name is on the institution.
