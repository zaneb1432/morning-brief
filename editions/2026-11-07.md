## 1. TECH & AI NEWS

**White House "Super Intelligence Force" gets to work.** The Super Intelligence Force (SIF) is a federal AI task force. It reportedly held its first meeting around October 15–16 and adopted a charter that starts a 120-day clock. It's chaired by Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton. It told AI labs to report "rogue" behavior by their products and fix it immediately, and it agreed to commission a risk assessment from PCAST. Coverage notes that it hasn't defined what counts as an incident, how fast labs must report one, or what the penalties are, and that no new rules were discussed. It matters because it is the first standing federal body aimed at agentic and frontier-model misbehavior, and Clayton has reportedly said companies will be held responsible for hacking-related harms. *Caveat:* the "Anthropic test model that submitted visa applications" detail appears only in TNW's coverage, and the meeting date varies by source. Also, the search turned up no DOJ "Super Intelligence Task Force". DOJ's separate AI unit is a litigation group that challenges state AI laws. Sources: [TNW](https://thenextweb.com/news/super-intelligence-force-charter-first-meeting-anthropic), [CBS on DOJ's state-law task force](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/doj-creates-task-force-to-challenge-state-ai-regulations).

**AI finds bugs faster than anyone can patch them.** Commentary and reporting from the past two weeks converge on one point: with frontier models able to read entire codebases, vulnerability discovery has accelerated sharply, and remediation hasn't kept up. TechRadar reports Anthropic saying more than 99% of what its Mythos model found under Project Glasswing remains unpatched. Cisco's Tom Gillis told CyberScoop that models can now grasp whole codebases, which is why they're finding so much. The bottleneck has moved from finding flaws to prioritizing and shipping fixes, which favors tools for exploitability triage and automated patch validation. Much of this commentary comes from vendors with something to sell, but the core claim holds up across independent reporting. Sources: [TechRadar](https://techradar.com/pro/patch-window-is-officially-dead-as-ai-finds-bugs-faster-than-humans-can-squash-them), [Campus Technology](https://campustechnology.com/articles/2026/10/28/ai-is-accelerating-vulnerability-discovery-researchers-find.aspx), [Federal News Network](https://federalnewsnetwork.com/commentary/2026/07/ai-is-about-to-overwhelm-cyber-defenses-for-one-simple-reason/).

**Oracle's AI-cloud week: cancelled deals and export-control friction.** The Financial Times reported that Oracle cancelled a roughly $7B, five-year deal to supply Tencent with up to 100,000 advanced AI chips in Southeast Asian data centers after US officials raised concerns. Separately, reports say Oracle cancelled an order of 300–400 Nvidia GB300 NVL72 racks from Super Micro, valued at about $1.1–1.4B, and its shares fell about 6%. Business Insider reported that Microsoft pulled out of a $3B+ Oracle capacity lease over FedRAMP concerns; Oracle called that report "inaccurate". Oracle still cites $553B in remaining performance obligations and says demand outstrips supply in parts of its cloud. The picture is a market where AI infrastructure demand is real but where politics and customer security requirements are starting to cancel individual deals. Some of this rests on single-source reporting. Sources: [Gadget Review](https://www.gadgetreview.com/?p=426431), [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/oracle-shares-plunge-dumping-major-122435400.html), [TechRadar](https://techradar.com/pro/oracle-slams-report-claiming-a-massive-microsoft-cloud-deal-collapsed-as-inaccurate).

## 4. FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY — Chester Carlson (1906–1968), inventor of xerography

*Written from background knowledge, not from this session's searches. The repo had no prior editions, so there was nothing to repeat. Future editions should avoid Carlson.*

Chester Carlson was born in Seattle in 1906 to a father crippled by arthritis and a mother who died of tuberculosis while he was a teenager. He worked through high school and a junior college and took a physics degree at Caltech in 1930. Hundreds of job applications in the Depression turned up only a poor job at Bell Labs, from which he was laid off. He then took a job in the patent department at the electronics firm P. R. Mallory in New York.

The job gave him the problem. Patent work meant endless copying of drawings and specifications, by hand or through slow, messy photographic processes. Carlson, who also suffered from arthritis, started experimenting at home on the idea of a dry copying process using electrostatic charge and light. He used a kitchen in his Queens apartment until the smells prompted his landlord and his wife to push him out. He then rented a room in Astoria and hired an out-of-work Austrian physicist, Otto Kornei.

On October 22, 1938, they made the first xerographic copy. Kornei wrote "10-22-38 Astoria" in ink on a glass slide, and they pressed it against a sulfur-coated zinc plate, charged it, exposed it to light, dusted it with powder, and transferred the image to wax paper. The copy was the proof of concept. Kornei left, and Carlson spent years trying to sell the idea. He was turned down by more than 20 companies, including IBM, Kodak and GE, which saw no need given carbon paper and existing photographic methods.

In 1944 Battelle Memorial Institute, a nonprofit research organization in Columbus, Ohio, agreed to develop the process in exchange for a share of royalties. In 1947 Haloid, a small Rochester photographic paper company, licensed it. Haloid coined the name "xerography" from Greek words for "dry writing". It spent years and enormous sums on engineering, and it launched the Model 914 plain-paper copier in 1959. The 914 was a huge commercial success. The company became Xerox, and Carlson became very rich.

He gave away most of his fortune, reportedly more than $100 million, to causes including Quaker groups, civil rights work, and Zen Buddhism and other spiritual interests. He lived modestly and died of a heart attack in 1968, in a movie theater.

**Why it matters for founders:** Carlson's story is a case of persistence in the face of repeated rejection. The invention took about 21 years to become a product, and it needed institutional partners to build it. He couldn't have done it alone.
