## 🤖 TECH & AI NEWS

### 1. OpenAI countersues Apple, alleging supplier pressure and "trap" employment contracts
On Friday (Nov 27), [OpenAI](https://openai.com) filed counterclaims in federal court in San Jose. [Apple](https://www.apple.com) sued OpenAI over trade secrets in July, and these counterclaims turn that case into a two-way fight. OpenAI alleges that Apple's supply-management staff pressured suppliers both companies use to reveal details of OpenAI's hardware projects, and to keep ex-Apple engineers away from them. The filing says *"suppliers have expressed fear of losing Apple business and have moved or rescheduled meetings with OpenAI to avoid Apple seeing them."* It names none of the suppliers. OpenAI also says Apple's employment agreements unfairly stop engineers from leaving for OpenAI. It accuses Apple of taking OpenAI's own confidential information too, including more than 100 files about an unreleased OpenAI device that a returning employee allegedly sent to personal accounts. Outlets disagree on whether OpenAI filed 11 or 12 counterclaims. Apple called the filing "a transparent attempt to distract." In October OpenAI had already agreed to stop using Apple's confidential information. Everything above is an allegation; no court has ruled on any of it.
**Why it matters:** OpenAI's device work (the Jony Ive collaboration) depends on the same small group of Asian contract manufacturers that Apple controls through sheer order volume. If OpenAI's claims hold up, they describe a supply-chain chokehold: a new hardware entrant can be starved of suppliers without anyone saying no out loud. Expect discovery in this case to reveal unusually concrete detail about how both companies' device plans are going.

### 2. SAP reportedly weighing at least 10,000 job cuts to reorganize around AI
Bloomberg reported Friday that [SAP](https://www.sap.com) is considering cutting at least **10,000 jobs, about 9% of its workforce**, as it reorganizes its software business around AI. Every department has reportedly been asked to find cuts, with product and engineering teams and sales as the main targets. The final number could change after negotiations with labor representatives. In Germany, a job-security agreement runs until 2028, which limits how cuts can be made there. SAP told CNBC it is "continuously reviewing how our organization and workforce need to evolve" and does "not comment on speculation." SAP's 2024 restructuring hit a similar 9,000–10,000 roles, mostly through voluntary exits, and its headcount still grew afterward to about 113,000.
**Why it matters:** SAP is Europe's most valuable software company and the system of record for much of global manufacturing and finance. If it is cutting engineers and salespeople specifically, enterprise software vendors are betting that AI agents will do a meaningful share of building and selling software. Canva made a similar restructuring move this month (see the founder section), and the pattern is spreading from startups to incumbents.
🔗 [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/27/sap-job-cuts-ai-restructuring.html) · [Silicon Republic](https://www.siliconrepublic.com/business/sap-eliminate-positions-restructuring-plans-working-life)

### 3. AI trade rebounds: Anthropic closes above its IPO price; S&P 500 has its best week since April 2025
After a rough first week of trading (see yesterday's brief), [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com) (ANT) rose to **$91.88** on Friday, its highest close since its Nov 19 debut and comfortably above its **$83 IPO price**. The broader AI trade also recovered. The Nasdaq 100 rose 2.6% after nearly sliding into a bear market, and the S&P 500 had its best week since April 2025. Bloomberg's market wrap describes the bulls as "stirring again after weeks of AI anxiety."
**Why it matters:** ANT has become the public market's daily vote on the AI trade. In one week it went from a pop, to a slide back to its offer price, to a rebound. That swing will set expectations for the OpenAI and SpaceX listings and for late-stage private rounds like Sierra's. One good week doesn't settle the concerns the Bank of England raised this week about AI financing, so expect more swings.

### 4. Huawei teases an AI memory design aimed at Samsung and SK Hynix
Bloomberg reported Thursday (Nov 26) that [Huawei](https://www.huawei.com) has developed a high-bandwidth memory (HBM) design with a **4,096-bit interface**. That is twice the data-pin width of the HBM4 that [Samsung](https://www.samsung.com) and [SK hynix](https://www.skhynix.com) mass-produce. Huawei plans to present it at ISSCC in San Francisco in February 2027. It hasn't disclosed speed, measured bandwidth, capacity or power, so the design is better on paper only for now. It's also unclear whether Huawei can manufacture it in volume. The Information separately reported that Huawei told customers its next flagship AI chip, the **Ascend 960DT**, will still largely run on Samsung and SK hynix memory. US rules bar those suppliers from selling HBM to Chinese customers. Memory stocks, including [Micron](https://www.micron.com), dipped on the news.
**Why it matters:** HBM, not chip logic, has become China's tightest AI bottleneck. Nvidia-class accelerators are useless without stacks of fast memory, and only three companies make it at scale. A credible domestic HBM would remove one of Washington's most effective chokepoints. The Ascend 960DT report suggests Huawei isn't there yet.
🔗 [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/huawei-hbm-ai-memory-samsung-sk-hynix-isscc) · [TechRepublic](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-huawei-high-bandwidth-memory-design-china-apac/) · [The Edge Singapore (Bloomberg syndication)](https://www.theedgesingapore.com/news/tech/huawei-teases-next-gen-ai-memory-chip-take-samsung-hynix)

### 5. Nvidia's China squeeze spreads: neoclouds told to rule out Chinese renters
This follows yesterday's report that [Nvidia](https://www.nvidia.com) paused server shipments to four Southeast Asian countries. CNBC reported (Nov 26) that GPU sellers now tell "neoclouds" (independent GPU-rental clouds) to **rule out Chinese renters** before they can buy chips. Meanwhile, an unusually large number of Nvidia GPUs has hit the resale market since Commerce's Nov 16 interim rule took effect. The rule requires a license to export advanced AI chips to data centers in Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil. Commerce has also said license requirements apply to any entity *headquartered* in China, wherever it operates. That closes the overseas-subsidiary route. TechCrunch ran a long piece on Alice Huang, a former executive at Megaspeed, the Southeast Asian data center operator at the center of the smuggling investigation. TechCrunch concludes that claims she is a Chinese agent are still unproven.
**Why it matters:** Remote rental was the main way Chinese labs reached frontier compute. This crackdown shifts compliance onto every reseller and cloud operator, so Nvidia's legal risk now extends to its customers too. It will also strand capacity: data centers built for Chinese demand now need new tenants, which could push GPU rental prices down for everyone else.

### 1. A $4B quant fund's founder is building an operating system kernel for AI agents
Feng Ji runs **Baiont Quant**, a roughly $4B Chinese quant hedge fund. He is backing a startup that will release the kernel of an operating system built for AI agents, not humans. Bloomberg didn't name it, but secondary outlets call it **Cosmic Dawn**. The kernel would manage file access, memory and permissions for large numbers of agents at once, with guardrails to stop agents acting beyond their authority. Several outlets say the release is targeted for the end of 2026. One report says the startup raised seed money at a **$300M valuation** and plans another round in Q1 2027; that comes from a single source, so treat it as unconfirmed. The first deployment will be inside Baiont's own investment research. *(Official sites for Baiont and Cosmic Dawn couldn't be confirmed, so they aren't linked.)*
**Why it matters:** Today's agents run on operating systems designed for one human at a keyboard. Once a firm runs thousands of agents, scheduling, memory isolation and permissions become problems for the OS itself, not the app. A quant fund is a natural first customer, since it already treats compute and latency as a source of profit. It's also a sign that Chinese AI talent is moving into systems software, partly because export controls make hardware efficiency worth more. StepFun's Step AOS, an Android-kernel fork for agents launched in July, is the domestic rival.

### 2. General Catalyst leads pre-seed for VVS, a Vienna quant firm run by a 17-year-old

### 1. Saviu closes €40M Fund II for Francophone Africa (Nov 27)
Paris and Abidjan-based **Saviu Ventures** (official site not confirmed; [Tech In Africa](https://www.techinafrica.com/saviu-secures-43-6-million-fund-to-expand-startup-investments-across-francophone-africa/)) held the final close of **Saviu II at about €40M (~$43.6M)**, roughly four times the size of its ~€10M first fund. The **African Development Bank** and Bpifrance's **Averroès Africa** fund-of-funds joined at the final close, alongside corporate and private LPs (Proparco and Axian came in earlier). The fund invests from seed to Series A, mainly in French-speaking Africa. It has made 11 investments and one exit so far and expects to back about 20 companies. The fund reached this total in stages: €12M first close in late 2023, €25M in early 2025, and now €40M, against a €30–50M target.
**Why it matters:** Francophone Africa gets a small share of African VC compared with Nigeria, Kenya, Egypt and South Africa. Development finance institutions anchoring a local, French-speaking manager is how that gap gets closed. Taking nearly three years to raise €40M also shows how hard emerging-market fundraising still is.

### 2. Thesis watch: "AI may not be a bubble, but the way we're financing it might be" (Nov 26)
In Fast Company, **Judah Taub**, managing partner at Israeli VC **Hetz Ventures**, argues investors need to separate their excitement about AI as a technology from their judgment of the businesses and capital structures behind it. The piece comes in the same week the Bank of England put hyperscaler AI exposure at about $4T. It also follows Howard Marks's Nov 11 Oaktree memo, *"A Layman Thinks About AI."* Marks cites Goldman data showing AI-related debt issuance near $600B this year. He warns that tech equities, data center loans and investment-grade bonds may all be exposed to the same risk: demand for compute.
**Why it matters:** The VC conversation has moved from "is AI real?" to "who is holding the leverage?" For early-stage investors, the practical takeaway is to back companies whose economics work even if compute stays expensive and capital gets tighter.

## 📖 FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: Melanie Perkins (b. 1987)
*Co-founder & CEO of [Canva](https://www.canva.com); earlier, co-founder of Fusion Books*

*The last three editions profiled founders from the 1930s–1970s (Ole Kirk Christiansen, Sandra Kurtzig, Ruth Handler). Today's is a contemporary one, chosen because her company is going through its own reckoning with AI this month.*

**The problem in the classroom.** In 2007, Melanie Perkins was a 19-year-old commerce and communications student at the University of Western Australia in Perth, tutoring classmates in design software on the side. What she noticed was how long it took students to learn programs like InDesign and Photoshop, and how hard the tools were to use. They spent a semester learning where the buttons were before they could make anything. Her conclusion: design software was built for professionals, but most people who need to make something visual aren't professionals. The software should be online, collaborative and simple enough to use in minutes.

**Starting small on purpose.** She didn't try to take on Adobe right away. She and her boyfriend (now husband) **Cliff Obrecht** picked one narrow, painful use case: **school yearbooks**. Out of her mother's living room in Perth they built **Fusion Books**, an online tool that let students design their own yearbook pages with drag-and-drop templates. Perkins dropped out of university to run it. Fusion grew into one of Australia's largest yearbook companies and expanded into France and New Zealand. More importantly, it proved her central bet that non-designers would happily design if the tool was easy enough.

**A hundred rejections, and kitesurfing.** The bigger vision, a design platform for everything, needed real capital, and Perth was far from Silicon Valley. In 2010 she met Silicon Valley investor **Bill Tai**, who was in Perth judging a startup competition. He invited her to San Francisco, where she spent months pitching and was turned down by more than 100 investors. Tai's network revolved around kitesurfing events, so Perkins learned to kitesurf to get into the rooms where deals happened. She kept rewriting the pitch deck after each rejection. Investors' objections were usually that she had no technical co-founder and that Perth was too far away. She solved the first by recruiting **Cameron Adams**, an ex-Google designer-engineer, as third co-founder, and by bringing on Google Maps co-creator **Lars Rasmussen** as an adviser. The money eventually came: Canva raised about **A$3M** in 2012, matched by an Australian government grant, and launched in **2013**. Early backers famously included Hollywood actors Owen Wilson and Woody Harrelson, who met the founders through Tai's circle.

**Freemium, templates, and the long game.** Canva's model was simple: free to start, with templates for everything (social posts, presentations, flyers, résumés) and paid plans for teams and premium content. It grew mostly by word of mouth and was **profitable as early as 2017**, which was rare for a consumer startup of its size. It reached a **$1B valuation in 2018** and **$40B in 2021**, at its peak one of the most valuable startups in the world run by a woman. Perkins and Obrecht kept the company headquartered in Sydney, and said they would give away most of their equity, roughly 30% of the company, through the Canva Foundation. Her stated philosophy is a two-step plan: *"Step one: build one of the world's most valuable companies. Step two: do the most good we can."*

**The test happening right now.** The AI era has been tough on Canva because it attacks Canva's core value: the person who just needs a decent-looking slide can now ask a model to make it. Canva acquired Affinity and the AI startup Leonardo.ai and moved deeper into enterprise. Growth still slowed. In August Canva cut its 2026 revenue growth forecast from about 30% to 20%, citing AI costs. Its Australian VC backers marked it down to roughly $35B, and secondary-market quotes are near $30B. On **Nov 4**, Perkins announced a restructuring affecting **1,271 people, about 22% of staff**, through layoffs, role changes or relocation. Product, Growth and Platform are merging into one organization with fewer management layers. She called it the company's first growth slowdown in a decade, and some coverage describes her as calling for a "refounding." COO Obrecht has said an IPO is targeted for **2027**, after the AI overhaul is done.

**Lessons for founders:**
- **Find a narrow wedge into the market.** Yearbooks were a small, unglamorous market, but they let her prove the thesis, earn revenue and learn about users before raising for the big vision.
- **Treat every rejection as feedback.** "No technical co-founder" wasn't an argument against the idea; it was a hole in the team, and she filled it.
- **Get into the room, wherever it is.** If deals happen at kitesurfing events, learn to kitesurf.
- **A platform shift can make you obsolete.** Canva won by making design easy for everyone. AI makes it easier still, so the company has to refound itself or risk being overtaken by tools that make design unnecessary.
