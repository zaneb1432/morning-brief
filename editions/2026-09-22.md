## TECH & AI NEWS

**Anthropic folds Cowork back into Claude, launches Docs and Slides.** On September 16, Anthropic announced it is merging Claude Cowork — the separate workspace it built earlier this year for bigger, multi-step tasks — back into the main Claude chat interface, dropping the product from three modes down to two (Claude Chat and Claude Code). The company said users kept getting stuck deciding which mode a task belonged in, and work started in one didn't carry into the other. In its place, Claude gains native Docs and Slides tools plus in-conversation access to Claude Design, so any chat can now spin up a working document, a slide deck (exportable to PowerPoint or PDF), or a full agentic task using whatever context, skills, and connectors a user already has. Rollout starts with Pro and Max subscribers over the coming weeks, with Team, Free, and Enterprise following on a delayed schedule. It's a notable bet that "one smart surface" beats separate modes for different job sizes — the opposite direction from how most productivity suites have evolved. [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/16/anthropic-merges-claude-chat-and-cowork-in-one-interface/)

**OpenAI launches Astra for Law, a legal-specialized configuration of GPT-6.** Released September 17, Astra for Law wraps OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra model in a legal search index spanning U.S. case law, statutes, regulations, court rules, and administrative decisions across more than 230 million URLs, updated daily, alongside instructions tuned for legal research and drafting. On Vals AI's Legal Research Bench, it passed 54% of 200 questions outright versus 38.7% for GPT-6 Astra with plain web search — a meaningful jump, though still short of "reliable enough to skip human review." Select firms including Sullivan & Cromwell, Ropes & Gray, Cooley, Latham & Watkins, and Wachtell Lipton have been testing it through a Trusted Access program. The launch escalates a fast-moving race for law-firm AI spend, an area OpenAI, Anthropic, Harvey, and others are all now targeting directly rather than treating as a general-purpose use case. [SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/09/17/openai-launches-astra-for-law-a-gpt-6-configuration-for-legal-research/) · [LawSites](https://www.lawnext.com/2026/09/openai-releases-astra-for-law-a-gpt-6-model-configured-for-legal-work.html)

**Anthropic says Claude now leads 26% of its own AI research and development.** In a disclosure published September 18, Anthropic revealed internal metrics showing Claude "leads" — meaning it can carry a task end-to-end from a high-level prompt under human supervision — 26% of the R&D work that goes into building Anthropic's own future models, up from under 1% in February. More than 90% of measured tasks now sit at or above the automation tier where AI "collaborates" rather than just assists. The company frames this as tracking, not achieving, progress toward recursive self-improvement — the point where an AI system could autonomously develop its successor — and stresses Claude has not reached full autonomy in any category. Still, with over 30,000 agents reportedly doing research and engineering work inside Anthropic at any given time as of August, the trajectory is the story: this is one of the first times a frontier lab has quantified, with real numbers, how much of its own progress is now AI-driven. [BetaNews](https://betanews.com/article/claude-ai-rd-development/) · [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/recursive-self-improvement)

**Novo Nordisk taps Anthropic's Claude to speed drug discovery.** Announced September 16, the pharma giant will use Anthropic's frontier models and test the new "Claude Science" workbench across specific R&D workflows, building on Novo's existing use of Claude to automate trial-report generation (cutting patient documentation timelines from months to minutes). The companies say they'll jointly target discovery bottlenecks identified by Novo's own computational and research teams. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei framed the stakes expansively, saying AI could "compress a century's worth of biological and medical breakthroughs into a decade." It's the latest sign that frontier AI labs are moving from generic enterprise deals toward deep, workflow-specific partnerships with regulated industries — pharma chief among them — where the upside (and liability) is much higher than chatbot subscriptions. [Drug Discovery News](https://www.drugdiscoverynews.com/novo-nordisk-taps-anthropic-s-claude-to-speed-drug-discovery-17533) · [BioSpace](https://www.biospace.com/press-releases/novo-and-anthropic-will-collaborate-to-advance-drug-discovery-with-claude)

**OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind are quietly building a FINRA-style AI safety body.** Reporting this month confirmed the three labs have been in working-group talks since July on an industry-led standards body for frontier AI, modeled on FINRA — the industry-funded group that writes rules for U.S. brokers and can refer violators to the SEC. Google DeepMind CEO Demis Hassabis first floated the idea publicly on July 14. The proposed scope centers on shared pre-release testing protocols: independent evaluations, safety reviews, and standardized risk assessments before frontier models ship. Sam Altman has reportedly told OpenAI staff he'd back it but expects labs will have to build it themselves absent federal backing, while Anthropic has pushed harder for direct government involvement. Self-regulation carries an obvious credibility question — the labs writing the rules are the ones being graded — but with Washington actively deregulating (see below), it may be the only oversight mechanism moving faster than a legislative stalemate. [Forkast](https://forkast.news/three-frontier-labs-are-building-a-finra-style-safety-body-history-suggests-it-wont-be-a-brake/)

**AI takes center stage as Trump and Xi prepare to meet.** Ahead of Thursday's (September 25) White House summit, Bloomberg reported September 21 that both governments now treat AI as the central axis of their economic rivalry, with U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent calling recent AI, trade, and rare-earths talks with China "very successful." Trump has publicly dismissed calls from Anthropic and OpenAI's CEOs — who argue the two superpowers should coordinate on pacing frontier development to manage risk — insisting the U.S. cannot afford to slow down while competing with China for AI dominance. Analysts don't expect any formal slowdown agreement to emerge from the meeting. The subtext: the industry's own safety-body ambitions (above) are partly a hedge against exactly this kind of governmental disinterest in coordinated guardrails. [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-09-21/trump-presses-ai-case-ahead-of-xi-meeting-as-calls-rise-for-slowdown) · [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-21/trump-xi-meeting-china-us-ai-rivalry-poses-questions-for-summit)

## STARTUP WORLD

**Profound becomes the first GEO/AEO unicorn, hitting $1.8B valuation.** On September 15, the two-year-old startup — which helps brands understand and improve how they show up in AI search answers from ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude — raised a $180 million Series D led by Sequoia Capital and Kleiner Perkins, just seven months after a $96 million Series C. Total funding now exceeds $335 million. Revenue reportedly tripled in six months, and the company counts Comcast, Estée Lauder, and Walmart among 1,000+ enterprise customers. The broader "generative engine optimization" category it anchors was worth roughly $1 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach $17 billion by 2034 — a sign of how fast an entirely new marketing discipline has formed around the shift from search engines to AI answer engines. [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/15/aeo-startup-profound-hits-unicorn-valuation-raises-180m-series-d-7-months-after-last-round/)

**Gimlet Labs raises $300M Series B at a $3B valuation to rebuild AI inference.** The San Francisco infrastructure startup, announced September 4-5, is building what it calls the industry's first "multi-silicon inference cloud" for agentic AI — running inference workloads across multiple chip architectures rather than locking into one vendor's silicon. Andreessen Horowitz led the round, joined by new investors Arm, M12 (Microsoft's venture arm), and Sapphire Ventures, plus existing backers Menlo Ventures and Factory. The raise comes just six months after Gimlet's $80 million Series A, bringing total funding to $392 million. The pitch: as agentic workloads push inference into the "quadrillions of tokens per month" range, homogeneous hardware stacks can't keep up — Gimlet claims 3-10x faster performance for frontier workloads by mixing silicon types. [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-04/andreessen-backed-ai-startup-gimlet-is-valued-at-3-billion-in-new-round)

**Pixxel raises $100M Series C, India's largest-ever space-tech round.** The Bangalore hyperspectral-imaging satellite company announced the raise September 7-8, co-led by Temasek and Seraphim Space with new participation from 360 ONE Asset and IMM Investment. Total funding hits $195 million. Pixxel is using the capital to push beyond its original hyperspectral niche into a broader "planetary infrastructure" platform — adding synthetic aperture radar and ultra-high-resolution optical sensing to its existing six-satellite Firefly constellation, while building out its Aurora earth-intelligence software. The company already holds contracts with NASA and the NRO and was selected to lead India's first public-private Earth observation constellation. It's a strong signal that space-tech fundraising, long dominated by U.S. and European players, is maturing meaningfully in India. [Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/companies/news/pixxel-series-c-funding-temasek-seraphim-space-tech-126090700126_1.html) · [Pixxel](https://www.pixxel.space/news/pixxel-raises-100-million-in-series-c-funding-to-build-the-infrastructure-for-a-changing-planet)

**Scan.com raises $220M ($90M equity + $130M debt) to build a national medical-imaging network.** Announced in early September, the equity portion was led by Noteus Partners with Aviva, Concord Health Partners, YZR Capital, and Oxford Capital participating; VerisFi Capital and Atempo Growth supplied the debt facilities. Scan.com operates an API layer connecting patients to imaging providers (MRI, CT, etc.), and says revenue doubled over the past year to a $165 million annualized run rate, with more than 900,000 patients served. The funding will expand its provider network and fund agentic AI infrastructure that routes patients, schedules scans, and returns diagnostic results — healthcare "plumbing" that's unglamorous but structurally important as imaging demand outpaces radiologist supply. [Fierce Healthcare](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/finance/scancom-raises-220m-build-largest-medical-imaging-network-us)

## VENTURE CAPITAL

The firm's framing is distinctive: it argues AGI (defined loosely as agents performing many tasks as well as humans can) has effectively already arrived, so the next wave of value creation isn't in the model layer but in the application and workflow infrastructure needed to make agentic capability run efficiently and reliably at scale. It's a useful data point on where late-2026 VC conviction is shifting — away from funding the next frontier lab, toward funding what gets built once frontier capability is a commodity input. ## FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: Chester Carlson

Long before "founder" meant someone pitching a deck to Sand Hill Road, Chester Carlson spent a decade being told no by nearly everyone in American industry — and ended up creating one of the most valuable inventions of the 20th century almost entirely alone.

Carlson was born in Seattle in 1906 into genuine poverty. His parents were chronically ill, and by age 14 he was the family's primary breadwinner, working before and after school to keep a roof over their heads. He nonetheless put himself through Caltech, graduating in 1930 with a physics degree — timing that landed him squarely in the Great Depression job market. He eventually found work in the patent department of an electronics firm in New York, where he ran headlong into a problem that would define the rest of his life: getting copies made of patent drawings and documents was slow, expensive, and required sending originals out to specialists. There had to be a better way.

Working alone in his apartment kitchen in Queens in the late 1930s — with occasional help from an unemployed German physicist he hired as an assistant — Carlson began experimenting with electrostatics and photoconductivity. On October 22, 1938, he produced the first xerographic image: the notation "10-22-38 ASTORIA" transferred from one surface to another using static electricity and light-sensitive material, with no liquid chemicals or wet processing involved. He called the process electrophotography; it would later be renamed xerography, from the Greek for "dry writing."

Then came the part of the story that should reassure every founder currently being ghosted by investors: Carlson spent the next six years trying to sell the idea to industry, and more than twenty companies — including IBM, Kodak, and General Electric — turned him down. Most couldn't see why anyone would want a dry office copier when carbon paper and mimeographs already existed; the market for "better copies" simply wasn't obvious yet. It wasn't until 1944 that the nonprofit Battelle Memorial Institute agreed to help develop the technology further, and not until 1947 that a small Rochester, New York photo-paper company called Haloid took a licensing bet on it. It took eleven more years of engineering work before Haloid — by then renamed Xerox — shipped the Xerox 914, the first automatic plain-paper office copier, in 1959.

The 914 became one of the most successful products in American business history, and Carlson, who had held onto patent royalties and equity through two decades of rejection, became a multimillionaire almost overnight in his fifties. He spent much of that fortune quietly: funding Zen Buddhist and Quaker causes, supporting civil rights organizations, and giving away the majority of his estate before his death in 1968, reportedly having decided he wanted to die with no money left. His patience is the real lesson — twenty years from kitchen-table experiment to ubiquitous office machine, most of it spent being told the idea wasn't worth backing.
