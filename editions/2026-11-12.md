## 4. FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY — Josephine Cochrane (1839–1913), Garis-Cochran Manufacturing Company

Josephine Garis did not set out to start a manufacturing company. She was born in Ohio in 1839. Her father was a civil engineer, and her great-grandfather John Fitch had built one of America's first steamboats, but she had no technical training. At 19 she married William Cochran, a merchant and later a politician in Shelbyville, Illinois, and spent her adult life as a well-off hostess. (She added the "e" to her surname herself.) The often-repeated origin story is that her servants kept chipping her heirloom china while washing it, so she started washing it herself, hated the job, and decided a machine should do it.

Her husband died in 1883 and left her with debts and little cash, which made the project urgent. Working with a mechanic, George Butters, in a shed behind her house, she designed something new. Earlier "dishwashers" scrubbed dishes, but hers held plates, cups and saucers in wire compartments sized to each piece, fitted into a wheel inside a copper boiler. A hand crank or motor turned the wheel while pumps sprayed hot, soapy water on the dishes. It was the first commercially successful machine to clean with **water pressure instead of scrubbers**, the principle every dishwasher still uses. She received a patent on December 28, 1886.

Home buyers were not interested, because servants were cheap and few homes had enough hot water. Cochrane turned to businesses: hotels, restaurants and hospitals, whose high volume made the machine pay for itself. She sold in person, at a time when, as she later remarked, a woman walking alone through a hotel lobby was a scandal. Her breakthrough came at the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago. Her machines washed dishes in the fair's restaurants, won an award for mechanical construction and durability, and brought in orders from Chicago hotels. She founded the Garis-Cochran Manufacturing Company, sold to institutional kitchens and kept running the company until she died in 1913, at 73.

After her death the company was acquired by Hobart (sources give 1916 or 1926), and its dishwasher line became part of **KitchenAid**, now owned by Whirlpool. KitchenAid still names her as its founder. Home dishwashers did not catch on until the 1950s, after her death, once home plumbing and changing household economics caught up with the idea.

**Lessons for founders:** (1) **If the obvious market says no, find the one where the economics already work.** Homes didn't need the machine yet, but hotel kitchens washing thousands of plates a day did. (2) **A showcase can work as a sales channel.** The 1893 Exposition gave her credibility, awards and buyers in one place. (3) **Necessity drives persistence.** The idea came from frustration, but debt is what turned it into a company.
