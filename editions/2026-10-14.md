## 1. TECH & AI NEWS

**Anthropic's Frontier Red Team: small models may soon self-replicate on cheap gadgets.** On October 12, Anthropic researchers William Zhou and Daniel Freeman published "AI models may be capable of self-replicating without access to a datacenter." Per secondary coverage, a 9B-parameter model ran inference on 8 of 9 hardware types tested, including smart ovens and off-brand Android TV boxes. One test ran distributed inference across 15 TV boxes costing about $55 each. Models up to about 30B parameters can complete many individual replication steps but struggle to chain them end to end, while stronger open-weight variants managed end-to-end replication in simplified, emulated tests. The authors report "signs of life," not a loose replicator, but warn that practical replication on commercial IoT hardware could arrive late 2026 or shortly after. Such a system would have no central point of failure and be hard to shut down. Why it matters: it shifts the safety debate from "can a datacenter-scale model escape?" to "what happens when capable-enough models run on a billion unpatched devices?" Sources: [CNBC](https://cnbc.com/2026/10/13/anthropic-ai-models-self-replication-smart-devices.html), [Crypto Briefing](https://cryptobriefing.com/anthropic-small-ai-models-self-replicate-iot/), [explainx.ai](https://explainx.ai/blog/anthropic-self-replicating-ai-iot-smart-tv-botnet-frontier-red-team-2026).

## 4. FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: Madam C.J. Walker (1867–1919)

*(Written from background knowledge; first edition on record, so no repeats.)*

Sarah Breedlove was born December 23, 1867, on a Louisiana cotton plantation, the first of her family born free after emancipation. Orphaned at seven, she worked as a domestic, married at 14 to escape an abusive home, and was widowed at 20 with a young daughter, A'Lelia. She spent years washing laundry in St. Louis for about a dollar a day, saving for her daughter's schooling.

In the 1890s she suffered severe scalp ailments and hair loss, common among women with poor sanitation and harsh products. Working with Annie Turnbo Malone's Poro company as a sales agent, she developed her own scalp treatment and in 1906 married newspaperman Charles J. Walker, whose marketing savvy gave her the name "Madam C.J. Walker." She started with door-to-door sales and demonstrations across the South, then built a manufacturing base in Indianapolis in 1910.

Her innovation was less the formula than the system: a trained network of "beauty culturists" earning commissions and independence, a Lelia College to train them, mail-order marketing, and a recognizable brand with her face on the tin. By the 1910s she employed thousands of women, many of whom would otherwise have worked as maids or farm laborers. She funded the NAACP's anti-lynching efforts, scholarships at Tuskegee, and the YMCA, and helped organize the National Negro Business League's women's network. She built a Hudson River mansion, Villa Lewaro, in 1918.

She died May 25, 1919, aged 51, reportedly the wealthiest self-made Black woman in America. Lesson: distribution and dignity of work can be a bigger moat than the product itself.
