## 4. FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY — Masaru Ibuka (1908–1997), co-founder of Sony

Born in Nikko, Japan, Ibuka was an engineer first. His early work at Photo-Chemical Laboratory and Japan Optical during the war built his conviction that Japan's rebuilding would depend on technology products. In September 1945, with Tokyo in ruins, he rented a bombed-out department-store room and founded a radio-repair outfit with about 20 people; in 1946 it became Tokyo Tsushin Kogyo (Totsuko), with Akio Morita joining as co-founder. His founding prospectus famously spoke of creating "an ideal workplace" where engineers could "work to their heart's content."

Early products flopped — a rice cooker, an electric cushion — before a tape recorder (1950) found a market. In 1952 Ibuka traveled to the US and learned Western Electric would license transistor technology; Totsuko bet on it despite doubters who said the transistor was unsuited to radios. The 1955 TR-55 and then the 1957 TR-63 "pocket" radio put the company on the world stage. Ibuka pushed the brand name Sony (1958) against advisors who wanted to keep the old name, and the company later pioneered Trinitron TVs and the Walkman, which Ibuka reportedly prompted by asking for a compact way to listen to stereo music on flights.

His style: protect engineers, chase products no one had made, and let Morita sell them. He later championed early childhood education and was awarded Japan's Order of Culture (1992). Lesson: a founder can be the "product conscience" of a company and still build something global by pairing with a complementary partner.
