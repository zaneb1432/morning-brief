**Under Kress, Nvidia reportedly pulls back from lending to its own customers (reported Nov 14–16).** Several reports, most citing The Information, say Kress opposed a Huang-backed plan to guarantee up to $250B in financing for OpenAI's Ohio data-center campus. **United Airlines bans personal AI agents from booking flights, a first big test of agent commerce (Nov 11, still developing).** United has changed its contract of carriage to list third-party AI agents among prohibited ticketing practices, even when a customer is supervising the agent. The ban covers its website and app, and agencies or aggregators that route bookings through AI tools. United says it does not yet work with any third-party agents. It cites security risks: account credentials shared with assistants could lead to unintended purchases, account changes or MileagePlus redemptions. It says it wants "secure integrations" with authorization and accountability built in. CEO Scott Kirby has been blunter, saying he doesn't want "another middleman." Analysts doubt the ban can be enforced, because an agent booking through Expedia looks like any other agency booking. Southwest has gone the other way and opened its flight search to ChatGPT. **Why it matters:** This came out in the same week OpenAI launched ChatGPT Wallet so its "dots" agents can pay. It shows that the main obstacle to agent commerce may be merchants who refuse to let an AI sit between them and their customers, more than payments or the technology. Expect other merchants with valuable loyalty programs, such as airlines, hotels and ticketing companies, to negotiate with agent platforms rather than open up to them. ## 2. STARTUP WORLD

**Bharat Semi raises a $24M seed round to build AI image sensors in India (Nov 15).** The fabless chip startup is designing image sensors with AI processing built in, for markets such as automotive, industrial vision and defense. It is pre-revenue and plans its first commercial production in 2027. Info Edge, South Park Commons and Indus Bridge Ventures led the all-equity round, with participation from General Atomics, the US defense contractor, and Kaynes Semicon, an Indian electronics manufacturer. The company told TechCrunch the money is not for a fab. It will remain fabless and outsource manufacturing. **Why it matters:** $24M is a very large seed round for an Indian hardware startup. A US defense prime and a domestic manufacturer on the cap table suggests Bharat Semi is aiming at "trusted supply chain" demand from both India's semiconductor push and Western buyers who want to avoid Chinese and Japanese sensor dependence. Image sensors are a narrow market that Sony dominates, which makes them a realistic entry point compared with competing in logic chips. ## 4. FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY — Marion Donovan (1917–1998), the Boater and Donovan Enterprises

**A shower curtain, a sewing machine and a $1 million exit that let other companies take the biggest prize.**

Marion O'Brien was born on 15 October 1917 in Fort Wayne, Indiana, and grew up among machinery. Her father, Miles O'Brien, and his twin brother ran the South Bend Lathe Works and invented industrial lathes. After her mother died in 1925, when Marion was seven, she spent much of her childhood in the family factory, watching her father and uncle design things. She studied English at Rosemont College in Pennsylvania, graduating in 1939. She moved to New York and worked in fashion magazines, including as an assistant beauty editor at *Vogue*. In 1942 she married James Donovan and moved to Westport, Connecticut.

The idea came, as it often does, from a frustrating household chore. After World War II, with a second baby at home, Donovan was constantly changing cloth diapers, wet bedding and clothes. The rubber pants available at the time caused diaper rash and pinched babies' skin. In 1946 she cut up her bathroom's shower curtain and sewed a waterproof diaper cover. She then refined it with parachute nylon, which was breathable and did not chafe the way rubber did. She replaced the dangerous safety pin with plastic snaps. She called it the **Boater**, because it kept the baby "afloat."

Manufacturers weren't interested, so she started a company, Donovan Enterprises. In 1949 she persuaded Saks Fifth Avenue to stock the Boater, and it sold out immediately. She received her patents in 1951. That same year she sold the company and her diaper patents to the Keko Corporation, a children's clothing maker, for **$1 million**, roughly $12M today. For a housewife with no capital, no factory and no network, that was a remarkable result.

Her next idea was even bigger: a fully disposable diaper made of absorbent paper. She spent years developing a paper strong enough and absorbent enough to keep a baby dry. She took it to the major paper companies, and every one turned her down. Executives told her it was "unnecessary and impractical," saying no one needed it. About a decade later, Procter & Gamble engineer Victor Mills developed Pampers, launched in 1961. Disposable diapers became one of the largest consumer-goods categories in the world, now worth tens of billions of dollars a year. Donovan earned nothing from it.

She wasn't discouraged. In 1958, with children at home, she earned a master's degree in architecture from Yale, one of only a handful of women in her class. She kept inventing all her life and received about 20 patents. They included a single-strand dental floss device, a hanger that keeps many garments organized in a small space, a device for zipping up the back of a dress without help, and a soap dish that drains so the soap doesn't sit in water. She died in New York on 4 November 1998, aged 81. In 2015 she was inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame, nearly 70 years after she cut up her shower curtain.

**Lessons for founders:**
- **Your own frustration is valid market research.** Donovan had the problem every day, and so did millions of other parents. She didn't need a survey to know the market existed.
- **When incumbents say no, go straight to the customer.** Manufacturers turned down the Boater, so she went to Saks. Proven sales changed what the industry thought was possible.
- **An early exit can be the right call, but know what you're giving up.** $1M in 1951 was life-changing, but it gave the next, much bigger opportunity to companies with factories and distribution.
- **Being dismissed as "impractical" can be a sign you're early.** The paper companies that rejected her disposable diaper were wrong about the market by tens of billions of dollars. Being early only pays off if you keep control of the idea until the market is ready.
