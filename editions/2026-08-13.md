## TECH & AI NEWS

**Nvidia lines up over $500 billion in outside financing to build the AI compute buildout.** Nvidia announced memorandums of understanding with six of the largest asset managers on earth — Apollo Global Management, Blackstone, BlackRock, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR — to establish financing platforms aimed at mobilizing more than $500 billion in third-party capital for AI data centers and power infrastructure. Crucially, the money flows through independent platforms rather than Nvidia's own balance sheet, letting customers who couldn't otherwise afford to finance AI compute buy it anyway, while Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang described the goal as turning AI chips into a Wall Street asset class comparable to commercial real estate or toll roads. Huang told CNBC he approached only these six firms and none turned him down. This matters because it formalizes the mechanism by which Wall Street, not just hyperscalers' own cash flow, will now underwrite the trillion-dollar AI infrastructure race — raising the stakes on whether AI compute demand justifies debt-financed buildouts at this scale. [Source (Nvidia Newsroom)](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/nvidia-partners-with-apollo-blackrock-blackstone-brookfield-goldman-sachs-and-kkr-to-establish-ai-compute-infrastructure-financing-platforms-to-mobilize-over-500-billion-of-third-party-capital) · [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/10/nvidia-wall-street-asset-managers-500-billion-ai-push.html)

**Meta open-sources Muse Glimmer, a 30B-parameter agentic model that runs on a single consumer GPU.** Meta Superintelligence Labs released Muse Glimmer under the permissive Apache 2.0 license — a notable return to open-weight releases after a period of Meta pulling back from open Llama models. Unlike chat-tuned models, Glimmer is purpose-built for always-on agent workloads: long-horizon reasoning, reliable tool calling, failure recovery, coding, and multimodal reasoning, with support for 100+ languages and compatibility with agent frameworks like OpenClaw. Meta compressed the model to roughly 4-bit precision and added block-level speculative decoding so it fits in 24GB of VRAM and responds fast enough to sit inside a real agent loop, with no network call required. It matters because it pushes capable, private, locally-run agents within reach of a single gaming GPU rather than a data center — a meaningful shift in who can deploy autonomous AI agents and at what cost. [Source (Meta AI Research)](https://research.meta.ai/blog/introducing-muse-glimmer-open-agentic-model) · [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/meta-returns-to-open-source-with-muse-glimmer-an-apache-2-0-licensed-30b-parameter-ai-model-optimized-for-agents-available-now)

**Google unveils the Pixel 11 lineup, Pixel Watch 5, and a Find Hub tracker at Made by Google 2026.** At its New York hardware event, Google introduced the Pixel 11, 11 Pro, 11 Pro XL, and 11 Pro Fold, all built on the new 2nm Tensor G6 chip. Headline upgrades include a 48MP wide sensor with 56% more light sensitivity, 30x SuperRes Zoom via the 5x telephoto, a thinner all-glass camera bar, and — on the Pro models — improved drop resistance and a new anti-scratch coating with more than double the durability of last year's glass (the Pixel 11 Pro starts at $1,099, up from $999). The Pro Fold is nearly 10% lighter and almost 1mm thinner than its predecessor. Google also launched Pixel Tag, a $29 Find Hub tracker aimed squarely at Apple's AirTag, shipping November 11, and devoted a large share of the keynote to expanded on-device Gemini Intelligence features across the lineup. It matters as the clearest signal yet that hardware differentiation in phones is shifting from camera specs alone to how deeply on-device AI is woven into the OS. [Source (TechCrunch)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/12/google-unveils-pixel-11-lineup-new-airtag-rival-and-gemini-features-at-made-by-google-2026/)

**"Zoomsday": a zero-click flaw let meeting participants silently take over other attendees' devices.** Security researchers at A Security disclosed three vulnerabilities (CVE-2026-53413, -53414, -53415) in Zoom's annotation engine that allowed a malicious meeting participant to achieve remote code execution on other attendees' devices — across Windows, macOS, Linux, iOS, and Android — without the victim clicking a link, accepting a prompt, or taking any visible action beyond joining the call. Researcher Idan Levcovich found the bug in a single workday using fewer than 20 prompts to publicly available AI models, underscoring how AI tooling is compressing vulnerability-discovery timelines for both defenders and attackers. Zoom shipped client-side fixes in versions 7.1.0 (June 22) and 7.1.5 (July 20), plus a server-side mitigation on July 15. It matters because it's a stark case study in zero-click, cross-platform exploitation of the world's dominant videoconferencing tool, and in AI's growing role in accelerating exploit development. [Source (CyberInsider)](https://cyberinsider.com/zoom-zero-click-flaw-allowed-rce-attacks-during-meetings/) · [Privacy Guides](https://www.privacyguides.org/news/2026/08/12/severe-zoom-vulnerabilities-allow-malicious-meeting-participants-to-take-over-your-device/)

**The LiteLLM supply-chain breach's blast radius comes into focus: 2,500+ companies, 434,000 CI/CD pipelines.** New research from CloudSEK reconstructed the scope of what's now considered the largest AI-infrastructure supply-chain attack of 2026. The threat group "Team PCP" compromised the Trivy security scanner inside LiteLLM's build pipeline in March, then published two malicious PyPI packages (versions 1.82.7 and 1.82.8) that stayed live for just 40 minutes — long enough to potentially expose credentials across major organizations including Nvidia, AWS, Samsung, Salesforce, Cisco, ServiceNow, Siemens, Deloitte, and Vodafone. The FBI issued a FLASH advisory in July warning stolen credentials remain a live threat months later. It matters because LiteLLM sits inside countless AI application build pipelines, and the incident shows how a single compromised dependency in the AI toolchain can cascade into thousands of downstream victims in minutes. [Source (CloudSEK)](https://www.cloudsek.com/blog/ai-supply-chain-breach-2500-companies-434000-cicd-pipelines)

## FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: An Wang

Long before "vibe coding" startups chased $13 billion valuations, An Wang built one of America's first great technology fortunes — and lost most of it just as fast, in a story that still reads as a cautionary tale about founders, succession, and the limits of genius.

Wang was born in Shanghai in 1920, the son of a teacher. He earned a science degree from Chiao-t'ung University in 1940, then immigrated to the United States in 1945 in the aftermath of World War II, arriving with little money and limited English. He enrolled at Harvard, where he earned a Ph.D. in applied physics and engineering by 1948 — and where he was first introduced to the primitive computers of the era.

That same year, Wang made the discovery that would define his career: he found that a small ring of magnetic material — a "core" — could be used to store a bit of computer memory, and crucially, that the data could be read and then immediately rewritten without being lost. It was an elegant, almost obvious-in-hindsight insight that solved a fundamental problem in early computing, and magnetic-core memory went on to become the dominant form of computer memory for nearly two decades, powering everything from IBM mainframes to the guidance computer on Apollo spacecraft.

Rather than stay inside a corporate research lab, Wang struck out on his own. In 1951, with $600 in savings, he founded Wang Laboratories in a small office in Boston. In 1956 he sold his core-memory patents to IBM for $500,000 — a fortune at the time — and poured the proceeds back into his own company. Over the following two decades, Wang Laboratories rode two successive waves: first, desktop calculators that undercut and outperformed mechanical competitors in the 1960s, and then, decisively, dedicated word-processing systems in the 1970s that made Wang a household name in offices worldwide. By the early 1980s, Wang Laboratories was one of the most successful technology companies in America, employing over 30,000 people at its peak and making An Wang, by some estimates, one of the wealthiest immigrants in the United States.

But the company that had thrived on Wang's singular technical instincts struggled to adapt once the ground shifted beneath it — first with the rise of the IBM PC and PC-compatible word processors, which ate into Wang's proprietary dedicated systems, and then with a succession decision that would prove fatal. In 1986, over the objections of his own senior executives — several of whom quit in protest, including company president John Cunningham — An Wang installed his elder son, Frederick, as president. Fred struggled to navigate the company through the shift to personal computing and networked office software, and by 1989, with losses mounting, An Wang was forced to fire his own son. An Wang died in 1990, having spent his final years watching the company he built unravel. Wang Laboratories filed for bankruptcy in August 1992, a little over two years after his death.

An Wang's story endures less as a rise story than as a complete arc: an immigrant engineer whose single insight reshaped computer memory, who built a industrial giant from $600 and a rented office, and whose reluctance to separate family loyalty from executive judgment ultimately cost the company everything he had built. It remains one of the starkest founder-succession cautionary tales in American technology history — one worth remembering every time a next-generation startup founder starts talking about who inherits the company.
