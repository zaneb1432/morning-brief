## 4. Founder Biography: Chester Carlson (1906–1968), inventor of xerography

Chester Carlson grew up poor in California. His father had crippling arthritis and tuberculosis, and Carlson supported his family from his teens. He worked his way through Riverside Junior College and then Caltech, earning a physics degree in 1930, and graduated into the Depression.

He landed in the patent department of an electronics firm in New York, where the pain point was obvious: patent work required endless copies of documents, made by hand, photostat or carbon paper. Carlson, who also suffered from arthritis, hated the drudgery. He studied law at night and began experimenting with the idea that light could create an image on a photoconductive surface.

On Oct. 22, 1938, in a rented room in Astoria, Queens, with his assistant Otto Kornei, he charged a sulfur-coated zinc plate, exposed it to a glass slide marked "10-22-38 Astoria," dusted it with lycopodium powder and pressed wax paper to it. The image transferred. He called the process electrophotography; the name "xerography" (Greek for "dry writing") came later.

Then came years of rejection. Carlson pitched more than 20 companies, including IBM, GE and Kodak, and nobody wanted it. Wet photocopying and carbon paper seemed good enough, and the dry process looked like an expensive novelty. He patented it in 1942 and kept refining it. In 1944, the nonprofit Battelle Memorial Institute agreed to develop it, in exchange for a cut of the proceeds. In 1947, a small Rochester firm, Haloid Company, licensed it. Haloid later renamed itself Haloid Xerox and then Xerox. The Xerox 914, introduced in 1959, was the first successful plain-paper office copier, and it made the company one of the great growth stories of the era. Carlson, who held the patents, became very rich.

What makes him unusual is what he did next. A private, ascetic man, he gave away most of his fortune, reportedly more than $150 million, to causes including civil rights, the arts, and peace work, and he explored Zen Buddhism. He lived modestly. He died in 1968 in New York.

**Lesson for founders:** Carlson's idea was right for 20 years before anyone would back it. The market was not asking for a better copier, because it did not know one was possible. He survived by keeping his day job, protecting his patent, and finding a partner small enough to bet its future on him.
