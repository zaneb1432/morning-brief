## 1. TECH & AI NEWS

**Meta's new commercial terms may let it scrape news sites for AI training.**
Nieman Lab reports that updated Meta commercial terms, effective October 30, let Meta use content from domains and pages tied to a business account to "provide, develop, improve, research, train, and maintain" its products. Three legal experts told Nieman Lab this could justify scraping news publishers' sites for AI training, possibly overriding robots.txt blocks, and that publishers should assume the terms are enforceable. There is no opt-out; continued use of Meta's services after the effective date counts as acceptance. Meta says this is only a consolidation of existing commercial and advertising terms and changes nothing about how it crawls. It matters because it tests whether a contract clause can do what copyright law and robots.txt have not settled: grant AI-training access to publishers' content. (An aggregator also claimed Meta had walked the language back; I found no confirmation of that.)
Source: [Nieman Lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/2026/10/metas-new-commercial-terms-will-allow-the)

**The DOE is reportedly lining up about $4.4B in loans to restart three nuclear plants for AI power demand.**
A short news roundup puts the total at roughly $4.4B across three restarts. More detailed reports give the pieces as $1.52B to Holtec for Palisades in Michigan, $1B to Constellation for Three Mile Island's Crane unit (backed by a 20-year, 835 MW power agreement with Microsoft, targeted for 2027), and $1.9B to NextEra for Duane Arnold in Iowa (targeted for Q1 2029). All remain subject to NRC approval. The $4.4B total appears only in the roundup, not in a primary announcement. It matters because power, not chips, is increasingly the binding constraint on AI buildout, and Washington is now financing the supply directly.
Sources: [NewsBytes](https://www.newsbytesapp.com/news/world/us-seeks-44b-loans-to-restart-nuclear-plants-for-ai/tldr), [The Register on the Crane loan](https://www.theregister.com/2025/11/19/us_three_mile_island_loan/)

## 4. FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: Soichiro Honda (1906–1991)

Soichiro Honda was born in a village near Hamamatsu, Japan, the son of a blacksmith and bicycle repairman. He left school after elementary-level studies and at 15 went to Tokyo to apprentice at Art Shokai, a car repair shop. He stayed six years, and by the time he returned to Hamamatsu in 1928 he had opened a branch there.

He was an inventor before he was a manager. In the 1930s he began making piston rings, failed his first batches (Toyota rejected them), then went back to study metallurgy and materials until he got them right, an early instance of the pattern that defined him: fail visibly, then go learn the missing science. His company, Tokai Seiki, supplied Toyota. War and the 1945 earthquake wrecked his factory, and he sold what was left to Toyota.

After the war he sat idle for about a year, then bought surplus military generator engines and fitted them to bicycles. Demand outran supply, so he built his own engine, and in 1948 founded Honda Motor Co. The 1949 Dream-type motorcycle was the first of a line of hits, and the 1958 Super Cub, cheap, easy to ride and sold to ordinary people, became one of the best-selling vehicles in history.

His defining act was entering the Isle of Man TT in 1954, when Honda was still small, declaring publicly that he would win. By 1961 Honda riders took the top places in the TT. Racing, to him, was the research lab. He took the same approach to cars, and in the 1970s the CVCC engine met US emissions rules without a catalytic converter, which helped establish Honda as a serious global automaker.

Honda was famously hands-on and temperamental, and famously unsentimental about titles. In 1973 he stepped down as president together with his co-founder, Takeo Fujisawa, saying it was time for younger people to run the company, and he did not install a family member. He spent his later years traveling and supporting engineering education, and died in 1991.

Lesson for founders: treat failure as data, put your product in a public test (racing) and hand the company over while it is still strong.
