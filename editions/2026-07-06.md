## 1. TECH & AI NEWS

**The world's governments convene in Geneva to figure out what to do about AI.** The UN's inaugural Global Dialogue on AI Governance opened today (July 6) at Palexpo in Geneva, bringing all 193 member states together for the first intergovernmental summit dedicated solely to AI oversight, co-chaired by Ambassador Egriselda López of El Salvador and Ambassador Rein Tammsaar of Estonia. The two-day session runs alongside the WSIS Forum and ITU's "AI for Good" summit, and arrives as an independent scientific panel warned that AI capability is accelerating faster than any government's ability to regulate it. It matters because this is the moment AI formally shifted from a "tech policy" problem handled piecemeal by national regulators (the EU's AI Act reaches full applicability August 2) into an explicit geopolitical negotiation, with a second session already scheduled for New York in May 2027. Source: [UN News](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/07/1167862)

**OpenAI began a narrow, government-vetted preview of its next model family.** GPT-5.6 arrives as three durable capability tiers rather than a single model: Sol (flagship, frontier reasoning, new SOTA on Terminal-Bench 2.1), Terra (GPT-5.5-competitive at half the cost), and Luna (fastest and cheapest). The rollout is deliberately restricted — OpenAI shared the models and release plans with the U.S. government first and is limiting access to roughly 20 partner organizations before general availability "in the coming weeks." It's notable both for the new naming convention (numbers = generation, names = capability tier, allowing each to advance independently) and for what it signals about how closely frontier labs are now coordinating releases with Washington. Source: [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/previewing-gpt-5-6-sol/)

**Microsoft is putting $2.5 billion behind the bet that AI's bottleneck is now deployment, not model quality.** The new "Microsoft Frontier Company" will embed 6,000 industry and engineering specialists directly with enterprise customers — including early partners Unilever, Novo Nordisk, London Stock Exchange Group, Land O'Lakes, and Accenture — to co-design and continuously tune AI systems against measurable business outcomes. It lands just two days after AWS unveiled its own $1 billion internal AI-deployment unit, and mirrors similar forward-deployed-engineering plays already underway at OpenAI and Anthropic (structured as joint ventures with outside private equity). The pattern across all four hyperscalers/labs: the model layer is commoditizing fast enough that the real competition is shifting to who can actually get enterprises live on it. Source: [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/02/microsoft-launches-its-own-ai-deployment-company-with-2-5-billion-commitment/)

**Anthropic is turning Claude loose on drug discovery — including its own in-house research program.** The company launched Claude Science in beta, a workbench with 60+ pre-configured tools spanning genomics, proteomics, structural biology, and cheminformatics, aimed at consolidating today's fragmented drug-discovery tooling into one environment for literature review, code, compute jobs, and reproducible results. Alongside it, Anthropic is starting its own internal drug-discovery program targeting "neglected" diseases — partly to advance the science, partly to stress-test Claude Science on real research problems — and is offering researchers up to $30,000 in usage credits. It's a meaningful signal that frontier AI labs no longer see themselves purely as model vendors, but as direct participants in scientific R&D. Source: [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/06/30/anthropic-launches-ai-drug-discovery-program-claude-science.html)

**The U.S. government reversed an unprecedented AI export ban after less than three weeks.** Commerce had ordered Anthropic to disable all access — including for the company's own non-citizen employees — to its Fable 5 and Mythos 5 models after officials learned of a technique that could bypass Fable 5's safeguards on Mythos's powerful cybersecurity capabilities, with reporting pointing to concerns that a China-linked group had accessed the underlying model. Anthropic says it has now implemented a new method to close that specific workaround, and Commerce has lifted the controls. The episode is being read across the industry as a first-of-its-kind precedent for using national-security export law directly against a specific AI model rather than a whole class of hardware or software — a tool regulators now know they can reach for again. Source: [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/06/30/anthropic-says-trump-admin-has-lifted-export-controls-on-claude-fable-5-and-mythos-5.html)

**Zuckerberg told Meta staff the company's AI agent push has gone nowhere for four months — right before his AI chief claimed the opposite.** At a July 2 town hall, Zuckerberg admitted the agent work "hasn't accelerated the way we expected" since May's reorg, which cut roughly 8,000 jobs and reassigned 7,000 more onto AI teams; he says a payoff is still three to six months out. Minutes later, AI chief Alexandr Wang told the same room that Meta's unreleased "Watermelon" model has already caught up to GPT-5.5 on internal benchmarks — a claim Meta hasn't published data to support. The gap between the two messages, against a backdrop of $125–145B in planned 2026 AI capex, is exactly the kind of mixed signal investors have been punishing: Meta shares closed down 4.9% on the news. Source: [The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/metas-ai-agent-push-is-moving-slower-than-zuckerberg-planned/)

## 2. STARTUP WORLD

**Together AI just became one of the most richly valued "neoclouds" in the business.** The GPU-rental and open-model infrastructure startup raised an $800 million Series C at an $8.3 billion valuation — up from $3.3 billion just eighteen months ago — led by Aramco Ventures with NVIDIA, Vista Equity Partners, General Catalyst, Salesforce Ventures, Lux Capital, and others participating. Investors also committed over 500 megawatts of compute capacity separately from the cash round. The company says annual bookings topped $1.15 billion in Q2 alone and it's targeting roughly 50x growth in infrastructure footprint over five years — a bet that enterprises will keep preferring managed inference/fine-tuning over building their own AI pipelines. Source: [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/01/neocloud-together-ai-raises-800m-leaps-to-8-3b-valuation/)

**A German drone maker just raised $1.2 billion — and it's already profitable.** Quantum Systems closed a Series D co-led by Blackstone, Airbus, Advent, and Noteus at roughly an $8 billion valuation, with its autonomous land/air/sea systems having flown more than 19,000 missions in Ukraine in 2025 alone. The round deepens an existing partnership with Airbus even as Airbus's own defense unit could eventually compete with Quantum, underscoring how fluid the lines between primes and startups have become in European defense tech. Proceeds go toward production capacity, supply-chain resilience, and — notably — potential acquisitions as it tries to industrialize a multi-domain platform across allied markets. Source: [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/02/autonomous-defense-startup-quantum-systems.html)

**A Canadian defense startup just closed the largest Series A in the country's history.** Dominion Dynamics raised C$139 million (~US$100 million) led by Georgian, with OMERS, BDC, RBC, Valor Equity Partners, Lakestar, and Bessemer Venture Partners participating, to scale AuraNet — a command-and-control platform fusing data across land, sea, air, and space — and Scout, an autonomous "loyal wingman" drone designed to fly alongside crewed fighter jets. The company is staffing up with veterans of the Canadian Armed Forces alongside engineers pulled from Anduril, Tesla, Rheinmetall, Google, and Rivian, and plans to cross 100 employees by year-end. It's another data point in the broader story below: defense tech is where a huge share of venture enthusiasm has relocated in 2026. Source: [The Globe and Mail](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/business/economy/article-dominion-dynamics-raises-139-million-to-scale-secure-federal/)

**A PureTech spinout raised $180 million to run the first head-to-head trial against an approved lung-disease drug.** Celea Therapeutics, launched out of PureTech Health in 2025, will use the financing — backed by RA Capital Management, Leaps by Bayer, PureTech, a large U.S. healthcare fund, and a sovereign wealth fund — to run SURPASS-IPF, a Phase 3 trial pitting its deupirfenidone candidate directly against Roche's Esbriet (pirfenidone) for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Phase 2 data from late 2024 showed it slowed lung-function decline versus placebo over six months. It's a reminder that plenty of substantial, well-capitalized startup activity is still happening well outside the AI/defense narrative dominating headlines. Source: [BioPharma Dive](https://www.biopharmadive.com/news/celea-puretech-idiopathic-pulmonary-fibrosis-raise/824330/)

**Nearly 40 companies have crossed the unicorn threshold so far in 2026, and the list skews almost entirely AI.** TechCrunch's running tally of new $1B+ valuations this year shows the concentration of venture enthusiasm in AI-adjacent categories continuing unabated even as overall deal counts tighten elsewhere in the market. Worth watching alongside the funding rounds above: the "new unicorn" pace is one of the cleaner real-time signals of where late-stage capital is actually flowing, as opposed to where headlines suggest it's flowing. Source: [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/05/almost-40-new-unicorns-have-been-minted-so-far-this-year-here-they-are/)

## 3. VENTURE CAPITAL

**Europe's original "first cheque" investor just raised its biggest fund ever.** Seedcamp closed $320 million across two vehicles — a $220 million Fund VII for first checks (targeting ~100-120 startups at roughly $1M each) and a $100 million Select fund to double down on winners through Series B and beyond. It's double the size of 2023's Fund VI, backed by British Business Bank, HarbourVest, Schroders, Sofina, and roughly 80 of Seedcamp's own portfolio founders as angel LPs — and comes with a deliberate U.S. expansion via a new New York office, a sign that even early-stage European funds now see a domestic-only strategy as a handicap. Source: [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/22/seedcamp-raises-320m-for-its-new-fund-to-expand-its-us-footprint/)

**JPMorgan waded into the AI-bubble debate with a number: $5.5 trillion.** That's the bank's estimate of cumulative AI capex, and its house view is that — for now — the spending is being justified by real, profitable demand rather than speculative excess, differentiating this buildout from the dot-com era on the grounds that today's AI leaders are already generating substantial free cash flow. The memo matters less for its conclusion than for what it represents: the biggest bank on Wall Street now feels compelled to formally weigh in on whether trillions in AI infrastructure spending is rational, which tells you how mainstream the bubble question has become among LPs deciding whether to keep funding it. Source: [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/06/25/what-bubble-jpmorgan-5-5-trillion-ai-capex-explosion-profitable-for-now/)

**A second, sharper bubble warning — this time aimed squarely at defense tech.** Fortune argues the same dynamics inflating AI valuations are now inflating defense tech: VC poured $12.3 billion into the sector in H1 2026 (nearly double last year), but with Anduril's $5B round alone accounting for almost half of the $11.4B raised by U.S. startups, the market is arguably a handful of mega-deals wearing a "sector boom" costume. The piece is a useful counterweight to the framing in our Startup World section above — Quantum Systems and Dominion Dynamics are real, profitable-or-near-profitable businesses, but the capital flooding into the category overall is concentrating hard around a small number of winners, precisely the pattern that preceded corrections in prior VC cycles. Source: [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/07/02/defense-tech-boom-bubble-startups-venture-capital/)

**Global startup investment hit a record $510 billion in H1 2026 — and the concentration underneath that number is the real story.** Crunchbase's data shows the AI boom driving both funding and exits (IPOs and M&A) to new highs, but other reporting this cycle has pointed out that OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, Waymo, and Databricks alone captured roughly 73% of all U.S. venture deal value in Q1 2026. For LPs, that reframes "venture capital" in 2026 as something closer to a concentrated bet on a handful of AI infrastructure names than a diversified asset class — worth keeping in mind every time a headline number like $510B gets cited without the distribution behind it. Source: [Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/venture/global-startup-exits-ipo-ma-soar-ai-q2-h1-2026/)

## 4. FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY: Dame Stephanie "Steve" Shirley

In July 1939, a five-year-old German Jewish refugee named Vera Buchthal arrived alone in Britain on the Kindertransport, sent ahead of her parents by a family desperate to get her out of Nazi territory. She was taken in by a childless couple in the English Midlands, given the name Stephanie, and grew up haunted by a question she'd later say shaped her entire life: what was she going to do with the life that had been saved?

The answer, eventually, was to build a company almost nobody expected to succeed. In 1962, working from her dining room table with £6 in starting capital, Stephanie Shirley founded Freelance Programmers — a software business built entirely around employing women, mostly mothers, who wanted skilled work but couldn't take a conventional office job because of caring responsibilities. She hired programmers to work from home decades before "remote work" was a phrase anyone used, and built genuinely flexible, family-first employment into the company's DNA at a time when it was, by her own account, close to unthinkable in British industry. Of her first 300 staff, 297 were women.

The tech industry of the early 1960s did not make this easy. Shirley found that her carefully crafted pitch letters to potential clients went unanswered when signed "Stephanie" — so, on her husband Derek's suggestion, she started signing them "Steve." Doors that had been closed suddenly opened, at least until clients discovered who they were actually meeting. She kept the name for the rest of her life. Her firm went on to write software for the Concorde's black box flight recorder and a long list of blue-chip contracts, competing successfully against firms nobody thought a company of "just housewives" could touch.

Shirley's real innovation wasn't just flexible scheduling — it was ownership. She structured the company so employees could hold equity, and by the time it went public in the 1990s (by then renamed Xansa), the stock made millionaires of more than 70 of her staff, an outcome almost unheard of for a services firm built on part-time, work-from-home labor. She herself became one of the wealthiest self-made women in Britain, with a personal fortune she later gave away almost entirely — including tens of millions toward autism research and services, motivated by her own son Giles, who had autism and epilepsy and died at age 35.

She was made a Dame in 2017 and remained an outspoken advocate for women in technology until her death in 2024. Her story is a useful corrective to the standard founder mythology: no elite pedigree, no venture capital, and a business model — flexible, distributed, equity-sharing knowledge work — that the rest of the software industry wouldn't catch up to for the better part of sixty years.
