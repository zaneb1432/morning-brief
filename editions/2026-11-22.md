## 1. TECH & AI NEWS

**An Anthropic mathematician says he and Claude proved the Riemann hypothesis, released only as Lean code (Nov 20–21).** **Levent Alpöge**, a mathematician at Anthropic, posted a **four-page note on Nov 20**. It says he and Claude found a proof of the **Riemann hypothesis** and of the **Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH)**, and it credits Claude with much of the final work. The proof itself exists only as **Lean** code, a formal language whose compiler checks every logical step. Reports say a human-readable version would be enormous. On Nov 21, early checks came in. MIT's **Andrew Sutherland** reportedly compiled the code independently and found it relies only on Lean's three standard axioms. **Elliot Glazer** confirmed that the formal statement really is GRH and not a slightly different, easier statement. Secondary reports also describe a **joint statement from the Lean/Mathlib community** expressing confidence in the formalization. **Terence Tao** wrote that understanding the proof will take time, and he said early analysis suggests it draws on the **Langlands program and arithmetic geometry**. *(Tao's comments come via secondary coverage. I couldn't reach his blog.)* A few hours later, Stanford's **Ravi Vakil**, who is also president of the AMS, shared a separate draft proof of GRH by **Alessandro Masullo** that Vakil says was written **without AI**. It has not been verified either. The release has drawn criticism: a widely quoted line calls publishing a proof its author does not fully understand "frankly completely outrageous" (reports disagree on who said it). Nature reports **Andrew Granville** suggested the timing was linked to Anthropic's stock slide *(single source)*. For context, in August Anthropic announced a much smaller, peer-checked result in which Claude raised the proven share of zeta zeros on the critical line from 41.6% to 67.2%. **Why it matters:** If the result holds, it would be the first proof of a Millennium Prize problem and probably the biggest result in mathematics in a century. It would also be the first major theorem whose correctness was established by a machine checker before any person understood it. That separates two things mathematicians have always treated as the same: knowing a proof is *correct*, which Lean can now tell us, and knowing *why* it's true, which may take years. Three cautions: Lean confirms the proof is consistent with its axioms and library, not that the library has no bugs. There are no Clay Institute or peer-review verdicts yet. And the person making the claim works for a company whose stock has fallen hard this week. **Dragos planted ~2 million fake secrets in AI prompts and found three inference providers breached. OpenRouter has dropped three providers (Nov 19–22).** Industrial-security firm **Dragos** (the work is credited to research officer **HD Moore**) sent about **two million prompts** containing unique, trackable "canary" credentials and URLs through AI inference providers and routers, then watched where they turned up. Findings, per Dragos and secondary coverage: at least **three inference providers had been compromised**, and **none had noticed** before Dragos told them. Stolen canary **AWS keys were reused in tens of thousands of API calls**. Sellers on a **discount inference marketplace** could **view and keep customer prompts using standard request logging**, with no exploit needed. Of roughly **2,000 traced routes**, several hundred passed through an **unidentified system**, and every hop on a multi-hop path can see the full prompt and response. **Zero-data-retention flags were not reliably honored.** One report says a Chinese lab's systems fetched secret URLs from more than half the prompts sent to it, including about two-thirds of requests marked ZDR *(single source)*. **OpenRouter** said it **off-boarded three providers** and removed **125 endpoints** from ZDR eligibility, and that its own systems weren't breached. The compromised providers haven't been publicly named. **Why it matters:** Many companies send proprietary code, customer data and API keys to whichever provider a router picks as cheapest. This is the first large-scale evidence that the middle of that chain leaks. Anyone using third-party routers or cheap hosted open-weight models should review which providers their requests can reach, enforce provider allow-lists, rotate any secret that has passed through a prompt, and consider canary tokens of their own. ## 4. FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY — Olive Ann Beech (1903–1993), Beech Aircraft

**The bookkeeper who ran a wartime aircraft company from a hospital room, then ran it for three more decades.**

*Main facts were checked against search results (Kansas Historical Society, Britannica, Wikipedia). Some details, especially figures, come from standard histories and should be verified before citing. Yesterday's founder was medical hardware. Today's is aviation, and someone even business historians often leave out.*

Olive Ann Mellor was born in **1903** on a farm near **Waverly, Kansas**. The story her biographers tell most often: at **seven**, her parents gave her a **bank account and a checkbook** and had her pay household bills. By her teens she had finished business college. In **1925** she took a job as bookkeeper and secretary at a new Wichita airplane company, **Travel Air**, founded by **Walter Beech**, Clyde Cessna and Lloyd Stearman. Those three names later became three of America's best-known general-aviation brands. The office was so small that she handled nearly everything except building planes.

She married Walter Beech in **1930**. After Travel Air was absorbed into Curtiss-Wright, the couple left in the depths of the Depression and in **1932** founded the **Beech Aircraft Company** in Wichita. Walter was president and handled design and sales. Olive Ann was **secretary-treasurer** and handled everything financial. Their first product, the **Model 17 "Staggerwing,"** was an expensive, fast cabin biplane sold to wealthy pilots during the worst economy of the century. It sold anyway, largely because it was the best plane in its class and Olive Ann kept the company's credit intact.

The real test came in **1940**. Walter developed **encephalitis** and was in the hospital, in a coma for part of that time, for most of a year. Olive Ann was pregnant with their second daughter. War orders were coming in. She took over the company, reportedly holding management meetings from his hospital room, and **fired executives** she believed were trying to take control while Walter was ill. She then scaled Beech to meet wartime demand. Employment grew **from a few hundred to almost 14,000**, and Beech built thousands of aircraft for the military, mostly the twin-engine **Model 18** used to train most U.S. bombardiers and navigators. She financed the expansion and is widely credited with negotiating the contracts. The company won the Army-Navy "E" award for production.

Walter died suddenly of a heart attack in **1950**. Olive Ann became **president and chairman**, one of the first women to lead a major American aircraft manufacturer. *Time* covered the appointment under the headline "A Job for Olive Ann." Over the next 30 years she led Beech through the **Bonanza** (introduced in 1947, it became one of the longest-produced aircraft in history), Korean War and Cold War contracts, cryogenic and fuel systems for the space program, including work connected to Apollo, and the **King Air** (1964), which created the business-turboprop category and is still in production. She was known for exact standards, which extended to dress codes, purchasing approvals and company colors (she reportedly had final say over aircraft paint schemes), and for taking customers and dealers seriously.

In **1980** she merged Beech into **Raytheon** in a stock deal reported at roughly **$800M** *(figure from one source)* and joined Raytheon's board. She became chair emeritus of Beech in **1982** and died in Wichita in **1993**. She is often called the "**First Lady of Aviation**." The King Air, Bonanza and Baron she oversaw are still flying, and today they are sold under **Textron Aviation**.

**Takeaways for founders:**
- **The finance co-founder is a co-founder.** Beech Aircraft survived the Depression because the company kept its credit, not just because it built good planes. The person who controls cash and contracts usually decides whether the company survives.
- **In a crisis, act decisively.** When Walter was incapacitated, Olive Ann didn't wait to see what would happen. She took control, including over executives trying to move in.
- **Durable product lines outlast trends.** The Bonanza and King Air have been in production for decades. Building a category-defining product and continuing to improve it can be worth more than chasing the next new thing.
- **Know when to sell.** She merged Beech into Raytheon at the top of her own career and before general aviation's 1980s collapse, which hurt independent planemakers badly.
