## 1. TECH & AI NEWS

**Nvidia's board reportedly approached Arm CEO Rene Haas to replace Jensen Huang (Nov 13–14).** The Financial Times reported that Nvidia directors sounded out Haas as a possible permanent CEO. Haas spent seven years at Nvidia, where he ran its computing products business, before joining Arm in 2013, and he has led Arm since 2022. Nvidia's board pushed back on the report, saying it is still choosing a search firm and "has not formally engaged any potential candidates." Other names being discussed include Nvidia's Ian Buck and Jay Puri, Micron CEO Sanjay Mehrotra, and Google Cloud CEO Thomas Kurian, whom analyst Dan Ives called one of the three strongest outside candidates. Colette Kress remains interim CEO. **Why it matters:** Nvidia reports quarterly earnings on Tuesday, Nov 17. Hiring an outsider with a CPU and licensing background like Haas would signal a very different Nvidia from Huang's, which was built around GPUs and a large investment portfolio. For Arm, and for SoftBank, which controls it, losing its CEO would be a major disruption. **Google ships Gemini 4 Hydrogen, a fast, cheap model for coding and agents (Nov 12).** Hydrogen is the fast, low-cost tier of the Gemini 4 family. Carbon and Argon are the larger models for the hardest reasoning tasks. Google says Hydrogen beats Argon on some benchmarks and is built for everyday coding and multi-step agent work. It supports four configurable "thinking levels." It went live everywhere at once: the Gemini app, Google Search, the Gemini API, AI Studio, Android Studio and the Antigravity coding tool. Antigravity also gets an "Orchestrator" mode that sends a single prompt across Hydrogen and Carbon together. Reported prices vary between outlets, and I couldn't confirm them on Google's pricing page, so check there before relying on a figure. **Why it matters:** Most agent traffic runs on small, fast models, because agents make many calls per task. Putting Hydrogen into Search on day one gives Google enormous usage immediately, and it competes on price with OpenAI's and Anthropic's small models as agents become the main way these products are used. **Thinking Machines releases the weights of Inkling 2 Small, as promised (Nov 12).** Mira Murati's lab released Inkling 2 Small on Nov 5 as a hosted-only model on its Tinker platform. A permissively licensed model of this size from a US lab gives enterprises an option besides Chinese open models. ## 3. VENTURE CAPITAL

**a16z essay: "Big Pharma Can Rival Big Software" (Nov 13).** a16z argues that AI could make drug discovery repeatable enough that investors start valuing a pharma company's *discovery engine*, not just its pipeline of individual drugs. Today, it says, investors give pharma little credit for that engine. It notes that nine of the top 30 pharma companies are each worth more than $200B, compared with 16 software companies. It puts the present-value cost of one new drug at nearly $2B and about 13 years, and says its scenario of halving that cost is an illustration, not a forecast. The essay describes two business models: selling the technology, or using it to develop and sell drugs directly. Another a16z piece pushes back, arguing that AI can make drugs easier to create without making each drug more valuable. **Why it matters:** This is the thesis behind large rounds for full-stack AI biotechs such as Chai Discovery ($400M at $3.8B in July). One analysis notes that only about 3% of AI drug-discovery deal value is paid at signing, so the "software multiple" argument hasn't been proven yet. ## 4. FOUNDER BIOGRAPHY — Anita Roddick (1942–2007), The Body Shop

**A £4,000 shop next to a funeral parlour that made ethics into a retail brand.**

Anita Perilli was born in 1942 in a bomb shelter in Littlehampton, a seaside town on England's south coast. Her parents were Italian immigrants who ran a café there, and she grew up working behind the counter. She trained as a teacher. Then she left to travel: she worked for the United Nations' International Labour Organization in Geneva, and spent time in Polynesia, Australia and Africa. She noticed how women in those places cared for their skin and hair with simple local ingredients such as cocoa butter, aloe and clay. Later she said these observations were the original research behind her company.

She married Scottish poet Gordon Roddick in 1970, and they ran a restaurant and then a small hotel in Littlehampton. In 1976 Gordon decided to ride a horse from Buenos Aires to New York, a trip of about two years. Anita needed an income for herself and their two daughters. She took out a £4,000 bank loan, secured against the hotel, and on 27 March 1976 opened a small shop at 22 Kensington Gardens in Brighton. She called it The Body Shop, after American car-repair shops.

Many of the brand's famous features began as ways to save money. She painted the walls dark green to cover the damp. She sold about 15 products in cheap plastic bottles bought from a hospital supplier, the kind used for urine samples. She offered refills because she couldn't afford enough bottles, and she wrote the labels by hand. She sold lotions in several sizes because she had only enough stock to fill the shelves that way. Two funeral directors next door threatened to sue over a shop called "The Body Shop" beside a funeral parlour. She tipped off the local paper, and the dispute got her free publicity. Years later she told interviewers she had never had a marketing budget, and she meant it. The Body Shop famously did almost no traditional advertising for decades.

She needed £4,000 more to open a second shop and the banks refused. Ian McGlinn, a local garage owner, put up the money in exchange for half the business. It became one of the most profitable small investments in British business history: when the company was sold in 2006, his stake was worth over £100 million. The Roddicks expanded through franchising, which let the brand spread faster than their own capital allowed. By 1984, when The Body Shop listed on the London stock market, it had well over a hundred stores. In the following years it grew to thousands in dozens of countries.

What made Roddick unusual was her use of the shops for activism. The windows and carrier bags carried campaigns: "Save the Whales" with Greenpeace in 1986, against animal testing in cosmetics, for human rights and against the destruction of the rainforest. Her "Trade Not Aid" program bought ingredients directly from communities, such as Brazil nut oil from the Kayapó people of the Amazon. She argued that business was the most powerful force in society and therefore had a duty to do good. That claim sounded radical in the 1980s and became the basis of today's "purpose-driven brand" movement.

There were also setbacks and controversies. In 1994 the journalist Jon Entine published an investigation questioning the company's claims about its ingredients and its fair-trade sourcing. The stock fell, and the company spent years defending itself. Roddick hated the City's quarterly scrutiny and said publicly that she regretted taking the company public. In 2006 she sold The Body Shop to L'Oréal for £652 million. Many longtime customers were outraged, because L'Oréal was partly owned by Nestlé and was not cruelty-free at the time. Roddick argued she could change the giant from inside, the way a "Trojan horse" would. She said she would give away most of her fortune, and she did, through her foundation.

In 2004 she learned she had hepatitis C, contracted from a blood transfusion during the birth of her daughter in 1971, and she campaigned to raise awareness of it. She died of a brain haemorrhage on 10 September 2007, aged 64. The company she built had a difficult time after her death: it was sold on to Natura, then to private equity, and in 2024 its UK arm went into administration before being rescued. The model she pioneered, a brand defined by its values and supported by loyal customers, is now standard practice in consumer retail.

**Lessons for founders:**
- **Constraints can become the brand.** Refills, plain bottles and the green walls started as ways to save money and ended up as the brand's identity.
- **A strong mission can replace an ad budget.** She turned campaigns, controversy and press coverage into free distribution for decades.
- **Franchising is a form of financing.** It let her grow internationally without giving up more equity after selling half to McGlinn.
- **Be careful about who owns your mission.** Public markets and a strategic buyer both put pressure on the values that made the company valuable, and the Entine investigation showed how costly any gap between claims and supply chains can be.
