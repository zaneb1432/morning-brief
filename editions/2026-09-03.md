## 1. Tech & AI News

**Google ships Gemini 3.8 Flash, its third Flash refresh in three months.** Google released Gemini 3.8 Flash on September 2, positioning it as the company's workhorse model with meaningful gains over 3.7 Flash on software engineering, agentic tasks, and multi-step reasoning in specialized domains — often approaching the performance of costlier frontier models. **OpenAI's Astra becomes the first model to cross its "Critical" cybersecurity threshold.** OpenAI disclosed that Astra, an internal model, independently discovered novel software vulnerabilities and could construct complex cyberattacks without step-by-step human guidance — triggering the highest risk tier in its Preparedness Framework for the first time. Rather than shelve it, OpenAI built additional safeguards it says "sufficiently minimize the risk of severe harm" and is releasing advanced cyber capabilities first to a small group of testers, with wider defensive access planned through a program called Daybreak Blue. It matters because it's the clearest public marker yet that frontier models have crossed from cyber-assistant to cyber-operator territory, forcing labs to build release gates that didn't previously need to exist. The tiered-access approach — a broadly available model paired with a walled-off, higher-capability sibling — mirrors the same risk-gating logic showing up across the industry this week. [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-fable-and-mythos-5-1) · [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/anthropics-claude-fable-5-1-and-mythos-5-1-arrive-with-a-75-cost-reduction-for-fable-cache-reads)

**Perplexity splits AI tasks between the cloud and your Mac.** Perplexity introduced Hybrid Compute for its Computer app on Apple silicon Macs (macOS 15+, 24GB+ unified memory), which routes hard reasoning and planning to cloud models while handing sensitive steps to a local model running on-device. The centerpiece is a "Privacy Gate" — an on-device classifier that flags names, addresses, account numbers, and secrets before anything leaves the machine, then masks, blocks, or asks for consent as needed. It's available now to Pro, Max, and Enterprise subscribers and is a notable bet that privacy-by-architecture, not just policy promises, will be how AI agents earn trust with sensitive local data. [Perplexity](https://www.perplexity.ai/hub/blog/introducing-hybrid-compute-on-mac) · [Unite.AI](https://www.unite.ai/perplexity-launches-hybrid-compute-on-mac-with-local-privacy-gate/)

## 4. Founder Biography: Dame Stephanie "Steve" Shirley

In 1962, a 29-year-old refugee sat down at her kitchen table in England with £6 of capital and founded Freelance Programmers — a software company built almost entirely around a workforce nobody else in the industry wanted to hire.

Stephanie Shirley had arrived in Britain in 1939 as a five-year-old on the Kindertransport, one of thousands of Jewish children evacuated from Nazi-occupied Europe without their parents. She trained as a mathematician, worked as a computer programmer at the Post Office Research Station, and ran headlong into the era's rigid expectation that women — especially mothers, especially caregivers — didn't belong in a technical career. So she built her own company designed around the constraint everyone else treated as disqualifying: Freelance Programmers hired women who were raising children or caring for elderly relatives and let them write software from home, on their own schedules, decades before "remote work" or "flexible hours" were terms anyone used in a business context.

The company almost didn't survive contact with the market. Shirley found that her business letters went unanswered — until she started signing them "Steve" instead of Stephanie. The name stuck, and so did the company. By 1975, Freelance Programmers employed more than 300 programmers, 297 of them women, working on contracts that included writing the software for the black-box flight recorder used on the Concorde. The company was later renamed F International, then FI Group, then Xansa — and by the time it was acquired by the French IT firm now known as Sopra Steria in 2007, it had grown to roughly 8,500 staff and a valuation approaching $3 billion. Because Shirley had structured the firm with broad employee ownership from early on, the sale made an estimated 70 of her staff into millionaires.

She retired in 1993 and spent much of the rest of her life in philanthropy through the Shirley Foundation, focused heavily on autism research — motivated in part by her son's own autism diagnosis — as well as on emerging technology. Shirley died in 2025, but her company stands as one of the earliest proof points that a business built around inclusion, not despite it, could outcompete an industry that excluded the same people by default.
